The intellect cannot issue legislation:
There is no doubt that the intellect is the tool for a person to know their Creator and to understand the secret of their existence in life, to unlock many of the mysteries of this existence and interpret them through intelligent connections, understanding, and comprehension, as well as the creation of ideas, research, inference, and induction. The intellect is the basis of accountability. Given the immense status of the intellect in the "plan of existence," and in distinguishing the rational human from other creatures, and relying on the intellect to perceive the connection with the Creator; it is undoubtedly a tool of great importance and capability. There is no doubt that its judgment, which it will make in the process of reasoning about the Creator or in interpreting existence, that judgment based on evidence and connected to the parameters that provide certainty, is a judgment whose correctness is indisputable, and we must accept it without hesitation. However, when examining the ability of the intellect to issue judgments on matters of lesser significance than issues of belief, such as some rulings related to organizing human life, like judging the goodness or badness of an action, or whether it is a good ruling or a bad one, or whether it carries reward or punishment, or whether it improves or corrupts human livelihood, or whether it brings benefit or averts harm, we will find that the intellect cannot issue such judgments. It is worth noting that the intellect cannot issue judgments not because it is prohibited from doing so, but because it has not been provided with the tools, capabilities, and information necessary for its ability to issue them.

Thus, the intellect is not a legislator, as its ability to establish laws, issue judgments on actions, interests, consequences, good and evil, beauty and ugliness, reward and punishment, and justice and injustice relies on tools, information, and capabilities that are not available to it, such as knowledge of the future consequences of actions or realities—will it be good for him or bad? Since the interests arising from performing certain actions may lead to conflicts in the types of interests that will be realized or that may be canceled or pushed aside due to their conflict with other interests, does the interest lie in material benefit, or in moral, humanitarian, or spiritual value? And how can one weigh them when they conflict? These are keys that the intellect cannot unlock. Moreover, some issues necessary for establishing legislation are partially accessible to the intellect, but this is insufficient for issuing judgments, such as understanding the nature of reality. Additionally, the issues upon which the correctness of judgment regarding goodness and beauty or their absence depends are complex, or known only relatively or incompletely to the intellect, which will certainly affect its judgments. Perhaps a person may perceive the ugly as beautiful, or turn away from an action that contains his benefit to its opposite for the reasons mentioned above. Therefore, human legislations have tended to shift from one extreme to another, and the capabilities of intellects vary in strength and weakness, in estimating harm and benefit, and judgments are influenced by whims and inclinations. Judgments differ among people; what one person sees as good, another sees as ugly, and the estimation of intellects regarding the methods that achieve interests varies. Intellects also differ in understanding the harmony of laws with the fabric of public law in the state and their realization of the system of values and legislative objectives. Intellects vary in studying the impact of the many and complex factors affecting the issue and in estimating the preference between them as well, along with the immediate or subsequent effects that result from this, and other factors whose existence cannot be denied, which necessitate the acknowledgment of the incapacity of the intellect to legislate or to truly comprehend interests. For example, justice is a value that is universally agreed upon as good and correct, yet the dilemma lies in how to achieve justice. This can only be accomplished by establishing a correct system that realizes justice. Thus, the value of justice is realized through external matters, not from itself, and these external matters are the controls of actions, namely legislations and systems.
