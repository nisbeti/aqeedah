The Three Stages of Ijtihad
Ibn al-Qayyim (may Allah have mercy on him) said: The mufti and the judge cannot issue a fatwa or rule with truth except through two types of understanding:
The first is understanding the reality and comprehending it, and deriving knowledge of the truth of what has occurred through indications, signs, and markers, until one has complete knowledge of it.
The second type is understanding the obligation in reality, which is understanding the ruling of Allah that He has decreed in His Book or on the tongue of His Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him) regarding this reality, and then applying one to the other(209). Ibn al-Qayyim said: "The obligation is one thing and the reality is another, and the jurist is the one who reconciles between the reality and the obligation"(210).
Establishing the legal basis (taḥqīq al-manāṭ): When a jurist strives to derive the ruling of Sharia on a particular issue, there must be three stages for ijtihad:
First: Understanding the reality(211) and comprehending it, with the aim of establishing the legal basis. Second: Understanding the obligation in reality. Third: Applying the ruling of the obligation to the reality and clarifying its legal ruling.
First: Understanding the reality and comprehending it, with the aim of establishing the legal basis, and this is an intellectual inquiry that includes understanding the essence of things, their definitions, their realities, and the details of what has occurred. In this stage, one must scrutinize the issue to understand it accurately and refine it.
The Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) questioned Ma'iz when he confessed before him to adultery, asking him, "Perhaps you kissed or touched or looked?" ... The purpose of this questioning is to accurately determine the reality. Because the punishment for kissing is different from the punishment for adultery, establishing the legal basis is necessary and must be precise.
For example, the jurist must distinguish between the reality of the call to the Islamic movement, the reality of enjoining good and forbidding evil, and the work to revive Islamic life, as well as the rulings of defensive jihad and the rulings of offensive jihad. Do these actions share the same reality?
I believe no one would disagree with me that every call to any idea of Islamic belief and to any ruling of Sharia is an enjoining of good, and that any prohibition of what contradicts Islam in belief and law is a forbidding of evil. Thus, the good is Islam and the evil is anything other than Islam. I do not think anyone would disagree with me that the greatest good is belief in Allah and His oneness in servitude, and the greatest evil is disbelief in Allah and associating partners with Him.
I do not think anyone would disagree with me that the predecessors of the Ummah, the best of generations, strove in the way of Allah to spread Islam, opening lands and calling people to the greatest good, which is entering into Islam, and forbidding the greatest evil, which is disbelief in Allah and associating partners with Him. Nevertheless, we have not received any report that any of them referred to this action as enjoining good and forbidding evil; rather, they called it jihad.
