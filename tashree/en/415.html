The Qur'an has linked the vicegerency of humanity on earth to the establishment of justice and the prevention of corruption: ﴾So that people may maintain justice﴿, thus whenever one follows a path other than that of Allah, corruption prevails and blood is shed.

The system of caliphate is an integral part of the Sharia (Islamic law) and not merely a historical experiment. This is evidenced by the numerous hadiths commanding allegiance to a single imam with obedience to him, warning against division and differing states, and necessitating the arbitration of the Book and the Sunnah in every dispute. The actions of the companions after the death of the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him), beginning with the appointment of a caliph before they occupied themselves with his burial, is the clearest practical proof that the establishment of a state is not merely a worldly matter left to their whims, but a religious obligation they prioritized above all else.

The Qur'an and the Sunnah have elaborated on many legislative aspects of governance: from the method of selecting the ruler (consultation and allegiance) to his rights and duties, from the relationship between the ruler and the ruled (the constitution of the state and the judiciary) to the systems of managing public funds (booty, taxes, and almsgiving), from the policies of war and peace (jihad and treaties) to the organization of international relations. When one examines the texts, one finds the structure of the Islamic state delineated: a general presidency (caliphate), specific executive and judicial authorities (governors and judges), a consultative council and accountability (those with the power to resolve matters), a jihad army and internal security, and a management of funds (the treasury)... etc. These institutions are not products of human philosophical thought, but were derived by the nation from the texts of the Sharia and the experience of the rightly guided caliphate. The scholars of the rulings of authority have further detailed them in a way that ensures the realization of the objectives of the Sharia in governance and justice.

Moreover, the relationship between the ruler and the subjects is itself regulated by the Sharia through a legal contract (allegiance) that has its pillars and conditions. The allegiance is a contract for obedience on the condition of ruling by the Book and the Sunnah. If the ruler violates this condition, then there is no obedience or listening. He (peace and blessings be upon him) said: "Obedience is only in what is good," and he said: "There is no obedience to a creature in disobedience to the Creator." Thus, the nation is not a captive to the whims of the ruler as in despotic systems, but the ruler is an employee of the nation, leading it by the law of Allah. This understanding completely refutes the idea that "Islam left the matter of governing the nation to its intellects to invent a system that suits its era," and also the idea of "free rational politics" that allows the ruler to enact any law he sees fit as long as it serves what he perceives as beneficial. The ruler in Islam is bound by the Sharia and is held accountable by the subjects. This is a constraint that secular systems have only recognized theoretically. Democracy, for example, restricts the ruler by a secular constitution that can be amended by majority vote, while Islam restricts the ruler by a divine constitution that no one has the authority to amend or deviate from.

In summary, the philosophy of governance in Islam revolves around the supreme authority of the reference of revelation. The state is not disconnected from religion; rather, it is a tool for implementing religion. This is contrary to what colonizers and their followers have propagated since the abolition of the caliphate, when they claimed that Islam is "a religion without a state" and that Muslims can lead their lives with an individual spiritual Islam under the umbrella of secular national states. The tangible reality and the legal evidence together indicate the fallacy of this illusion; for most of the collective obligations of Islam are suspended without a state: from the application of punishments and the establishment of laws, to the protection of the community and defensive jihad, to the unity of the nation and the establishment of social solidarity... etc. Therefore, the scholars have said: the establishment of the caliphate is one of the greatest obligations of the religion.
