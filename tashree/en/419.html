It is necessary, and it varies according to different methods and means, whether it is a notebook or a computer. Likewise, the organization of public facilities, such as "roads," is considered an administrative organization. The Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) organized the affairs of the road by making the width of the road seven arms in cases of dispute. However, this was an administrative organization at that time. Therefore, in the Shafi'i (madhhab), we find that the width of the road may increase when needed. From this, it follows that the regulation of measures and executive arrangements for facilities (such as roads) is considered an administrative organization that changes according to necessity, not a fixed ritual ruling applicable at all times and places.

Consultation and Accountability: Consultation (shura) is obligatory for the ruler in matters where there is no text, and in situations where there are multiple opinions to achieve the public interest (maslaha). Islam has established consultation as a fundamental principle of governance. Likewise, holding rulers accountable by the subjects is a permissible and even obligatory matter if they fall short or commit injustice, as indicated by the general statement of the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him): "The best jihad is a word of truth in the presence of a tyrannical ruler." All of these are legal rulings that define the nature of the relationship between the shepherd and the flock. As for the form of the institutions that implement these principles, it is left to ijtihad (independent juristic reasoning). Consultation may take place through an elected council from the community (the Council of the Ummah), which is legally permissible as a means to involve the public, or it may occur through a council of scholars and experts only, or both together. The important thing is the existence of a mechanism through which opinions are gathered and voices are heard. The method of election, the number of members, the duration of membership, etc., are all organizational details. The condition is that they do not contradict the principle of consultation itself or any other ruling: for example, if the council's system stipulates that it cannot oppose decisions that contradict the Sharia, that would be invalid. However, stipulating a certain age for voters, or dividing electoral districts in a specific manner, are purely administrative matters that are determined by public interest.

Treaties and Foreign Policy: The legal origin is that the external relations of the Islamic state are governed by the rulings of jihad, loyalty, and disavowal. For example, it is not permissible to ally with an enemy state that is at war or to sign a pact with it against the interests of Muslims (this is prohibited by the text: ﴾The believers should not take the disbelievers as allies instead of the believers﴿ [Al-Imran 28]). Likewise, it is not permissible to conclude a permanent truce that halts jihad indefinitely, nor to form a military alliance with a disbelieving state against a Muslim state; these are established legal prohibitions. Conversely, the Sharia permits the conclusion of temporary treaties (ceasefire, good neighborliness, trade exchange) according to public interest (maslaha) as long as they do not involve prohibited concessions or commitments that contradict the Sharia. In fact, fulfilling sound contracts is obligatory: ﴾And if they seek your help in religion, it is your duty to help them, except against a people with whom you have a treaty...﴿ [Al-Anfal 72]. Thus, the framework of foreign policy is defined by values and regulations (invitation and jihad, fulfillment and covenant, preventing allegiance to disbelievers...), but the details of managing international relations – such as the mechanisms of diplomacy, organizing embassies, the format of written agreements, and the style of negotiations – are left to political expertise as long as they adhere to the objectives of the Sharia. For example, the language of diplomatic discourse, the protocols for receiving ambassadors, or the visa system for visitors and traders, all of this is regulated by the ruler in a manner that he sees achieves the interests of the state and does not contradict a legal ruling (such as facilitating espionage or allowing missionaries to enter, for instance).

State Administration (Administrative and Economic Structure): Islam has mandated that the state establish institutions that ensure the achievement of justice and the care of people's interests. It has made it obligatory to have an independent judiciary to settle disputes according to the Sharia. It has also required the appointment of governors over the regions to implement God's rule in every area. Furthermore, it has mandated the management of the Muslim treasury (Bayt al-Mal) to collect resources.
