Since the Qur'an is the fundamental source of strength for Muslims, and its continued presence(21) in their hands, alive, leads to their return to their strength and civilization, their plan to eliminate or distort the Qur'an was based on:
Denial of the Qur'an's connection to revelation. "Western efforts to attack the Noble Qur'an took several paths; missionary research emerged regarding the Noble Qur'an, addressing claims about its sources, asserting that they consist of paganism, hanifism, sabianism, zoroastrianism, Christianity, and Judaism. These claims have been proven false by the contradictory reality, by the Qur'an's refutation of them, and by the ongoing challenge to produce something like this Qur'an - if it were from human sources -!"(22)
Islam has established precise foundational methodologies for understanding the Qur'an and the Sunnah and for deriving legislation from them according to a disciplined process. Thus, there have been persistent attempts to undermine these methodologies, aiming to destroy the entire structure of ijtihad (independent juristic reasoning) by prioritizing reason and desires over revelation, bypassing texts and their implications, fabricating conflicts between reason and transmission, the Qur'an and science, and attacking tradition and derived rulings to adapt them to align with Western legislations and laws.
In Dr. Joseph Massad's book: Islam in Liberalism: "The 'Arab Human Development Report 2005' issued by the United Nations Development Programme seeks to: 'Address transformations in attitudes and reform cultural frameworks. Specifically, it will modernize interpretation and jurisprudence through the comprehensive adoption of enlightened readings of ijtihad, which must liberate itself from the bondage of existing religious institutions and figures, becoming a right and duty for every educated Muslim, whether male or female, who has the ability to engage in the study of their religion'"(23). Dr. Massad states: "Thus, the report's call for reform does not aim to present new jurisprudential readings of the Qur'an by Muslims, but rather aims to align new jurisprudential readings with 'international' liberal legislations"(24).
Cheryl Bernard wrote for the Rand Corporation, supported by the U.S. State Department, in her book: Civil Democratic Islam: "There are other solutions as well, as modernity applies the principle of public interest (maslaha), which dates back to reformist Muslim jurists in the thirteenth century, as something believed to potentially surpass or even annul the Qur'an itself." This approach represents an extension established by the school of Jamal al-Din al-Afghani and Muhammad Abduh, prioritizing reason over transmission(25).
