Alongside this, the West has promoted its civilization as the culmination of human civilizations and the end of history, asserting that other nations should merely follow its lead, adopt its way of life, and cease their efforts to establish a system based on their own civilizations rooted in their beliefs. Unfortunately, many Muslims have been dazzled by this presentation.

Let no one think that civilization is universal, inherited by nations, such that when it settles in the West and unloads its cargo of accumulated human experiences, it reaches its peak and becomes the direction that seekers of happiness and advancement must turn towards.

Let no one think this, for "Spengler" (1880-1936) asserts that "each civilization has its own entity, isolated from other civilizations, and each forms a unit or a closed circle unto itself. The similarities observed in subjects between one civilization and another are merely superficial, for each civilization is an expression of a spirit, and the spirit differs from one civilization to another. If external influencing elements are shared between two civilizations, each accepts these elements in a manner that is entirely different from how the other civilization accepts them, for neither can digest these elements unless it transforms them into its own nature. Thus, the illusions of historians regarding influence, continuity, and historical unity are invalidated"(68).

And here is "Toynbee" rejecting the idea of the unity of civilizations, which he sees as a notion imposed by the erroneous views of Western historians derived from their deceptive perspective, which they intended to use to establish an economic system foreign to the entire world according to the Western model specifically, as a prelude to politically unifying it in the Western manner. Western historians, especially modern ones, believe that there is a fundamental source of civilization that is the Western source; thus, they conclude, based on this perspective, to a unity of global civilization"(69).

Similarly, Samuel Huntington objects to those who say "that Western civilization is a universal civilization suitable for all people," stating: "While it is superficially true that Western civilization has indeed permeated the rest of the world, Western concepts, at a more fundamental level, differ significantly from those prevalent in other civilizations. Western ideas about individuality, liberalism, constitutionalism, human rights, equality, freedom, the rule of law, democracy, free markets, and the separation of church and state typically do not hold great appeal in Islamic, Confucian, Japanese, Hindu, Buddhist, or Orthodox cultures... The notion that there can be a 'universal civilization' is itself a Western idea that directly contradicts the particularities of most Asian societies and their focus on what distinguishes one people from another"(70).
