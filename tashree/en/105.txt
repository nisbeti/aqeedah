The Companions (may Allah be pleased with them), the Successors (tabi'un), and the jurists (fuqaha) after them distinguished between two matters: jihad and enjoining good and forbidding wrong. They discussed these two actions separately in the books of jurisprudence, and each had its own rulings.
Jihad (struggle) pertains to the relationship of Muslims with others among the disbelievers, while enjoining good (amr bil ma'ruf) and forbidding wrong (nahi an al-munkar) pertains to the relationship of Muslims with one another. Therefore, carrying the call (da'wah) is distinct from enjoining good and forbidding wrong. The subject of carrying the call is conveying Islam to the disbelievers, and its legal method is jihad, while the subject of enjoining good and forbidding wrong is the reform of a Muslim when he violates the Sharia.
On one hand, regarding the work to resume Islamic life, and considering the reality of this work, we find that it differs from carrying the call (da'wah). The subject of carrying the call is conveying Islam to non-Muslims, while the work to resume Islamic life has as its subject conveying Islam to Muslims in order to live based on Islam by establishing the caliphate.
At first glance, it seems that this work is related to enjoining good (amr bil ma'ruf) and forbidding what is wrong (nahi an al-munkar), but upon closer examination, it becomes clear that there are differences. Among these is that the work to revive Islamic life aims to establish the entirety of Islam in practice, whereas enjoining good and forbidding what is wrong is a call to adhere to a specific legal ruling from the rulings of Islam. When scholars discussed the rulings of enjoining good and forbidding what is wrong, they examined legal violations whether they originated from the ruler or the ruled, and they did not address the establishment of the entirety of Islam in practice. This difference is what leads us to say that the reality of the work to revive Islamic life is different from the reality of enjoining good and forbidding what is wrong.
And every person who is dismissive or indifferent to these differences in reality, no matter how subtle, will be astonished by our distinction between these actions and will mock and attack us. However, we will not care and will not pay attention to their actions, for our role model in scrutinizing reality before issuing a judgment is the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him).
If it becomes clear that there is a difference in the criteria for each of these three actions, then there will certainly be a difference in the ruling for each of them.
This applies to all actions whose realities differ even if they are similar; prayer, the funeral prayer, and the eclipse prayer are all prayers, but each has rulings that differ from the others. Reciting Al-Fatiha is a condition in prayer, and omitting it invalidates the prayer, while in the funeral prayer it is only recited after the first takbir, and not otherwise. The funeral prayer has no bowing or prostration in it, unlike the regular prayer and the eclipse prayer. The eclipse prayer has two bowings in each unit, and performing them is obligatory, while if someone does that in the regular prayer without forgetting, their prayer is invalid.
This example shows that if the reality differs, even if it appears similar, the rulings will differ; what is permissible in one case may be forbidden in another, and so on. Similarly, regarding carrying the call (da'wah), enjoining good (amr bil ma'ruf), forbidding evil (nahi an al-munkar), and working to resume Islamic life, what is permissible in one context may not be permissible in another.
For example, the method of Islam in carrying the call is jihad, and it revolves around fighting, while the method for resuming Islamic life is the method of the Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him), and among its rulings is the prohibition of fighting. Additionally, among the rulings of carrying the call is inviting the disbelievers to Islam; if they refuse, then the jizyah (poll tax) is required, and if they refuse that, then fighting is permitted. However, the one who enjoins good and forbids evil is not called to these three options; rather, taking the jizyah from him is forbidden, as he is a Muslim. I will not delve into explaining the rulings of these three actions now and clarifying their differences, for when the differences in reality become clear, there is no surprise in the differences in rulings.
