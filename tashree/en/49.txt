The legal system in Islamic law is characterized by the most just rules, the most sound judgments, and the highest and most enduring principles, which are the most attentive to the higher human ideals, and it enriches the legal element with the moral element."(89)
Islamic legislation is also distinguished by establishing a set of lofty values, noble morals(90), reference standards for rights, and legislative objectives, which in turn will transform into values intended to be realized in life and to prevail in relationships within society. This is taken into account in both legislation and application, leading to the integration of laws and regulations with their objectives, so that their impact is felt in society as a coherent and integrated effective system, where each part supports the other, and individuals and society find a motivation from piety, values, and morals during application, leading to happiness and stability. All of this is something that human legislators have not grasped or even contemplated!
Islamic legislation is characterized by its establishment of laws with intended objectives behind them, and wise reasons motivating them, and causes(91) and objectives driving towards them(92). This means it sets standards for laws and regulations, forming the framework within which all laws operate across various aspects of life(93). The principle is that laws should be complementary, supporting one another; for example, the system of penalties is based on the proper functioning of the economic system, social solidarity, and the responsibilities of the state in its guardianship role(94). Thus, laws—whether religious or secular—are established to serve the values that are intended to prevail in people's lives(95).

(91) Regarding Islamic law, we mean by objectives and causes here: the result that follows from the law, i.e., the wisdom resulting from its application, not the motivation for its legislation, i.e., the goal of the legislator that he aims for through the legislation, nor the cause in its foundational sense, nor should one seek the motivating cause for the legislation, nor the wisdom unless the legislator mentions them. Verses have come that clarify general goals and wisdoms that result from the application of the law, such as being a mercy, and such as establishing justice among people, and the like. Jurists have derived objectives for the law as established in the books of usul, and the discussion here is not about every legal ruling, but rather about the objectives of the law as a whole, and the impact of those objectives on the laws regulating relationships in society.
(92) Among the legal sciences parallel to the science of the objectives of Islamic law is the philosophy of law (or jurisprudence), which has taken a leading position in Western legal studies under the title: (Philosophy of law), due to the nature of its subjects that focus on studying the rules of granting authority and obedience, the goals and wisdoms underlying legal systems, and revealing the relationship between law and society. Generally, it revolves around: protecting "human rights," achieving justice and the common good; legal stability and legal security, and every goal has foundations upon which it stands and means that lead to its realization. It examines the nature of law, especially in its relationship to human values, practices, and societies, and ethics—its connection to some ethical elements; according to the natural law school, or conversely: the necessity of its separation from them as established by the positivist school for this separation; so that the law does not contain any ethical elements in its content. This philosophy attempts to establish legislation that is suitable for all laws (not for a specific country's law) at all times (not in a specific era).
