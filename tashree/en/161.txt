The universal term is the term that shares its concept (that is, its meaning and implication) with many individuals, who are the individuals of the term or its particulars. The universal is constituted by its particulars, such as the term "human," which is universal and includes Zayd, who is a particular. Zayd is a human who embodies humanity completely; thus, it is a universal ruling applicable to its particulars, that is, a universal description of the legal reality brought by the evidences, namely the legal texts, in terms of their applicability to many particulars.
The general term is the term that indicates two or more meanings, or it is a term that encompasses everything that is suitable for it, as there are forms and terms for generality such as (any) and (what), and definite collective nouns if they are not for reference, and indefinite collective nouns and the confirmed names for them such as "all" and "everyone," and the indefinite in the context of negation, condition, and questioning.
Therefore, when the speech of the Legislator uses a form of generality, it will encompass everything indicated by the speech, such as His saying, Exalted is He: ﴾And whatever the Messenger has given you, take it; and what he has forbidden you, refrain from it; and fear Allah; indeed, Allah is severe in penalty﴿ [al-Hashr 7]. Thus, the form ﴾what﴿ is a form of generality that encompasses everything brought by the Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him).(269)

(269) The question that arises here is: What is the difference between the singular general term and the singular universal term in terms of generality? And are universality and generality the same in singular terms in terms of language and usage? Yes, they are the same; universality falls under generality. The term "man" has a universal meaning, and when we add the definite article "the," it becomes general, and when we pluralize it to "men," it becomes general. The forms of generality apply to all terms in all their classifications, whether they are classifications in terms of the signifier and the signified or in terms of the signifier alone (the universal), transforming indefinites into definites, and the ruling on them becomes a general ruling. In understanding the legal texts, the discussion of the singular universal term does not arise; rather, the general, specific, absolute, and restricted terms arise... and there are no universal and particular texts in Sharia, nor universal and particular evidences, but there are general and specific texts and general and detailed evidences.
Yes: there are universal principles in Sharia, which are universal rulings, but the universality and particularity in them are not from the perspective of logical reasoning. The use of the term universal for them is metaphorical, not literal. Universality and particularity are categories of the implication of the singular term, not the compound term, and the legal ruling is a compound term, not a singular one. See: the difference between the universal and the general and between the universal principle and the general one by Abdul Hamid al-Shurbati.
(270) As for clarifying that the universal principle is a legal ruling, the universal principle is: the universal ruling applicable to its particulars. As for it being a ruling, it is because it is derived from the speech of the Legislator; it is the implication of the speech of the Legislator. As for this ruling being universal, it is because it is not a matter of attributing a ruling to a term from the terms of generality, so that it can be said that it is a general ruling, like His saying, Exalted is He: ﴾And Allah has permitted trade and prohibited usury﴿ [al-Baqarah 275], which applies to all types of trade; it is a general ruling. Rather, universal principles, legal definitions, and legal rulings are all legal rulings, even if their names differ. "And inferring a ruling from a universal principle, or from a legal definition, or from a legal ruling, is a matter of deriving from the ruling, not of inferring from the evidence."
