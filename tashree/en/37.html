There is nothing between truth and falsehood except falsehood.

Perhaps the eyes of some Muslims have been drawn towards Western values such as democracy and secularism, after the West has surrounded them with an aura of sanctity, adorned them with embellishments, and covered their flaws with fig leaves. Or perhaps some Muslims think there is no harm in adopting some systems from other civilizations, even temporarily, as they believe that temporary solutions are acceptable, as long as the ultimate goal is to implement Islam entirely. However, we say to all of them: there is nothing between truth and falsehood except falsehood!

The entry of falsehood into this system has hindered its goals of liberating humans from the worship of humans, thus entrenching it, contrary to the conditions of truth, which require disbelief in the tyrant (taghut) first, and instead reinforcing belief in the tyrant rather than disbelieving in him!

Therefore, we find that the position of truth towards falsehood is strict, as Islam emerged amidst a massive accumulation of the products of other civilizations: the Persians and their poetry, the Greeks and their myths, metaphysics, and gods, their societal and political systems, the theologians and their philosophy, the Indians and their mysticism and gods. The centers of those concepts and philosophies were deep within the Arabian Peninsula and its outskirts where Islam arose(74). They were not far from it, yet it did not take from all of this even a speck or less, neither doctrinal concepts nor systems of life!

Truth came with a goal that cannot be achieved by adopting values from other civilizations, which is the pleasure of Allah, Exalted is He, and His reward for deeds: Paradise, and His promise of victory, empowerment, and succession for those who believe and do righteous deeds. It came mandating that commands and prohibitions be derived exclusively from revelation, for Islam is taken only from revelation, exclusively, and accountability is only based on following revelation or turning away from it exclusively. The command is to follow revelation exclusively, and guidance is only through what has been revealed by revelation, exclusively. There is no place for reason in legislation except for understanding and deduction, and rulings do not change with the change of time and place. How could it be, when the Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him) has no right to change it on his own, so how about those who are beneath him (peace and blessings be upon him)?

Thus, one of the essential implications of the Islamic methodology is to impose complete emotional isolation from ignorance (jahiliyyah) and its systems, especially with its highest stages represented by the tyrant, as the position towards it is one of pure disbelief, isolation from connections with the ignorant society and its values and customs, and a close connection with Islamic values, and a complete detachment and rejection of the concepts of ignorance, falsehood, and tyranny, and total loyalty to truth and its cause, interspersed with a purification that distinguishes the truth and its people from falsehood, distinguishing the good from the bad!

The Islamic methodology clarifies that it is not permissible to mix truth with falsehood, ﴾And do not mix truth with falsehood﴿ [al-Baqarah 42], as mixing, as Ibn Ashur (may Allah have mercy on him) says, "is a confusion between similar attributes that makes it difficult or impossible to distinguish, and it extends to what has been confused by several letters such as 'ala, the lam, and the ba' depending on the context that necessitates the meaning of some of those letters... Thus, mixing truth with falsehood is the promotion of falsehood in the guise of truth, and this mixing is the principle of misguidance and heresy in matters.

Other centers included Edessa, Nisibis, Jundaysabur, and Harran south of Edessa. From these centers, those ideas spread and influenced Syriac culture, then Christian culture mixed with paganism (see: The Dawn of Islam, Ahmad Amin, pp. 150-155). Therefore, the environment surrounding the Arabian Peninsula was burdened with Greek, Egyptian, Persian, theological, and pagan philosophy (see our book: Theory of Knowledge and Methods of Thinking and Reasoning, chapter: Methods of Thinking between the Greeks and Muslims).
