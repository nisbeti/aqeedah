The human aspects or ethical issues are not the intended focus; rather, the purpose is merely worship. Therefore, one must consider achieving its spiritual value alone, regardless of other values.

These are the values for all actions, and they are what a person strives to achieve when performing any of their actions.

The measure of human societies in their worldly life is based on these values, and it is proportional to how much of them is realized in society, and what guarantees their realization in terms of welfare and tranquility.

When the intellect judges an action, it must link it to the intention behind it and see its impact on society and relationships, so that it achieves progress and civilization for humanity, rather than regression, degradation, and absurdity that reflect negatively on the individual and society.

When secularism states that the judgment of an action should not be influenced by values, it thus separates the action from the values intended to be achieved at the individual level, for example, it disregards his spiritual aspirations and instinctual needs, and it nullifies the human value in saving drowning people, not making it a basis for the perspective on the act of rescuing the drowning person. So, what will remain after separating the action from its value, intention, and purpose?

Moreover, when a person engages in many actions, they find themselves "innately" aligned with the values that these actions achieve. For instance, it may not occur to them that they are realizing a human value when saving a drowning person, but it is undoubtedly part of the instinct to preserve the species that is ingrained in them. How can secularism seek to separate a person from their instincts and from what is innately embedded in their depths that makes them human?

The intellect cannot judge a matter as good or bad, and thus cannot determine the correctness or incorrectness of performing it, as it may conceive that goodness and badness are inherent in the action—like knowledge and ignorance, truthfulness and falsehood—while the truth is that there is no inherent goodness or badness in actions. To issue a judgment that killing the killer is good, it requires imposing certain moral or religious values on the action, thus judging the killing of an innocent soul as bad based on those values that are external to the action.

Secularism seeks to prevent the imposition of values on the action when judging it. Are goodness and badness inherent in actions, evident, and does the judgment of the intellect on them remain consistent at all times and places? Is there only "white" and "black"?

If actions themselves had the capacity to indicate the necessary judgment regarding them, isolated from the values that drive the legislator to rule in favor of one of the prohibitions, obligations, or permissibility, then the perspective of the secular world would be uniform for all actions, and the legislations in Britain, America, and France, for example, would be the same. However, their differences indicate that the source of this difference is the influence of certain values in the minds of the legislators, thus nullifying the possibility of isolating judgment from value, and it becomes impossible for the intellect to be neutral when issuing judgments on actions.

The intellect may differ in its view of the action itself; one person's intellect may judge killing as a good thing, as Hitler, for example, saw the justification for killing those who oppose the superiority of the Aryan race over other races, while another intellect may find the killing of Hitler himself acceptable, and a third intellect may judge that killing the killer is not commendable. Therefore, you find Western laws varying in their perspective on the punishment of the killer, from permitting the death penalty to prohibiting it.
