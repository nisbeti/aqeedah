Sources of Islamic Legislation: Foundations of Religion and Foundations of Jurisprudence:
Indeed, "the origin of everything is what that thing relies on for its realization"(134)
Two primary sciences have emerged in the history of Islamic civilization regarding foundations: the science of the foundations of religion (usul al-din) and the science of the foundations of jurisprudence (usul al-fiqh). The former addresses the methodologies of reasoning and proof concerning matters of creed, while the latter deals with the methodologies of reasoning and proof regarding matters of legal rulings! (Similarly, the sciences of hadith have divided into the science of knowledge (ilm al-dirayah), which is the science of the foundations of hadith, and the science of narration (ilm al-riwayah), which applies the rulings and principles established in the science of knowledge to each specific hadith).
From these, two sciences branched out: the science of monotheism and creed, which is referred to as the science of theology (kalam), addressing issues of belief, and the science of jurisprudence, which deals with matters of practical legal rulings that govern human behavior in life, encompassing the actions of the legally responsible (mukallaf) individuals, the rulings on human interactions with one another, and the rulings on the systems of the state, life, and society.
Thus, jurisprudence (fiqh) encompasses practical issues (such as the prohibition of usury, the punishment of the thief, the obligation of fasting, the rulings on prayer, purification, zakat and its shares, divorce and marriage, financial transactions, the pledge of allegiance to the caliph, and the apparatus of governance), while the foundations of jurisprudence consist of criteria, rules, a method of thinking, and a research approach, ensuring that its outcomes—namely, the issues of jurisprudence—are based on a correct method of inquiry that protects against error, or are regulated within a framework according to precise parameters derived from the foundations of jurisprudence, allowing the researcher to reach a legally acceptable conclusion, whereby it is said he has exerted effort (ijtihad) and arrived at a preponderance of evidence that the legal ruling on the matter is such and such, and his conclusion is legally acceptable. This is unlike matters of belief, where there is no room for disagreement, as most of them are based on definitive rational issues, the belief in which determines whether a person is a Muslim or a disbeliever; thus, certainty is required in these matters.

Thus, belief must have safeguards added to it to clarify the type of belief that is described as faith, preventing the inclusion of what is mere belief and does not belong to faith. Belief may be uncertain, and thus it is not called a creed or faith; hence, certainty must be safeguarded to prevent uncertain belief from infiltrating and corrupting the conviction. Allah, Exalted is He, said: ﴾And when it is said, "Indeed, the promise of Allah is true, and the Hour is no doubt coming," you said, "We do not know what the Hour is. We assume it is nothing but a conjecture, and we are not convinced."﴿ (Al-Jathiya) [32].
The condition of correspondence to reality is a criterion for eliminating ignorance and preventing error in belief. What is required is belief that corresponds to reality, not mere imagination; that is, the truth we believe in must truly exist or be a cause that is confirmed by events and does not contradict them, and it must correspond to the belief, not merely be an imagined idea that satisfies the instinct of sanctification, for example, believing firmly in something that has no reality, such as the notion of the Trinity or that Allah has a son—Exalted is He.
The condition of evidence (i.e., a cause) prevents imitation in belief, leading to belief at the level of knowledge, certainty, or conviction. This evidence is also understood as the necessary knowledge for entering into faith.
Scholars of the foundations have coined a definition for certainty, stating: Certainty is (in language, knowledge without doubt, and in terminology, the belief that something is such with the belief that it cannot be otherwise, corresponding to reality and not subject to removal). The first condition includes conjecture, the second excludes conjecture, the third excludes ignorance, and the fourth excludes the mistaken imitator. These conditions prevent the infiltration of what could lead to a belief that something is such; thus, the four conditions are: the belief that it is such, the condition of believing that it cannot be otherwise, the condition of correspondence to reality, and the condition of being non-removable, which excludes conjecture, ignorance, and the mistaken imitator, thus preventing anything that would undermine certainty from entering into this essence.
