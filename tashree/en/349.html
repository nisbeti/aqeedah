The community should not be a field for legislative experiments:

Human laws are tested and amended or abolished after social, moral, or economic harms occur. Thus, society is turned into a laboratory for uncertain experiments. There are many examples: laws regarding drug use, abortion, or financial transactions are constantly amended after their negative effects become apparent. This threatens the stability of the legal system and harms individuals and communities.

The necessity for legislation to be stable and applicable to all times and places:

Divine law (sharia) was legislated to be the ultimate framework for human life, and it can only be so if it is:

• Stable in its foundations and principles.

• Broad and comprehensive in its application through disciplined ijtihad (independent juristic reasoning).

• Applicable to all times and places because it originates from the All-Knowing, the Wise.

Thus, the stability of legislation is the foundation of justice and the stability of society.

Legislation is linked to its higher objectives (maqasid al-sharia):

Justice, truth, and mercy cannot be achieved through arbitrary laws; rather, there must be a true understanding of the realities of matters.

Any legislation that does not prevent injustice or establish rights is fundamentally flawed.

The overarching objectives, such as the preservation of religion, life, intellect, wealth, and honor, cannot be safeguarded by fluctuating human laws.

The issue of conflicting goals and interests:

The human mind cannot always balance conflicting values—such as security versus privacy or freedom, or public health versus freedom of choice—without falling into contradictions and subjective judgments.

These dilemmas produce contradictory laws that do not create a coherent system for social behavior and do not uphold the higher objectives and interests; rather, they encroach upon one another to achieve some at the expense of others.

The need for legislative integration across various aspects of life:

Islamic law provides a comprehensive system that unifies belief, ethics, worship, transactions, public order, and policies.

In contrast, positive legal systems suffer from contradictions between economic laws, civil liberties, criminal law, and family law due to the fragmented vision of their drafters.

The foundation from the philosophy of law:

In legal jurisprudence, especially in the theory of "natural law," there is an acknowledgment that true justice must be derived from a source beyond humanity.

Even positivist trends suffer from a lack of moral standards and acknowledge that the validity of law does not always mean its justice.
