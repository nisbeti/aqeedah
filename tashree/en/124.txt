Abu Hanifa states that the ablution (wudu) is not nullified, and this is in accordance with following the different principles on which al-Shafi'i and Abu Hanifa (may Allah be pleased with them) disagreed.
If the follower (muqallid) is one who follows and adheres, he is "someone who possesses some recognized knowledge in legislation, but is unable to exercise independent juristic reasoning (ijtihad), so he follows another in obtaining the ruling while also taking the evidence for the ruling, [and the principles upon which the evidence is based]." It is obligatory for him to adhere to a school of thought (madhhab), or to follow a specific issue in detail, in accordance with the established principles that apply to all rulings of that issue. Even if his actions are sufficient to weigh the evidence according to those very principles he follows, and he sees the opinion of one jurist as stronger than that of another jurist who follows the same principles, "if he takes the ruling but does not act upon it afterward, he is permitted to abandon it and take another based on a preference from the preferences that relate to seeking the pleasure of Allah [provided that his preference is based on the same principles and not on others]." However, if he has acted upon it, then this ruling has become the legal ruling in his case, and it is not permissible for him to abandon it and take another ruling unless the second ruling is accompanied by evidence and the first ruling is not accompanied by evidence, or it has been established to him through teaching that the evidence for the second ruling is stronger than the evidence for the first ruling, and he is convinced of that [provided that it is based on the same principles and not on others]. In this case, he must abandon the first ruling because his conviction in the legal evidence [derived from the same principles and not from others] and his belief in it has made it the legal ruling in his case, analogous to the jurist when he finds stronger evidence than the evidence from which he derived the ruling; he must abandon his previous opinion and adopt the new opinion due to the strength of the evidence. In other cases, it is not permissible for the follower to abandon the ruling he has followed for another if his actions are connected to that ruling."(226)
In addition, just as it is necessary for the jurist to ensure that his ijtihad is connected to a sound system of principles, it is also necessary for the follower not to follow anyone except one whose ijtihad (or reporting of the legal ruling) is known to be connected to a sound system of principles (such as following a jurist of a particular school, or following a scholar whom he knows possesses complete and sound principles and adheres to them in his ijtihad consistently, and who is a jurist in all rulings of the specific issue he is asked about and follows him in all rulings of that issue (like prayer, for example)).
The common follower (al-'ami) is one who does not possess some recognized knowledge in legislation; he asks about the ruling of the action or thing he intends to do, and he follows whoever he asks without knowing the evidence from which the ruling was derived [provided that he chooses for himself a scholar who has sound principles to ask about all the rulings he wishes to act upon, or that he chooses for himself a school of thought and asks someone to give him a ruling on the issue according to that school. However, if he asks a mufti who gives him a ruling once according to the Shafi'i school and another time according to the Hanafi school regarding issues of prayer, it is not permissible for him to follow him due to the same previous prohibitions of mixing between the principles and the rulings based on them].
We previously stated that we must differentiate between the era of the Companions and those who followed them before the corruption of the language, and the era of those who came after that.
It was the practice of the Companions that the general public during their time would ask the jurists among the Companions and follow them in legal rulings, and the scholars among them would promptly respond to their questions without referring to the evidence nor forbidding them from doing so. No one among the Companions denied this, so it was a consensus on the permissibility of the common person following the jurist unconditionally without mentioning the evidence, and on the permissibility of learning and teaching legal rulings without learning the evidence or teaching it.
