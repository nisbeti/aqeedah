There is no doubt that the purpose of legislation is to obligate individuals and society to apply it, and since "this application is contingent upon the individual's own will to obey and implement it, his will over himself is guaranteed if he legislates for himself"(391). However, when legislation comes from others, whether from the Creator or from human legislation, he needs an incentive to compel him to obedience and commitment, especially since laws have consequences such as penalties for transgressions, the weight of responsibilities they impose, and possibly financial repercussions, such as taxes, for example. Laws may also gain the approval of one group of people and the dislike of another, so there must be an incentive that drives individuals and society to apply them.
Islam and Slavery(392)
We will study the method of Islamic legislation in addressing problems by comparing Islam's approach to the issue of slavery with that of human legislators, taking the United States as an example of dealing with the problem. After this comparison, we will proceed to explain the pillars upon which Islam relies to ensure the effective application of its principles at the individual and societal levels, as we will highlight some of those pillars in this example, as a prelude to elaborating on them with Allah's help and guidance:
People today view slavery through the lens of the twenty-first century, envisioning the gladiatorial contests of Roman slaves in the arenas of the "Colosseum," fighting with swords against each other and with wild beasts for the entertainment of their masters, ending in mutual killings and severe injuries, while dozens of slaves sleep piled on top of one another, without the slightest care or rights or humanity. One may wonder: Does Islam permit slavery? The answer is that such slavery is undoubtedly prohibited in Islam!
Islam addressed slavery with a three-stage plan:
It began with restoring the humanity of slaves in their treatment, then preparing for their liberation, and finally, the act of liberation itself.
It dealt with two problems: the problem of the slaves themselves and the problem of enslavement, that is, the institution of slavery itself!
The liberation of slaves does not occur by decree, as when the American legislator sought to resolve the issue of slavery, Abraham Lincoln issued a decree proclaiming the emancipation of slaves in 1863 (two years before his assassination). Yes, the slaves were gradually liberated after that proclamation, but the problem of slavery was not resolved, as the racist practices in America—"the legacy of slavery, the slave trade, and a hundred years of legal segregation that followed the abolition of slavery"—according to the United Nations report itself, continue to this day.
