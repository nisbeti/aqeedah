"Every accountable person is required to refrain from what they know will cause them a significant harm, even if it is originally a permissible food." Thus, "harm is removed by restriction"; that is, what is permissible is prohibited if it leads to a specific harm, and the prohibition is measured according to the extent of the harm, in accordance with the principle that "what leads to harm is prohibited from the permissible."
The outcome and the result (the means): One considers the outcomes of an action; if the action is originally permissible but leads to a predominant subsequent harm (direct or indirect), it takes the ruling of prohibition to prevent the means. For example: Importing and trading are permissible actions, but if reliable studies indicate that importing a certain commodity will destroy a weak local sector (such as farmers or manufacturers) and cause them certain harm, it is permissible for the ruler to prohibit or restrict it "to the extent that alleviates the harm from them." Likewise, if it is observed that the spread of a permissible game or technology leads to predominant social or moral harm, the state takes measures to prevent its harm to the public. The principle is: "What leads to the forbidden is forbidden"; and if the harm arises from a permissible action predominantly, it is prevented to avert its consequence.
Harm in exchange for greater harm (necessity or greater interest): The Sharia has acknowledged that in certain cases, the lesser harm may be tolerated to avert the greater harm or to achieve a higher interest. Some actions involve inflicting intentional harm, but they are not considered blameworthy harm; rather, Allah has legislated them as a general mercy. An example is retribution (qisas) and the punishment for theft, which both entail pain for the offender (relative harm), yet they are legislated due to the preservation of people's lives and property; this is a permissible harm aimed at achieving collective mercy and comprehensive justice. Similarly, jihad involves exposing oneself to death and injury (which is personal harm) in exchange for averting a greater fitna and protecting the community and its religion.
The scientific or legal evidence for the occurrence of harm: An action is not judged to be a sinful harm except based on a predominant or certain assumption of harm resulting from it. As for weak probabilities and doubts not based on evidence, they do not serve as a basis for prohibition. The jurists have agreed on the prohibition of what has been confirmed or is predominant in leading to something unlawful or harmful, but they have emphasized the necessity of verification; not everything that is imagined to cause harm is prohibited merely due to that imagination. The principle states: "Certainty is not removed by doubt" and "There is no ruling for the imagined." Therefore, the jurists require precise estimation from experts in technical matters (such as medicine, environment, and economics) to rule that this action or that causes actual harm that necessitates prohibition.
The prohibition of removing harm with equal or greater harm: When addressing an existing harm, one should not resort to a solution that generates an equal or greater harm. For example, it is not permissible to address a crime with another injustice, nor to remove a wrongdoing with a greater wrongdoing. The principle is: "Harm is not removed by its like." Therefore, a balance must be considered: if removing the harm from one party will cause harm to another party of the same magnitude or greater, alternative solutions should be explored, or efforts should be made to mitigate both harms as much as possible.
These guidelines form the legal framework in the area of defining and estimating harm. The jurist (mujtahid) or the judge considering the public interest (maslaha) can rely on them as decision-making criteria when issuing fatwas: they present any new issue against these rules and examine where its correct description lies. In this way, the Sharia ensures that the definition of "harm" does not deviate according to whims, but remains regulated by the guidance of revelation and its detailed and general proofs, as well as its noble objectives in preserving religion, life, intellect, and lineage.
