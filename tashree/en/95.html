To ensure a correct understanding of the legal ruling and its derivation, what is necessary for the completion of an obligation is itself an obligation. This has led scholars to establish criteria for ijtihad (independent juristic reasoning), necessitating a distinction between the era before the corruption of language and that after it in dealing with the issue of ijtihad and taqlid (following a legal school).

Another important factor influencing the development of the concept of ijtihad and the regulation of its operational procedures is the spread of the Companions (sahaba) in various countries, and the differing methodologies of the Companions in reasoning and ijtihad. This led to the emergence of multiple jurisprudential schools. In Iraq, the school of opinion (ra'y) was established, which relied on qiyas (analogy), with some expansion into istihsan (juridical preference) based on deviating from qiyas when the result of qiyas conflicted with Abu Hanifa's understanding of the governing principles of ijtihad derived from the universals of Sharia and its foundations. He would then prefer what aligns with those principles and foundations. If he did not find a ruling in qiyas, he would refer to the transactions and customs of the people, which were a direct return to living an Islamic life established after ignorance (jahiliyyah). These customs served as a mirror for applying the evidences from the Qur'an and Sunnah, so his return to them was a return to the Qur'an and Sunnah, (just as Imam Malik took the consensus of the people of Medina). It must be noted that the validity of this return was contingent upon the period before the corruption of language and the transition of the customs brought by those who entered Islam from various non-Arab peoples, mixing their customs with those of the Muslims based on the Book and the Sunnah, and its invalidity after that era as a source of legislation. Thus, the designation of the school as one of opinion was due to their extensive branching out of issues based on opinion (qiyas, istihsan, and custom), within the criteria set by the methodology of Abu Hanifa, which traces its roots to the chief of the scholars of Iraq, the Companion Abdullah ibn Mas'ud (may Allah be pleased with him), and some of the judgments of Ali ibn Abi Talib (may Allah be pleased with him)(194). Therefore, Imam Abu Zahra organized the evidences in the principles of Abu Hanifa as follows: the Book, then the Sunnah, then the sayings of the Companions, then consensus, then qiyas, then istihsan, then custom.(195) Imam Abu Hanifa said: "We do not apply qiyas except in cases of extreme necessity, as we look for the evidence of the issue from the Book and the Sunnah or the judgments of the Companions. If we do not find evidence, we then apply qiyas to what is silent about based on what is explicitly stated"[ ^196].(196)

In Medina, the school of hadith was established, relying on the jurisprudence of Abdullah ibn Umar, Zayd ibn Thabit, and Aisha, the Mother of the Believers (may Allah be pleased with them all). Its methodology relies on the text while resorting to opinion (qiyas) when necessary. From this, the Maliki school emerged. In Mecca, the Meccan school arose, based on the jurisprudence of Abdullah ibn Abbas (may Allah be pleased with them), focusing on the interpretation of texts and understanding them in their linguistic context, and reconciling between texts and juristic derivation. This school influenced the jurisprudence of Imam al-Shafi'i (may Allah be pleased with him) in the second century of the Hijra, and the need to establish ijtihad became apparent. Imam al-Shafi'i (d. 204 AH) came to write the first book on the science of usul al-fiqh (principles of jurisprudence), titled "Al-Risala". Al-Shafi'i derived the sources of legislation and arranged them clearly: the Book, then the Sunnah, then consensus, and then qiyas, which made the process of ijtihad more disciplined and methodical. Al-Shafi'i added the importance of organizing the evidences and defining the criteria for qiyas, which established ijtihad as a science in its own right.
