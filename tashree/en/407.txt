The impact of differing temperaments and cultures on the assessment of harm
One important matter when discussing "harm" is the understanding that the assessment of what is harmful and what is beneficial may vary according to different natures and cultures. What one group finds abhorrent, another may appreciate, and what one society considers necessary may be viewed by another as luxury or even corruption. Thus, "what minds perceive as good varies, and what they perceive as bad also varies, and the Sharia is the measure."
The effect of cultural variation is evident: for instance, Western liberal culture tends to narrow the concept of harm to direct physical injury, while Islamic culture expands it to include moral, spiritual, and social harm. Therefore, a Westerner might consider the prohibition of alcohol, for example, as "harmful to personal freedom," because they do not see any harm in drinking it as long as it does not harm anyone; whereas a Muslim considers alcohol to be a grave harm because it corrupts the mind and society even if no immediate incidents occur, relying on the definitive text of revelation that prohibits it. The same can be said about immodesty: some societies may see it as "personal freedom" that harms no one, while Islamic culture considers it a moral harm that spreads temptation and threatens the structure of the family.
Personal temperaments also have an effect: an individual's personality and disposition may lead them to be lenient towards a certain type of harm and tolerate it, while another person may find it completely unacceptable. Take smoking as an example: in the past, it was socially acceptable despite its hidden health harms, because the general mood did not fully recognize its danger; today, with the availability of health studies, the assessment of some societies has changed, and its harm is clearly recognized. This illustrates the relativity of human perception of harm based on knowledge, experience, and custom.
Moreover, the cultural and social framework surrounding the mind can shape and influence it. Thus, people's judgments are colored by their customs, traditions, and the values they were raised upon. Therefore, the mind alone cannot serve as a singular, general criterion for assessing harm; otherwise, the standards would differ to the point of contradiction. What is prohibited in one nation may be permissible in another according to customs, and this is evident in various issues from daily life to major legislations. From here, we understand the wisdom of the Legislator in establishing fixed, objective criteria to define harm, which rise above the differences of desires and cultures. Allah, Exalted is He, said: ﴾And judge between them by what Allah has revealed, and do not follow their desires ...﴿ ﴾5:49﴿, thus commanding judgment by His law and prohibiting following what the desires of a people or the customs of a society dictate.
In summary: temperaments are varied and cultures are changeable, while God's judgment is constant. Therefore, referring to revelation is essential to regulate the standard of harm. The clear mind recognizes the wisdom of the Legislator in this; as the Sharia provides a high, unified standard that distinguishes harm from benefit in a way that achieves justice for all people, regardless of their environments: ﴾And We have not sent you except as a mercy to the worlds﴿ ﴾21:107﴿.
