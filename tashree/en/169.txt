And this will make our position on stability and change, on development and stagnation, clear. It raises the question of whether it is really necessary to change the rulings related to food, for example, because we will be riding an airplane instead of a camel? Certainly not. While some rulings related to travel will be affected as a result, we need to derive them, (and this is a type of renewal that allows jurisprudence to keep pace with the emerging issues of life), such as praying on the airplane, or combining prayers, or a woman traveling without a mahram. Thus, the original rulings have not changed; rather, we needed to apply the rulings to new realities. (Alcohol is forbidden, and the invention of new drinks that fall under the category of alcohol will require us to derive rulings for those drinks based on the ruling of the prohibition of alcohol itself. The rulings did not change with the change of circumstances, but we needed to apply to the new reality (vodka and whiskey) a ruling derived from the evidence related to the prohibition of alcohol). Likewise, we notice that the new rulings (the prohibition of vodka and cannabis) have come from the same evidence that prohibited alcohol and intoxicants, and they did not come from sources other than the texts themselves! Jurisprudential reasoning was employed to derive them using "the same usuli methodology," and we did not change it or introduce foreign elements into it—such as public interest (maslaha), time, and environment.
Similarly, we will see, for example, that our stance on coffee, trade, and marriage will not change just because we have started writing using computers instead of notebooks! From this, we see that raising the issue of changing legal rulings by developing and altering them to suit "the times and human progress" is nothing but a tempest in a teapot!
