Islamic legislation derived from general evidence (such as the Qur'an and the Sunnah) contains various detailed rulings, such as the prohibition of alcohol and the obligation of prayer. It applies to all human actions, which are diverse and varied, regardless of their differences. It has come in the form of "general" or "universal" evidence and rules, as well as in the form of detailed rulings. This characteristic gives the Noble Qur'an and the Pure Sunnah the ability to encompass rulings that address all human problems until the Day of Judgment.

The legal ruling (hukm shar'i) "is the address of the legislator concerning the actions of the servants, either by 'necessity or choice' (i.e., the address of obligation) or by 'status'"(265). Necessity refers to the demand for action or the demand for abstention, and choice (i.e., permissibility) when it provides solutions to problems, meaning the rulings of events and occurrences. This ruling creates a broad line, meaning a general concept; it describes actions in a general manner, coming with specific meanings that apply to everything that falls under them. Thus, when it gives a ruling for an action, it actually gives the ruling to the genus or type of action in a general description, not to a single action or a limited number of actions. For example, the prohibition of theft encompasses everything that the Sharia considers theft, whether it is the theft of gold or a loaf of bread. ﴾And Allah has permitted trade﴿, so the general ruling applies to its parts, whether one sells hides or airplanes. The rulings of status, such as condition, impediment, cause, obligation, concession, validity, nullity, and corruption of contracts, are the same whether they pertain to sale contracts, marriage, or the appointment of a ruler.

Therefore, it applies to every action of its kind or type, and to everything indicated by the general description and that falls under the general meaning if the description is not conditioned. It applies to everything that fits the general description or falls under the general meaning, along with everything that the reason for the ruling applies to if the description is conditioned.

(266) A specific term is any single or compound term that does not include anything else under it, such as (Zaid), a proper name for a man, or (Zaytunah), a proper name for the olive tree, or (al-Khalil), a proper name for the city of Hebron, or (Abdullah), a proper name for a man, ... like the testimony of Khuzaymah, which the Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him) considered as the testimony of two men; this is specific to Khuzaymah (Abu Dawood: 3607, al-Nasa'i: 4647, Ahmad: 5/215, al-Mustadrak: 2/21), and likewise the ruling related to a specific way of prayer, such as the prayer for rain, funeral prayer, or fear prayer, is attributed to a specific term, making it a specific ruling, unlike if it is attributed to prayer as a general term without specification or restriction, it would be a general ruling. (267) A general term is any single term that includes individuals under it, such as: tree, thief, carrion, usury, men, and Muslims.

A universal term is any compound term that includes particulars under it, such as: "what is not completed except by it," "the means to the forbidden."

The "universality" is one of the indications of the singular term, whether it is homonymous or ambiguous (see Chapter 3 - Section 1 - The Singular in Taysir al-Wusul by Abu al-Rashtah). However, if the compound can be formulated in such a way that particulars can fall under it, then the universality applies to it metaphorically due to the relationship of similarity with the singular. In this case, this compound is a universal term that includes particulars. When a legal ruling is attributed to this universal term, it is called a universal rule. (Taysir al-Wusul ila al-Usul by Alim Ataa Abu al-Rashtah, Volume 1, p. 45)

(268) A rule is a fixed, continuous ruling that can serve as a foundation upon which other rulings and ideas are built or under which others can be categorized as a matter of derivation.

The texts from which the universal rules are derived either contain a reason or the meaning of the reason (causality), which makes them similar in their function to legal and logical analogy, such as the saying of the Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him): "There should be neither harm nor reciprocation of harm," and His saying, Exalted is He: ﴾And do not insult those whom they invoke besides Allah, lest they insult Allah in enmity without knowledge﴿ [al-An'am]. The causality and the meaning of the reason are evident in both texts, but the universal rules do not include particulars under them based on the reason or analogy, but rather based on derivation from the ruling, meaning that the universal ruling encompasses the particular ruling. Thus, the legal universal rule is a universal ruling that includes particulars under it; it is a general legal ruling (in its meaning) that serves as a basis from which many rulings branch out, and the rulings derived from it are particular in relation to it. This rule is applicable in all areas of jurisprudence and is not specific to a particular chapter, such as legal principles.
