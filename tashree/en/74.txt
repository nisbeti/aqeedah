Imam al-Shatibi says: "The principles of jurisprudence (usul al-fiqh) in religion are definitive, not speculative, and the evidence for this is that they pertain to the universals of Sharia, and whatever is thus is definitive. [Moreover, it is even more appropriate for beliefs to be so, as is well known.] The explanation of the first is evident through the inductive reasoning that leads to certainty, and the explanation of the second is from several aspects: one of them is that they either return to rational principles, which are definitive, or to the universal induction from the evidences of Sharia, which is also definitive, and there is no third option besides these two except for the combination of both, and the composite of definitives is definitive, and that is the principles of jurisprudence.
The second aspect is that if they were speculative, they would not pertain to a rational matter, as speculation is not accepted in rational matters, nor to a legal universal, because speculation only relates to particulars; if speculation were permissible to pertain to the universals of Sharia, it would also be permissible to pertain to the foundation of Sharia, as it is the first universal, which is not usually permissible – and by universals here, I mean the necessities, needs, and enhancements – and also, if speculation were permissible to pertain to the foundation of Sharia, then doubt would also be permissible regarding it, and there is no doubt about it, and it would be permissible to change and alter it, which contradicts what Allah, Exalted is He, has guaranteed regarding its preservation.
The third aspect is that if it were permissible to establish the speculative as a foundation in the principles of jurisprudence, it would also be permissible to establish it as a foundation in the principles of religion, which is not the case by consensus. Thus, here too, because the relationship of the principles of jurisprudence to Sharia is like the relationship of the principles of religion, and although they differ in rank, they are equal in that they are recognized universals in every community and are included in the preservation of religion from necessities. … And he, may Allah have mercy on him, says: The answer is that the foundation, in any case, must be certain, because if it were speculative, the possibility of contradiction would arise, and such a thing cannot be made a foundation in religion based on inductive reasoning; and he said: Some have stated that there is no way to establish the principles of Sharia through speculation because it is legislation, and we have not been commanded to rely on speculation except in branches." (142)
And "the science of the principles of jurisprudence encompasses the following four matters:
First: the general evidences, their indicative direction, the validity of analogy (qiyas) as proof, and the obligation to act upon the report of the singular (khabar al-wahid).
The general evidences are the definitive evidences established on the obligatory rulings, that is, those from which the legal rulings are derived, which are the Book, the Sunnah, analogy, and consensus (ijma) of the Companions (143),
and the schools of thought have also differed regarding other evidences such as juristic preference (istihsan), public interest (maslaha mursala), customary practice (urf), the legislation of those before us, the opinion of a Companion (madhhab al-sahabi), presumption of continuity (istishab)(144), blocking the means (sadd al-dhara'i)(145), and other types of consensus like the consensus of the Ummah or the people of Medina,

(143) The Book is what has been established by continuous transmission, and it includes both literal and metaphorical meanings, as well as Arabic and non-Arabic expressions. As for the Sunnah: it refers to what has been attributed to the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) from speech, action, approval, or physical or moral attributes, or what has been attributed to a Companion or a Successor, in matters where there is no room for opinion. The methods of its establishment include continuous transmission and singular reports, and the methods of its narration include authentic and interrupted, and the characteristics of its narrators include justice and criticism, and the discussion of abrogation is connected to the Book and the Sunnah, but not to consensus. As for consensus: its reality, evidence, and categories.
(144) "Presumption of continuity is not a legal evidence; otherwise, it would require definitive proof for its establishment, and no definitive proof has been established for it; rather, it is a legal ruling, and a speculative evidence suffices for it." Islamic Personality, Volume Three, Principles of Jurisprudence, p. 447.
(145) As for the prohibition of means (sadd al-dhara'i), al-Amidi does not mention it among the recognized evidences nor among the imagined evidences in his view. The prohibition of means is not a principle, but rather a rule that falls under a general principle, which is the principle of the outcomes of actions. This principle or rule, along with the rules that fall under it, such as the prohibition of means, the rule of legal stratagems (hiyal), the rule of considering differences, and others, all refer to the fact that Sharia has come to bring about benefits and avert harms. For those who hold this understanding, the rule of the prohibition of means is recognized by al-Shatibi. Mursala benefits, Mahmoud Abdul Karim Hassan, p. 28.
