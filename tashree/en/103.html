Dealing with the understanding of language from a foundational perspective is essential for the mujtahid (jurist) to utilize that understanding in deriving legal rulings, such as the use of metaphor in the Qur'an, and whether the original meaning is the literal one and its relation to deviating from the linguistic context. How does the usuli (foundational scholar) deal with that? When is metaphor necessary? For example, when a name is added to something literally, but it is impossible to add it to that thing, it must be understood metaphorically in relation to something else, such as His saying, ﴾ وَاسْأَلِ الْقَرْيَةَ الَّتِي كُنَّا فِيهَا ﴿ [82]. The categories of action, the meanings of letters, and their types... All of these linguistic discussions are prerequisites for the principles of jurisprudence that determine for the mujtahid how to understand texts and interact with words systematically during the process of ijtihad (independent juristic reasoning).

As for the third and final section, it covers legal principles and rulings, such as the first principle: Who is the ruler? To whom do improvement and disapproval belong? And that there is no ruling before the arrival of the Sharia (Islamic law), and in the essence of the legal ruling (its definition), and its categories, the address of obligation, the address of status, and the address of choice. In what is ruled upon, which are the actions one is obligated to perform, and whether it is permissible to impose obligations that cannot be fulfilled, and in what is ruled against, the conditions of the obligated person, coercion to act, and the types of legal evidence. And to delve into the depths of the comprehensive legal principles that encompass the secrets and wisdoms of the Sharia, meaning the governing principles (the juristic opinions derived from the foundations and universals of the Sharia) that are fundamentally derived and govern the subsidiary ijtihads, such as the principle of "no harm and no reciprocating harm," and the principle of resolving disputes by the authority and its responsibility for its subjects. These are indeed the principles of the foundations of jurisprudence upon which one relies, and no mujtahid can be excused for being unaware of them, as ignorance of them cannot coexist with the ability to understand texts and apply them to realities in a manner that justifies the mujtahid and allows for the assertion that he has exerted his utmost effort.
