Because these fundamental questions of the major dilemma are essential, as they are not built upon prior ideas, they alone produce a principle upon which other ideas are constructed. If a person does not have a thought about himself, life, and the universe in terms of existence and creation, purpose and destiny, organization and management, he cannot provide a thought that serves as a foundation for his life. Therefore, his life remains aimless, fluid, colorful, shifting, and wandering from one extreme to another, unless this foundational thought exists, meaning that the comprehensive idea about himself, life, and the universe is absent.

And since all the ideas that branch from these answers are built upon a fundamental comprehensive idea, they alone are called a principle. Thus, the term principle is applied to the comprehensive idea and its systems, that is, the creed and its treatments; the term principle corresponds to the term ideology in Western expression.

However, this creed cannot give rise to ideas, nor can ideas be built upon it unless it is a thought, meaning it is the result of intellectual inquiry. If it is merely submission and reception, it is not considered a thought, nor is it called a comprehensive idea, even if it may be correctly termed a creed (like the Christian creed, for example, even if it is not rational). Therefore, it is necessary for the comprehensive idea to be reached by a person through reason, meaning it must be the result of intellectual inquiry, thus becoming a rational creed, from which ideas can emerge and upon which ideas can be built. These ideas are treatments for life's problems, meaning they are the rulings that organize a person's affairs in life.

Whenever this rational creed exists and rulings emerge from it to address life's problems, the principle has been established. Thus, the principle is defined as a rational creed from which a system emerges.

Since Islam has established the creed as a comprehensive idea, it thus provides an answer to everything, forming a concept that a person can take as a measure against the reality he wishes to engage with. This measure guides him through the complexities of life with intellectual leadership, such that the concepts of the creed about life provide treatments for life's problems, meaning they offer rulings that organize a person's affairs. From here, the Islamic system connects reality with the necessary solutions for human living.

It is also teleological, as Islamic legislation is characterized by its establishment of laws with intended goals behind them, and wisdoms that motivate them, as well as causes and objectives that drive them. That is, it will set standards for laws and legislations, forming the framework within which all laws operate in various fields of life (434). The principle is that laws should complement each other, supporting one another. For example, the system of punishments is based on the proper performance of the economic system, social solidarity, and the responsibilities of the state in its guardianship. Thus, laws are established to serve the values that are intended to prevail in people's lives (i.e., the concepts and convictions upon which authority, i.e., the state, is based), such as the value of justice (it is said: justice is the foundation of kingship).

The Islamic creed is a rational creed that aligns with human nature and is consistent with the definitive evidence scattered throughout the universe and within the self.
