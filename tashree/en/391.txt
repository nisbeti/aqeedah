The third consideration: Goodness is the entitlement to reward and praise, while badness is the entitlement to punishment and blame.
It has been said that the first and second considerations are for the intellect! This is an incorrect statement, for reasons we will study shortly, God willing. The lesson, as you have understood from the discussion in the previous two chapters, is not merely to issue a judgment, any judgment, but rather to ensure the correctness of the judgment and its ability to address the problem correctly!
As for describing actions as good or bad, it is from the perspective of judgment upon them by humans, and regarding the punishment and reward for them. Humans have given themselves the authority to judge an action as good or bad based on comparisons with things. When they found that they could judge a bitter thing as bad and a sweet thing as good, and an ugly form as bad and a beautiful form as good, they saw that they could judge truthfulness as good and falsehood as bad, and loyalty as good and treachery as bad. Indeed, truthfulness in battle is bad; it is an external consideration that governs behavior. The action in itself is devoid of the determinants of goodness and badness. Thus, they gave themselves the authority to judge actions as good or bad regardless of the subject of good and evil, which is not relevant to them in this case (that is, the discussion here is from the perspective of goodness and badness, not good and evil). Based on this judgment, they imposed penalties on the bad action and rewards on the good action.
The correction to this judgment came in that an action is not measured against a thing; rather, a thing perceives bitterness, sweetness, ugliness, and beauty, allowing it to be judged, with significant differences among minds in their evaluations. Is the consideration of sweetness as goodness a correct judgment? Why? There may be a remedy in bitterness for diseases, for example, while sweetness may have countless harms for humans!
As for the action, there is nothing in it that a person can sense to judge it as bad or good. Thus, it is not possible to judge it as good or bad absolutely from the action itself (and the fact that oppression is something that is praised or blamed is not something that a person senses; it is not something that can be felt, so it cannot be rationalized, meaning the intellect cannot issue a judgment on it. For example: when our master Umar ibn al-Khattab would appoint governors over the provinces, he would count their wealth, then recount it when their term ended, making certain estimates and depositing some surplus money in the public treasury, as he believed that the governor was not appointed as a merchant, and that people might flatter him due to his position, thus enriching themselves in an unlawful manner, even if he was not aware of it. Meanwhile, those whom he appointed to govern the provinces might see otherwise; their judgment on the surplus money might differ, as they might view its deposit in the public treasury as an injustice to them. Therefore, the criteria that determine whether an action is oppression or not are external to the action itself (i.e., to the act of depositing surplus money in the public treasury in our example). Consequently, the standards that apply to the study of oppression and justice are external to the action itself, and based on them, it is determined whether the action is just or oppressive).
Judgment on an action or thing is made by sensing it, and this sensation is transmitted to the brain to issue a judgment on it. As long as the action or thing is not perceivable, a judgment cannot be issued on it. However, the lesson is not merely to issue any judgment but to ensure the correctness of the judgment and its ability to address related human problems correctly and permanently.
