Religion is a way of life that regulates the relationship between the Creator and the created:
"If we look into the derivation of this word (religion) and its forms of inflection, we find(113) that it returns to three meanings, which are almost synonymous, and there is a slight difference between these three meanings, which is actually due to the fact that the word intended for explanation is not a single word, but three words, or more accurately: it encompasses three actions in alternation:
Its explanation: The word "religion (al-din)" is derived sometimes from a transitive verb by itself: "he judged him (dana yahdinu)" and sometimes from a transitive verb with "to" (lam): "he judged for him (dana lahu)" and sometimes from a transitive verb with "by" (bi): "he judged by it (dana bihi)", and with the difference in derivation, the conceptual meaning varies:
Our saying "he has a religion" means: he possesses it, governs it, manages it, and holds it accountable. Thus, in this usage, religion (din) revolves around the meaning of ownership and authority, as pertains to the affairs of kings, including governance, management, ruling, oppression, and accountability. From this is ﴾Master of the Day of Judgment﴿, meaning the Day of accountability and recompense. In the hadith: "The wise one is the one who holds himself accountable," meaning he judges and controls it.
And when we say: "He submitted to him," we mean that he obeyed him and submitted to him. Thus, religion (din) here refers to submission, obedience, and worship. The phrase "Religion is for Allah" can be understood to mean both: judgment is for Allah, or submission is for Allah.
And when we say "he adhered to something," it means he took it as a religion and a doctrine, that is, he believed in it, or became accustomed to it, or was characterized by it. Thus, religion, in this sense, is the doctrine or the way that a person follows, either theoretically or practically.
The gist of the matter regarding these linguistic meanings is that the word "religion" (din) among the Arabs refers to a relationship between two parties, where one party venerates the other and submits to it. If the first party is described, it signifies submission and obedience; if the second party is described, it signifies command, prohibition, authority, judgment, and obligation. When viewed in terms of the unifying bond between the two parties, it serves as the constitution that organizes that relationship, or the manifestation that expresses it. The essence revolves around the meaning of "necessity of submission," so religion in the first usage is the obligation of submission, in the second usage it is the commitment to submission, and in the third usage it is the principle to which a person commits to submit: "(114) And the summary of what has been mentioned is that we are faced with two parties: one is of the utmost exaltation, elevation, and authority, while the other is of the utmost humility, submission, and servitude to the first, and the relationship and methodology that governs the second's relationship with the first is what we call: religion"^[115](115)
It is mentioned in the Tafsir al-Bahr al-Muhit by Abu Hayyan al-Andalusi: ﴾Indeed, the religion with Allah is Islam﴿. The linguists have stated: "The term 'din' (religion) is a noun, while 'dīn' (with a kasra) is the name, and 'din' refers to governance, and 'dayyan' is the one who governs." In Lisan al-Arab, among the meanings of 'din' is: "Religion means the law and authority"; from this is His saying, Exalted is He: ﴾And fight them until there is no fitnah and the religion is all for Allah﴿. End of quote. Ibn Alaan al-Siddiqi mentioned in Dalil al-Falihin: "In al-Kashaf regarding His saying, Exalted is He: ﴾Indeed, we are indebted﴿ [as-Saffat: 53], its meaning is that we are governed, meaning ruled, from 'din' in the sense of governance, and from this is the hadith 'The wise one is the one who governs himself'(116)." (116) Some of the commentators have said that its meaning is to hold oneself accountable, but the more accurate meaning extends to governing oneself so that one does not fall into what one will be held accountable for. Do you not see that he (peace and blessings be upon him) explained it further by saying: "The wise one is the one who holds himself accountable and works for what comes after death, while the wicked one is the one who follows his desires and makes wishes upon Allah, the Exalted." Thus, the wicked one follows his desires, while the wise one governs himself according to the rulings of Allah and restrains his desires. Al-Azhari said in Tahdhib al-Lughah: Abu Ubaid said: His statement: "He held himself accountable" means he humbled and enslaved himself... and the religion for Allah from this is nothing but obedience to Him and worship of Him.
