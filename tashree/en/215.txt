(328) Enabling subjects, both Muslims and dhimmis, to all rights and to benefit from wealth, from public state facilities, and ensuring their basic needs, and equality among subjects in rights, Muslims and People of the Book, to them what is ours in fairness, and upon them what is ours in redress. As for "to them what is ours in fairness," this comes from the general statement of Allah (Exalted is He): ﴾And when you judge between people, judge with justice﴿ and His saying: ﴾And let not the hatred of a people prevent you from being just. Be just; that is nearer to righteousness﴿ and His saying regarding judging between the People of the Book: ﴾And when you judge, judge between them with justice﴿. As for "upon them what is ours in redress," this comes from the fact that the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) would impose punishment on non-Muslims just as he would on Muslims; the Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him) executed a Jew as punishment for killing a woman, and he was brought a Jewish man and woman who had committed adultery, and he stoned them. The dhimmis have upon us the same protection as Muslims, as the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) said: "Whoever kills a person under a covenant with Allah and His Messenger has broken the covenant of Allah, and he will not smell the fragrance of Paradise, although its fragrance can be detected from a distance of forty years." The Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) was brought a Muslim who killed a Jew, and he killed him for that, saying: "We are more deserving of fulfilling our covenant." The dhimmis have the same care for their affairs and guarantee for their livelihood as Muslims. Abu Wail narrated from Abu Musa or one of them with his chain that the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) said: "Feed the hungry, visit the sick, and free the captive." Abu Ubaid said: "And likewise, the dhimmis are defended against those who attack them, and their captives are freed. If they are rescued, they return to their covenant and promise as free people, and there are many hadiths on this." (329) "Islamic legislation preceded positive techniques in adopting the principle of legality of crimes and punishments, which is well-known in criminal and punitive laws, described as the most agreed-upon principle in human history, and it requires that there is no crime or punishment except by text. Islamic legislation clearly distinguishes between crimes for which Allah (Exalted is He) has prescribed punishments that cannot be substituted for others, such as the punishments for fixed penalties (hudud) - apostasy, adultery, theft, slander - and other crimes for which the ruler has been delegated to determine their elements and the prescribed punishments, which are discretionary crimes (ta'zir). There is no way to consider an act a crime unless it is so by the text of the Sharia or the authority of the ruler, even if the judge believes it contradicts justice, morals, or religion. It is completely prohibited for him to apply analogy (qiyas) to criminal Sharia rulings, and the juristic disagreement regarding analogy in punishments is only about its source in legislation, not in judicial application, meaning whether it is a valid source for enacting punishments, not for applying them."
The justice of punishment in the Islamic system is based on four pillars: entitlement, equivalence, care, and purification. It is not like the justice of positive law, which has one or at most two dimensions, but rather multiple dimensions: one related to society, another related to the victim, and a third related to the perpetrator himself; justice that concerns society and faces it, justice that concerns the victim and faces him, and justice that concerns the perpetrator and faces him. Combining all of this is quite difficult, and through the four previous pillars, the purposes of punishment are determined: - Expiation of the crime and purification of the perpetrator. - Achieving justice between the perpetrator and the victim. - General deterrence. - Specific deterrence and reform of the perpetrator. Thus, it can be said that the philosophy of justice in punishment in Islam fundamentally addresses society, while in positive law, it addresses the system."
