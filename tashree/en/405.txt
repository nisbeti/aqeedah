Appendix 2: Reason and Revelation in Defining the Concept of "Harm"
This appendix complements the research: The Limits of Islamic Legislation; Where Does Obligation Begin and Where Does It End? What is the Role of Reason and What is the Role of the Law?
Usulic studies have confirmed that human reason is incapable of fully distinguishing between benefit and harm in isolation from the light of revelation. For what is good and what is bad - from the Islamic perspective - is referred back to the Lawgiver, not to whims or independent reason; for "the one who determines what is good and what is bad ... and clarifies that a certain action contains harm or leads to harm is the Law, not reason." Leaving the definition of harm to whims and abstract minds is a great risk, as minds are relative and influenced by environment, upbringing, and culture. Moreover, the reasoning of each individual may differ in judgment from that of another, so there is no "collective or societal mind," which means that judgments can be confused and contradictory, and reason is not an infallible neutral force. Therefore, rational judgments differ among individuals and societies; indeed, the perspective of a single individual can change with their circumstances and experiences. This means that what one person considers good may be seen by another as bad, and what one nation views as a legitimate solution may be seen by another as a deviation and an assault on values. Even fundamental concepts such as justice, truth, and duty can vary widely in people's perceptions.
Thus, the legal principles have affirmed that "there is no ruling before the arrival of the Law"; the abstract conception of reason regarding benefits and harms is not valid unless it is illuminated by revelation and its regulations. The Qur'an has warned against following whims in legislation: ﴾And if the truth had followed their desires, the heavens and the earth and whoever is in them would have been corrupted﴿ [Al-Mu'minun 71], and condemned the arbitration of anything other than the Law of Allah in the most significant matters: ﴾Do they seek the judgment of ignorance?﴿ [Al-Ma'idah 50]. Accordingly, allowing reason to define harm in isolation from the Law leads to varying standards and biases in legislation towards whims and private interests. One of the dangers of this is that desires can become a legislative reference in the name of benefits: deviations are legitimized under the pretext of "personal freedom" or "public interest," leading to the legalization of evils and the dilution of moral and social values. For this reason, the Sharia has decisively stated: "There should be neither harm nor reciprocating harm," guiding that the determination of harm revolves around the rulings of revelation wherever they may be.
In summary, abstract reason cannot establish a fixed objective standard for defining harm. Whoever allows minds to legislate according to their whims will see judgments become confused and contradictory among different groups, and the Law will lose its stability and binding nature. Therefore, there must be a transcendent reference (divine revelation) that governs all people with an absolute standard that ensures justice and regulates good and evil, benefits and harms.
Contemporary Examples of the Deviation in Defining "Harm" by Abstract Reason
To illustrate the dangers of leaving the determination of harm to reason and whims, let us consider contemporary real-world examples from various fields:
Economic Field: The modern capitalist system views interest as beneficial to the economy and considers its prohibition a "harm" to investment. This utilitarian mental conception contradicts the definition of revelation; Islam has prohibited usury and considered it one of the greatest social and economic harms, due to the injustice and wrongful consumption of people's wealth.
