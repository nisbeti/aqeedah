Military affairs, customs duties, and the state's monopoly on minerals and certain goods were known in Islamic law since the ninth and tenth centuries, but they have become a model that all of Europe has emulated.
Historians say that the credit for the introduction of Maliki jurisprudence into Europe goes to Pope Sylvester II, who studied in Córdoba and was particularly drawn to the jurisprudence of Imam Malik ibn Anas. He devoted himself to it with exceptional care, achieving maximum effectiveness, and immersed himself in its study for many years. Upon his return to France, he came back laden with this jurisprudence and its principles, including contracts, compensations, sales, and conditions.
Among the examples of these comparisons: among the laws that affirm that French legislation largely derives from Maliki jurisprudence is, for instance, in contracts, where we find that what is stated in French law regarding the conditions for the validity of contracts through offer and acceptance by the contracting parties, the general conditions for the validity of the contract, defects in the contract, the freedom of the contracting parties, and the transfer of ownership to the principal, all of this is found in the jurisprudence of Imam Malik ibn Anas (may Allah be pleased with him).
The same applies to ownership, its proof, its establishment, the ruling on possession and its conditions and consequences, that possession of movable property is equivalent to proof of ownership, the permissibility of expropriation for public benefit, the rules of joint ownership, the determination of ownership of mines, the methods of transferring ownership in movable and immovable property, and the expiration of rights due to the passage of time.
The most important type of partnership today in the West is the partnership known as qirad (mudarabah), and qirad is considered the most important type of partnership in the Maliki school because it does not affect the capital contributed; rather, the liability is limited to the partner's share in the partnership, meaning that the capital providers are liable only to the extent of their investment, as in French law and other European laws, especially German law.
The American philosopher of Italian origin, Giorgio Santillani, a professor of the history of science at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), says: "It is remarkable that this Arab law has guided us to these details in commercial law, such as limited liability companies," and he gives many examples of commercial laws derived from Islamic legislation.
The English orientalist Herbert George Wells states: "Europe owes its international commercial laws to Muslims"(345).
However, I would like to draw attention to an important issue regarding Napoleon's adoption of Maliki jurisprudence and the formulation of French laws based on it. We are against promoting Western laws; it should not be understood that we are saying to the West, "Here, take your laws; this is our merchandise returned to us." It is important to highlight the difference between constitutional jurisprudence and the constitution itself. In the West, they have not taken anything from Islam in this regard; rather, their system is a liberal democratic secular system, and the constitution outlines the form of the state, its institutions, and the selection of the ruler, and so forth, which they did not derive from Islam. As for the specific laws that regulate the lives of individuals, such as the stance on trade, social relations, and others, these are what Napoleon took regarding trade and partnerships, especially in the areas of provisions, contracts, obligations, and ownership from Maliki jurisprudence; they do not change the form of his state, but rather its detailed laws!
