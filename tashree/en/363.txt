But it is legislation merely as a means to achieve goals related to regulating worldly life for the happiness of people. In this context, the advantages of Islamic law (sharia) emerge, which was established by the Creator, who knows best the conditions of His servants, and is most aware of what is in their best interest and righteousness, and what their ultimate outcome will be. It is distinguished from man-made techniques by characteristics and traits that make it more worthy of adherence and more deserving of application, as opinions do not control its enactment, nor do whims interfere with its direction. "The law of any nation is a mirror of its material, intellectual, and social conditions. If it is assumed that the law should be the source of happiness and progress for every society, as legal philosophers say, it will not be so if it does not fulfill their cultural, intellectual, and material aspirations dictated by the nature of their religious and social environment. Rather, it will be a burden upon them and a source of their misery and suffering, not their happiness and progress. Correct legislation is born from the spirit of society and is a result of its customs and culture. The legal cultural subjectivity is rooted in the collective behavior of society, which has developed through habit and tradition, according to the moral and religious values of the community."(501)
There is no doubt that the fluctuation of laws from one extreme to another will not achieve stability or justice. For example, when the law criminalizes the trade of cannabis and punishes the trader, then changes to legalize this trade so that it becomes on every street corner, the initial punishment becomes an injustice when viewed alongside the new legalization. Then, if it flips back to the opposite again, it will have inflicted upon society a health, economic, and moral harm of great detriment, with no room for repair. Therefore, it is not appropriate for society to be "a testing ground for legislators," and a fluctuating law cannot achieve any justice in society or any stability in its relationships.
The jurist, or mujtahid, or judge, or legal scholar studies the reality to be judged, and then examines the relevant legal or sharia texts (constitutional) related to that reality, applying the ruling to the reality. During this process, he brings to mind those objectives and recalls their reasons, contexts, and wisdoms when deriving the ruling!
The aim of this legislation is to achieve specific objectives(502), among them: providing correct solutions to problems, establishing truth, justice, and fairness, preventing oppression, and performing the most beneficial action that satisfies instinct and organic needs in a proper manner, which is suitable for people and reforms them, and is bearable for them, alleviating hardship from them, and establishing society on certain foundations where the systems of life and the state are integrated, while considering the eight essential objectives(503) for humanity, which are: the preservation of life, property, religion, intellect, and lineage, the preservation of the state, the preservation of security, and the preservation of human dignity. It may also include (on the basis of the above) the preservation of public interest (maslaha) and the promotion of welfare.

(501) The Miraculous Sharia, Dr. Muhammad Wafiq Zain al-Abidin, p. 18.
(502) Refer to the chapter on the objectives of the sharia in the book Islamic Personality, Volume Three, Usul al-Fiqh by the scholar Taqi al-Din al-Nabhani (may Allah have mercy on him), which contains details for understanding what we mean by the objectives of the sharia and the criteria that are difficult to enumerate here in order to understand the context of our discussion in light of those criteria.
(503) Imam al-Juwayni and his student Imam al-Ghazali derived the objectives of the sharia from the Shafi'i school, and Imam al-Shatibi followed them, identifying five essential objectives: the preservation of life, property, religion, intellect, and lineage. Imam Taqi al-Din al-Nabhani added the objectives of preserving security, preserving the state, and preserving human dignity, deriving them through the imposition of strict penalties on those who commit crimes that violate them legally. For example, those who rebel against the state with arms are fought, and those who break the pledge of obedience and pledge allegiance to a second caliph are killed, among other rulings and details that can be found in the book Islamic Personality, Volume Three. Thus, the essential objectives became eight.
