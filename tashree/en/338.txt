Detail: The capacities of minds vary in strength and weakness, in assessing harm and benefit, and understandings differ among people. What one person sees as good, another sees as bad. The estimation of minds varies regarding the methods that achieve interests, and minds differ in their understanding of the harmony of laws with the fabric of the general law in the state and their realization of the system of values and legislative objectives. There are also other factors whose existence cannot be denied, which lead to the inability of the mind to truly grasp interests,
There is no such thing as a "pure, absolute, or abstract intellect" that directs the course of social development, characterized as "good by nature," and devoid of passions and biases. Moreover, the intellect that is free from deficiency and desire, which is not influenced by lust and ignorance, does not exist in the realm of reality; rather, it is an "ideal." One of the most prominent manifestations of the limitations of human intellect in the field of legislation is its lack of a stable objective criterion for understanding values and determining solutions. The human intellect is not an abstract entity free from bias, nor is it a purely rational force capable of producing universal and binding legislation for all people. Rather, it is a relative intellect, influenced by cultural environment, social affiliations, personal experiences, temporal context, and the psychological and cognitive factors surrounding it. As a result, rational judgments differ among individuals and communities, and even the intellect of a single individual varies in evaluating issues based on their different stages and experiences.
What one mind perceives as good may be seen by another mind as evil, and what one nation considers a good trait or a permissible solution may be viewed by another nation as deviation or error; indeed, it may be seen as an aggression against values and identity. This variation is not limited to traditions or customs, but extends to fundamental concepts such as "justice" (adl), "freedom" (hurriya), "rights" (huquq), "duty" (wajib), and "public interest" (maslaha). Therefore, the legislations produced by human minds will always be subject to change and fluctuation according to these differing perspectives, which prevents the realization of the condition of stability and permanence necessary in legislation, and hinders the production of a legal system that possesses the quality of obligation or gains general acceptance.
For example, justice is a value that is universally agreed upon as good and correct, but the dilemma is how to achieve justice? This can only be accomplished by establishing a sound system that realizes justice. Therefore, the value of justice is achieved through external matters, not from itself, and these external matters are the regulations of actions, namely the legislations and systems. Given human ignorance of consequences and results, one may perceive the achievement of justice in matters that turn out to produce results that involve injustice. Similarly, this can be said regarding the pursuit of benefits and the prevention of harms.
Moreover, the interest (maslaha) may pertain to the individual alone, to bring about his own benefits, and the sought interest may be a public interest. Here, people's perspectives on what achieves these interests and what wards off harms conflict, and desires, immediate views, and trial and error take control, resulting in a devastating outcome for society. "What is the interest that is brought about, and what is the harm that is averted? Is it what a person loves and hates? Or is it what benefits him and harms him? Or is it good and evil? Or is it beautiful and ugly? Or is it something else? There is no doubt that we need criteria by which we can judge that the system brings about interest and wards off harm!"
