A question about the principle of "no harm and no harming," does the legislator leave the determination of harm to reason?

If we take a practical example of one of the general principles, such as: no harm and no harming, the question arises: does the legislator leave the determination of harm to reason, and thus, how does this align with our statement that one of the reasons preventing reason from being able to issue rulings and legislations is that it does not possess the ability to distinguish good from evil, benefit from harm, and beauty from ugliness? How then does the legislator enable reason here to determine harm, and consequently, it becomes that which involves harm is prohibited?

The answer is that the matter is indeed a general and comprehensive legislation, not a detailed legislation, because the legislator does not specify detailed rulings for every issue related to human activity individually, nor does he clarify in each detailed aspect that harm is realized by confining it to a specific form, such as saying that the height of the wall separating you from your neighbor to prevent exposing their nakedness should be at least two meters, for example. Or, for instance, it is observed in many substances that people consume that they may harm some without harming others (harm varies); tobacco may cause cancer for one person but not for another, and some people may be allergic to certain plants and foods, and their sensitivity may differ from one to another and what results from consuming those substances. Some people may have severe allergies that could lead to death from peanut oil. Therefore, the legislator does not provide detailed rulings nor does he give each issue the limits that separate harm from what precedes harm, but the legislator used a general term with individuals, and established a reason in the matter of harm. Wherever harm is realized and occurs, there is prohibition, and what beautifies and vilifies any matter, or clarifies that this specific act involves harm or leads to harm is the Sharia, not reason. For example, speculation in Western civilization is commendable, but it is prohibited in Islam as it leads to harm to others. Thus, the determination of harm is not left to whims and minds; reason determines the interest, and if it is not realized, it says: there is harm. It may say: not engaging in usury harms me because I will lose money that I cannot earn, or it may say conversely, zakat harms me because I will lose money; this is not correct. Reason does not have the authority to determine harm and benefit.

The Wise Legislator has brought general rulings that prevent causing harm to others: ﴾And those who harm the believers, both men and women, without what they have earned, have certainly borne slander and a manifest sin﴿. This verse prohibits causing harm to believing men and women without what they have earned, and the term harm is a meaningful description, including psychological harm and physical harm, such as hitting, for example. Islam has prohibited killing a soul except by right: ﴾And do not kill the soul which Allah has forbidden, except by right﴿. And from Abu Huraira, the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) said, "Every Muslim's blood, wealth, and honor are sacred." And he (peace and blessings be upon him) said, "People are partners in three: water, pasture, and fire," which includes public ownership of natural resources that Muslims can benefit from. Thus, the legislator has brought general rulings with meaningful descriptions that reason can understand and apply: preventing harm, preventing killing, preventing aggression against blood, wealth, and honor. Consequently, the matter becomes the realization of the basis, or the application of the common reason, and the reason is the rationality of the text. The function of reason is to connect the ruling to its description and to attempt to understand the relationship between the ruling and its description, which is called the meaningful description, that is, to understand the aspect of the causality. Here, the cause is harm, and the aspect of the causality falls under general provisions defined by the Sharia, such as harming others or aggressing against them. Reason understands the rationality of the text and applies it to the cases.
