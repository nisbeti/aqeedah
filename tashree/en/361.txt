Objectives and goals of legislation and law-making must be clarified before answering the question: who has the right to legislate?
The state is an executive entity of a set of concepts, standards, and convictions held by a nation, meaning the values and legislative objectives of the sultan and governance. You will find a manifestation of this definition in the following analysis, with God's permission:
By studying Islamic legal references and positive legal systems, we find that the legislator sets goals intended behind the laws, and wisdoms that motivate them, and reasons(493) and objectives that drive them(494). In other words, the legislator will establish standards for laws and regulations, forming the framework within which all laws operate across various aspects of life. The principle is that laws should complement each other, supporting one another. For example, the penal system is based on the proper functioning of the economic system, social solidarity, and the responsibilities of the state in its guardianship role(495). Thus, laws—whether they are religious or positive—are established to serve the values that are intended to prevail in people's lives(496), (that is, the concepts and convictions upon which the sultan, or the state, is based), such as the value of justice, (it is said: justice is the foundation of kingship). These values themselves may differ according to the beliefs upon which societies are based. Here lies a crucial point: these values may be misleading, mere slogans without reality, when weighed against the ideologies governing society. You may find those values to be an illusory mirage with no impact in reality! This is similar to the value of "pluralism" that democracy advocates, but in reality, it has limited it to the dominance of secular political parties over other ideological parties, and restricted cultural pluralism to the individual aspect of society. The original principle should be that states are based on sound beliefs before considering the validity of their legislations and laws. Therefore, the establishment of a state on intellectually flawed beliefs such as secularism(497), or practically impossible ones like democracy(498), places the legislators in those states before a formidable obstacle.

(493) Regarding Islamic law, we mean by objectives and reasons here: the outcome that results from the law, that is, the wisdom resulting from its application, not the motivation for its legislation, that is, the goal of the legislator in enacting the law, nor the cause in its foundational sense in usul al-fiqh. The motivation for legislation and the wisdom are only sought if mentioned by the legislator. There are verses that clarify general goals and wisdoms that result from the application of the law, such as being a mercy, and the establishment of justice among people, and similar concepts. Jurists have derived objectives for the law as established in usul al-fiqh books. The discussion here is not about every legal ruling, but about the objectives of the law as a whole and the impact of those objectives on the laws regulating relationships in society.
(494) Among the legal sciences parallel to the science of the objectives of Islamic law is the philosophy of law, which has gained a prominent position in Western legal studies under the title: Legal philosophy, due to the nature of its topics that focus on studying the goals and wisdoms underlying legal systems and revealing the relationship between law and society. Generally, it revolves around: protecting "human rights," achieving justice and the common good; legal stability and legal security, and every goal has foundations upon which it is based and means leading to its achievement. See: The Objectives of Positive Law in Light of the Objectives of Islamic Law, by Dr. Alyan Bouzian, published in issue 150 of the Contemporary Muslim magazine.
