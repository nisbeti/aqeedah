In this context, Professor Dr. Mustafa al-Zarqa clarifies that "the Roman nation, when it lived a life isolated from the world, clinging to its own specific customs, had laws that were merely limited customs and regulations. However, when it surged eastward and emerged from its original homeland, and interacted with other elements, it was compelled to move beyond the confines of its narrow legal framework, which developed and expanded, yet remained time-bound, as they had two laws: one enjoyed by the original Romans, and the other for foreigners. This continued until the third century after Christ, as a result of their interaction with Eastern civilizations and religions that had engaged with the teachings of the Lord Christ (peace be upon him). At that time, their legislative thinking reached a high level of civil sophistication, and thereafter became the foundation for modern European legislations."(406)

And more importantly, in terms of its impact and relation to the topic of human rights, the roots of the concept of rights in Western thought have relied on the writings of theorists of the social contract such as Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, and Blackstone. "Western constitutional scholars emphasize that the idea of human rights is: the common origin from which philosophical schools in the eighteenth century drew... and it is the offspring of the 'natural rights school'... as presented and defended by the philosopher Locke in 1690. Locke's ideas were later articulated in legal terms by the English jurist Blackstone in the mid-eighteenth century"(407). They established the concept of rights on three foundations: contract, conflict, and utility. Additionally, the esteemed thinker Muhammad Hussein Abdullah - quoting Dr. Mufti and Dr. Wakil - added the following three foundations to what they called natural rights in Western thought: "First: The natural rights of individuals precede political existence, that is, they precede the establishment of the state, and therefore the state has the responsibility to respect individual rights and freedoms and to refrain from infringing upon them.

Secondly: The remedy for the contradiction between the authority of the state and the freedom of the individual is resolved in favor of individual freedom, because the purpose of the establishment of the state is to protect individual liberties.

Thirdly: Making individual freedom the foundation of the political existence of the state necessitates restricting the authority of the state and preventing it from abusing its power to limit the freedom of individuals.

Based on these foundations, the thinkers of capitalism sought to exclude divine laws that restrict freedoms and define human rights, and to replace them with natural law based on reason and reality. Thus, individual rights emerged in opposition to the state, which later became known as human rights during the American Revolution in 1776 and the French Revolution in 1789. The idea then gained an international dimension after World War II in the form of the United Nations, an organization established by the major powers to safeguard their interests(408).
