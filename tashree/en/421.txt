The meaning is that legitimate governance encompasses many rational treatments and administrative measures that achieve the objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia), even if there is no detailed text regarding them, as long as they do not contradict a text or a legal principle. The Sharia has distinguished between constants and means, thus providing ample space for administration and ijtihad (independent juristic reasoning) within the divine framework.
Practical Application: Examples Illustrating the Ruling and Its Corresponding Means
Below is a brief textual map listing key examples in the field of governance and politics, distinguishing between the established legal rulings and the means and methods left to ijtihad (independent reasoning):
The unity of the ummah under one rule: establishing a single caliph for the Muslims is a collective religious obligation (fard kifaya) supported by texts and consensus; it is not permissible to appoint more than one imam, nor to accept permanent fragmentation. The method: the caliph is chosen through allegiance (bay'ah), while the means of conducting the allegiance (direct election by the general public of Muslims, selection by the people of authority and resolution, consultation among candidates, etc.) are ijtihadi (jurisprudential) methods to achieve that ruling; the important thing is that the allegiance is given voluntarily and meets its legal conditions. Each community has the right to develop methods of selection, provided that consultation (shura) and justice are achieved. For example, using modern ballot boxes is an acceptable means if it ensures the free participation of the ummah in choosing its imam, without legal violations such as vote buying or appointing those who are not legally qualified.
Sources of Legislation and the Supremacy of Law: The legal ruling is that the Book of Allah and the Sunnah of His Messenger, consensus (ijma), and analogy (qiyas) are the sources of legislation; it is not permissible to adjudicate based on other opinions and desires. The Sharia obligates judges and rulers not to issue a ruling that contradicts a text or legal evidence. This includes the obligation to codify laws in such a way that they are derived from detailed evidences (and this is a juristic endeavor carried out by scholars within legislative councils). The method: establishing a constitution or fundamental law for the state that stipulates that the Sharia is the highest source of legislation (this is required by the Sharia to inform people of what they are obliged to adhere to). As for the details of the constitution and laws – such as the number of articles and their technical formulations – they are means to organize people's lives; they are formulated through ijtihad (juristic reasoning) that follows the rules of Sharia ijtihad to ensure justice. For example: the Sharia has mandated consultation (shura) but did not specify the term of membership for its council; thus, specifying it as four years, for instance, is an administrative means that may be stipulated in the constitution.
The structure of the state and the division of powers: The ruling in Islamic law is that the system of governance in Islam is a single unit (caliphate), which is a centralized presidential system where authority is concentrated in the hands of the caliph, along with his aides. Dual sovereignty (such as the separation of the head of state from the executive authority as in modern parliamentary systems) is not permissible because the caliph is the Commander of the Faithful and the primary responsible authority. However, it is legally permissible to delegate powers: the caliph appoints ministers (aides) and governors over the provinces, judges, etc. This delegation is permissible based on the texts of the Prophet's (peace and blessings be upon him) appointing workers and judges during his lifetime. The means: the form of the administrative division of the state (provinces, governorates, municipalities) and the method of managing each level is an organizational means.
