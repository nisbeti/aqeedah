The system of governance and the unity of the caliphate: The Sharia has mandated that Muslims must have one leadership and one caliph upon whom they unite, and it has prohibited the multiplicity of states among them (as he (peace and blessings be upon him) said: "If two caliphs are pledged allegiance to, then kill the latter of the two"). This is a definitive legal ruling that outlines the form of the Islamic state as a single, unified caliphate. However, the method of selecting the caliph has been left by the Sharia to the ijtihad (juridical reasoning) of the community, as long as it is within the framework of Sharia principles: such as being chosen through the people of resolution and contract, or through a general vote, or other means that are legally valid to achieve the pledge of allegiance. The important thing is to fulfill the legal conditions in the elected individual (such as justice, knowledge, and competence), and to achieve general consent through the pledge of allegiance. As for the mechanisms and procedures (elections, consultative councils, nomination methods), they are organizational ijtihadi means that differ according to the circumstances of time. Any means adopted should achieve the legal purpose (the proper establishment of the pledge of allegiance) without contradicting a fixed ruling.

Legislative sovereignty (source of laws): Islam has established that sovereignty belongs to the Sharia; meaning that binding rulings must be based on a legal evidence. It is not permissible to enact a law that obliges people in contradiction to a Book, a Sunnah, or a consensus. This is a fundamental legal ruling that prevents, for example, the legislation of the permissibility of alcohol or usury or any prohibited matter - even if the assembly of the nation agrees on it - it is invalid. Conversely, organizing procedures and administrative laws (executive regulations, details of the internal system of institutions, traffic laws, etc.) is permissible within the realm of what is allowed, as the administrative apparatus is a method of management and not a ruling, as it is a means of carrying out actions, and a method among its methods, thus it does not require specific evidence for each particular administrative form, but rather the general evidence on the principle of the action suffices. Therefore, its detailed organization (its modalities) is left to reason as long as it does not conflict with a text. Likewise, the structuring of departments and interests (from circles, administrations, branches, and sections) is established according to need, (such as education, health, agriculture, labor, roads, and others with the details of these circles and their sections), and it is established for the purpose of advancing the affairs of the state, and it is permissible for them to differ from era to era, and from country to country, as these are administrative means aimed at facilitating life and achieving the interests of people without contradicting a legal principle. If the state needs a law to regulate a certain matter, it must first seek a legal ruling on the issue through the process of legal ijtihad; if found, it is followed, and if it pertains to matters that are of the nature of the previously mentioned administrative systems, such as the postal system or records and modern means or the dīwān (administrative office), which the legislator did not specify, the state legislates in these systems and administrations as long as it does not contradict the Sharia. For example, the legal principle requires the giving of zakat, without specifying the modalities of implementation, whether the worker goes riding or walking? Should he hire assistants? Should they keep records? Should they collect the funds in one place? Should means such as "houses for grains" or the like be used? Should there be a dīwān for the soldiers and another for the revenue, and so on? Therefore, administrative methods can be taken from any system unless there is a specific text prohibiting a certain administrative method. Thus, the choice of procedural means (record systems, collection methods, storage arrangements, and similar) is left to reason and experience because they are "methods" not "rulings". These human legislations are means that are not called "legal rulings", but rather "administrative systems" binding to the extent that they are based on a general legal principle (such as alleviating hardship and achieving public benefit).
