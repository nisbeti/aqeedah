The characteristics of Islamic law (sharia) here focus on studying the concept from a legislative perspective, the subject of rights from a cognitive (epistemological) standpoint, where rights represent the opposite of falsehood, and from an existential (ontological) perspective, which indicates the establishment of rights and how they are realized and brought into existence in reality, and from a value-based perspective, as rights are a value in themselves, akin to the values of goodness and justice. Rights are the realized and established, meaning the obligatory in the moral sense. In the language of the usuliyyin (jurisprudents), rights revolve around obligation and the correct action that aligns with the intent of the legislator. The legislator is the creator of rights; thus, if a Muslim acts correctly according to the legislator's intent and this action is realized in reality in a specific normative manner, it is said to be a right; for it is realized in every aspect and for its value-laden content. An incorrect action or one that contradicts the commands and prohibitions is not called a right. In the language of jurists, the term "right" is used to denote multiple meanings, such as wages, obligations arising from contracts, which define the rights of the contracting parties, the right to use public facilities, and others(314). The concept of rights in Islam is a comprehensive and self-standing concept, through which Islam has liberated humanity from the depths of oppression, falsehood, and corruption, demolishing them all with the concept of rights and entitlements. Islam has also dismantled the misuse of the concept of rights in other systems.
