The Sixth Column: The Concept of Rights, Human Rights between Islamic and Western Political Thought:
When we addressed the concept of rights in our discussion on the characteristics of Islamic law (sharia), we focused on studying the concept from a legislative perspective, the subject of rights from a cognitive (epistemological) standpoint, where rights represent the opposite of falsehood, and from an existential (ontological) perspective, which indicates the establishment of rights and how they are realized and manifested in reality, and from a value-based perspective, as rights are a value in themselves, akin to the values of goodness and justice. Rights are the established and fixed reality, meaning the obligatory in the ethical sense. In this chapter, we focus on the impact of the concept of rights on the effectiveness of applying Islamic law, its effect on shaping the mentality of individuals and society, and the influence of the concept of rights on behavior. The same can be said about responsibilities and accountability, which we discussed earlier. The balance in rights, obligations, and duties cannot be established, nor can the rights of individuals and the interests of society be preserved unless they are formulated through legislative rules that transform into societal customs or legal reference rules for the establishment of rights, which the community absorbs as a practical culture. At that point, legislation transforms into something akin to literature in the nation, reflecting the social and economic reality of the community and accurately expressing its development and reform. Therefore, the concept of rights is a crucial cornerstone in Islamic legislation for building individuals committed to fulfilling and preserving rights and performing duties as a means of upholding rights, and for building a society with legislative references that eliminate injustice and restore rights to their rightful owners.(404)
And if Western legislation has attempted to establish a theory of "human rights" in contemporary political thought, the gap is vast, the chasm deep, and the difference fundamental between the Islamic presentation of the principle of rights in its ideal vision and practical application, and the Western presentation. Professor Wisam Fouad, editor of the page: Islam and Contemporary Issues, on the Islam Online website, harshly criticized, with a solid argument, in his article "Human Rights, a Vast Gap Between the Western and Islamic Presentations," the attempts of some who are concerned about Islam, whom he referred to as apologetic thinkers, to place "the Universal Declaration of Human Rights in Islam"(405) in the same framework as the Universal Declaration issued by the United Nations, incorporating the same concepts and legal images, while asserting that Islam contains "the same rights," apologizing to the world for the lack of clarity in Islamic texts on this matter, or apologizing for the lack of interest of Islamic civilization in it until the time of its declaration by the United Nations! Their document was "deformed," molded in the complete framework of dependence on Western concepts.
As for the concept of human rights in its Western edition, it is difficult to trace its deep roots in Western thought. The ancient Greek civilization did not coin the concept of rights, and the notion of rights began to make its way into usage in Europe during the Middle Ages, with the priest Thomas Aquinas using it in discussing the concept of property rights, which he addressed as a right that entails social responsibility. This idea is well-known in the Islamic legislative context, represented in the concept of God's vicegerency (khilafa) over humans in wealth, so that they may spend it in His way on those in need.
