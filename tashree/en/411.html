Appendix 3: A Question About Politics: Did the Legislator Leave the Determination of Politics to Reason?

This appendix complements the research: The Limits of Islamic Legislation; Where Does Obligation Begin and Where Does It End? What is the Role of Reason and What is the Role of the Law?

As a prelude to this new chapter, we pose the problematic question: Did the Legislator leave the field of "politics" (the care of the affairs of the nation) empty for human reason to fill it with worldly interests as it wishes? Or did the Sharia - as it defined the door of harm and others - also elaborate on political Sharia and not leave legislation in it to pure reason, except within the limits permitted by the law? There is a common misconception among some that portrays "politics" as an area isolated from religion, subject to the logic of pure human interest under the slogan "You know best about your worldly affairs," in contrast to those who adopt the approach of "the law of interest" in governance, believing that they can undermine legal rulings to achieve immediate political goals.

We will demonstrate in this chapter that Islamic law has brought forth a well-structured political system that is no less detailed than other aspects of religion, and that it did not leave the affairs of governance and the state to human whims without regulation. At the same time, we will clarify the legitimate scope of reason in politics: that is, the aspects of administration and implementation left to ijtihad (independent juristic reasoning) to apply rulings to a changing reality, within the legal frameworks. This will be achieved through the following axes:

Clarifying political concepts in the Islamic perspective: (politics, governance, authority, state, obedience, consultation...).

The foundational rules for the theory of governance in Islam: clarifying the limits of political obligation, and the meaning of the hadith "You know best about your worldly affairs" in its context, and the principle of "no legislative vacuum" and the comprehensiveness of Sharia for all aspects of life.

The reference of revelation in state systems: presenting the philosophy of Islam regarding caliphate and governance, and refuting the idea of "free rational politics" historically and intellectually.

Critique of the "law of interest" approach in politics: as presented by some contemporary advocates of liberal Islamic thought; and highlighting the dangers of making human whims a legislator in matters of governance.

Distinguishing between legal political rulings and executive means: clarifying what is established by text (such as caliphate, consultation, unity of the nation…) and what is left to ijtihad in terms of methods and administrative means within the constraints.

An applied section: practical examples in decision-making, treaties, foreign policy, state management… with classification of each: which is governed by divine legislation and which falls under human means provided it does not contradict the law.

Conclusion of the chapter: summarizing the main results and providing a clear textual map that distinguishes what is a binding legal ruling and what is a flexible administrative means.

Through this dialectical and inferential methodology, we will prove that the Sharia did not leave politics to reason independently, but it also did not neglect the role of reason in implementing rulings and achieving interests within its framework. It is a delicate equation that achieves the centrality of the reference of revelation while allowing for disciplined human creativity in managing the affairs of the state.
