The disparity of minds in perceiving truths and legislations:
There is no doubt that people differ in their views on solutions to problems and the codification of those solutions. If you were to ask people about their opinion on the optimal solution for the relationship between a man and a woman, you would find a variety of answers shaped by the influence of cultures, customs, environments, desires, tastes, and differences in intellectual capacities, specializations, and innate inclinations. It is evident that the inclination towards baser instincts and their application in legislation can lead to a bestiality that legitimizes adultery or sexual deviance, for the reality of base inclinations is that they issue judgments of praise only on what aligns with them and of blame only on what contradicts them.
What Zayd sees as good, 'Ubayd sees as contrary to his desires, and what 'Amr believes to be beneficial may later reveal to him that harm lies in one of its shadowy corners. While drinking alcohol is deemed reprehensible and forbidden in Muslim societies, it is seen as good and acceptable in Western societies. Thus, you observe the disparity among individuals as well as among societies, and even among thinkers and legal scholars, which makes the laws issued by humans characterized by a deficiency in vision, and in navigating the realm of doubt—whether they achieve the public interest (maslaha) or bring about harm, whether they serve the interests of one group at the expense of another. You also find that they do not possess comprehensiveness, as they fail to cover all aspects of the issue being discussed, or to achieve reassurance and justice for all those affected among the subjects, which forces legislators to constantly review constitutions and issue amendments to them.
The immediate perspectives on interests corrupt the correctness of legislations, for a person may judge things to be good today and then judge them to be bad tomorrow, leading to a discrepancy in the judgment of a single individual on the same matter, and thus it is not a stable judgment, resulting in errors in judgment(536).
Thus, the capacities of minds vary in strength and weakness, and in assessing harm and benefit, and understandings among people differ. What one person sees as good, another sees as bad, and the appreciation of minds for the means that achieve interests varies. Minds also differ in understanding the harmony of laws with the fabric of public law in the state and their realization of the system of values and legislative objectives, among other factors whose existence cannot be denied.
Consequently, it is impossible for humans, or most of them, to agree on a single legislative law, let alone a set of laws that govern all aspects of their lives. It is impossible for the entire populace to issue a judgment on a single issue, let alone to reach a consensus on a constitution to govern by. Here, the matter of enacting laws is left to the state, which in turn delegates the matter of enacting laws to legal scholars. Thus, legislation in positive systems is, in reality, the product of the minds of a few lawyers, judges, and legal scholars, and then members of parliament vote on the laws. Here, in the matter of voting on an economic issue, for example, the vote of someone with the highest academic qualifications in economics among the members of parliament is equal to the vote of a parliament member who has received no education in economics, which makes the enacted law a result of the opinion of heads counted as sheep are counted, not a product of deliberation.

(536) See: Ghayat al-Maram – by al-Amidi, p. 235. Al-Amidi says: "For indeed, a thing may be judged by the mind of a person to be good because it aligns with his purpose, or because it contains his interest, or prevents his harm, or because it conforms to his habit, and the habit of his people, whether by custom or by law. And the mind of another may judge it to be bad because it contradicts what aligns with his purpose." Ghayat al-Maram, p. 235. Islam and the Foundations of Governance by Dr. Mahmoud al-Khalidi.
