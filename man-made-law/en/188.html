For instance, the advocacy of "folk literature" in colloquial dialects and the push to separate literary expression from classical Arabic emerged from within this reformist school—heavily influenced by Orientalist agendas. Similarly, Westernized poetic and literary tendencies—such as those seen in the Apollo group and the Dīwān school—were all shaped by the liberal thought planted by ʿAbduh’s movement in early 20th-century Egypt. Anwar al-Jundī (1917–2002), another influential thinker in modern Islamic discourse, dedicated dozens of books and articles to refuting the claims of Westernized reformists. In "Aʿlām wa Mawāqif" ("Figures and Stances") and other works, he dissected the biographies of al-Afghānī and ʿAbduh, revealing the roles of Freemasonry and Orientalism in undermining the Ottoman Caliphate and promoting narrow nationalist ideologies. He implied that colonialism could never have succeeded without the cooperation—intentional or not—of certain Eastern intellectuals. Al-Jundī viewed ʿAbduh as the “teacher” of an entire generation of writers who gradually distanced themselves from true Islam. He named Shiblī Shumayyil, Farah Anṭūn, Salāmah Mūsā, and Ṭāhā Ḥusayn as members of the Arab rationalist school. These figures, in al-Jundī’s estimation, were either overt atheists or extreme secularists.

Although ʿAbduh himself remained committed to core Islamic beliefs, his unregulated opening of the door to rational interpretation emboldened others to go beyond what he would have accepted. This, according to al-Jundī, was the real danger. Similarly, Abū al-Ḥasan al-Nadwī (1913–1999) of India adopted a firm stance. In his celebrated book "Mādhā Khasira al-ʿĀlam bi Inḥiṭāṭ al-Muslimīn?" ("What Did the World Lose by the Decline of the Muslims?"), he criticized the approach of certain so-called revivalists who imitated the West. He mentioned the modernist trend in Egypt and India, referencing figures like Sayyid Aḥmad Khān (the Indian counterpart to ʿAbduh), noting how his followers became enamored with the West and led reforms down perilous paths. Nadwī emphasized the need to distinguish between commendable change and blameworthy innovation, and argued that many reforms promoted by these so-called reformers were in fact destructive to the spiritual core of Islam, even if they appeared progressive on the surface.

In this, he echoed Dr. Ḥusayn’s critique. Nadwī also praised the efforts of God-fearing scholars who resisted these trends and revived sincere faith without excess or neglect—such as Shaykh Maḥmūd Ḥasan and Ḥusayn Aḥmad al-Madanī in India, who led liberation movements without compromising their religious principles; or ʿAbd al-Ḥamīd ibn Bādīs and al-Bashīr al-Ibrāhīmī in Algeria, who resisted colonialism through cultural revival while firmly upholding doctrinal orthodoxy. A clear contrast is drawn between a conservative reformist path—represented by these scholars—and the rationalist reformism of al-Afghānī and ʿAbduh in the Arab East. Nadwī favored the former, as it preserved the integrity of revelation while wisely engaging in renewal. Finally, Sayyid Quṭb (1906–1966) offered his own critique of the rationalist current—especially significant given his own journey from liberalism to Islamic revival. In his famous work "Maʿālim fī al-Ṭarīq" ("Milestones"), Quṭb diagnosed a “psychological defeat” suffered by some Muslim intellectuals in the face of Western civilization. This defeat drove them to contort Islam to conform to materialist modernity, which he regarded as deeply dangerous.
