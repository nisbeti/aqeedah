The public interest (maslaha) that is not explicitly mentioned: preventing economic harm. (This connection between the general principle and the description of the resulting public interest, namely their statement: preventing economic harm, is a problem we believe to be erroneous, and we will address it later, God willing).

The connection to revelation: a general inference from various texts regarding usury and the preservation of wealth.

Fourth: Precautions against the transition to human legislation

The Malikis have established three essential conditions for the acceptance of public interest (maslaha mursal):

The first condition: adherence to a general principle, so public interest is only accepted if it falls under a fixed legal objective established through induction. Al-Izz ibn Abd al-Salam (d. 660 AH) states: "Any public interest that is not supported by the foundations of Sharia is invalid, because the legislator does not neglect a recognized public interest." (Qawaid al-Ahkam, vol. 1, p. 8).

The second condition: not contradicting specific texts

If there is a specific evidence (text or consensus, any detailed evidence) that contradicts the public interest, the texts take precedence. It is reported from Imam Malik that he said: "No one should express an opinion when there is a text." (Tartib al-Madarik by Qadi Iyad, vol. 1, p. 150). The public interest according to Malik can never be in a case where there is a text. If there is a text or consensus, the public interest is what the text has determined, not what people perceive.

The third condition: that the public interest is general and collective, not based on the whims of an individual or a group: meaning that its consideration results in benefit for the community or the removal of harm from it, not the fulfillment of a desire for a ruler or a narrow benefit for a group at the expense of another. Therefore, some refer to it as "the public interest" to distinguish it. They exemplified this by mandating a certain trade in a time when conditions deteriorate, such as the obligation of the ruler to require merchants to set prices during extreme inflation to protect the public, despite the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) prohibiting price-fixing in normal circumstances (because it is unjust to merchants). Here, a public interest is considered because it is a general necessity to remove a greater injustice, and it is justified by the principle of preventing general harm. Thus, several scholars have determined that one of the criteria for working with public interest is "that the interest should not be specific to what the Sharia has generally prohibited." For example, organizing traffic by establishing modern signals and rules is considered a valid public interest; because it achieves the preservation of lives and wealth (which is one of the objectives of Sharia), and does not contradict any text, but rather falls under the general principle of "no harm and no harassment." However, legislating a usurious system under the pretext of economic prosperity is invalid, as it is a fictitious interest that contradicts the definitive texts prohibiting usury. (See the chapter: There is no legislative vacuum: ﴾Today I have perfected for you your religion﴿, and we have elaborated on it in the rulings regarding traffic organization and that it is not among those sent from the evidence.)

Fifth: Testimonies of the Usul scholars in validating this methodology

Imam al-Baji (d. 474 AH):

"The mursal (public interest) is what is inferred based on the intended purpose of the legislator in bringing about benefits and preventing harms, without being restricted to a specific principle." (Ihkam al-Fusul, p. 470).
