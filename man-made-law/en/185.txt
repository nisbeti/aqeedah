And Ismail (peace be upon him) followed a strict rational methodology influenced by Orientalism. Even al-Aqqad – despite maintaining a degree of religiosity – presented a modern rational Islamic thought that contradicts many of the certainties of the heritage.
We can say that the reformist rational current branched out after Rashid Rida into two wings: a relatively moderate conservative wing represented by Azharite figures who combine authenticity and facilitation (such as Sheikh Mahmoud Shaltut, who followed the Manar methodology but with less expansion, permitting some new transactions without neglecting the definitive texts). And a radical liberal wing represented by writers and thinkers who carried Abduh's ideas to their utmost extent, influenced by the Western environment. Among these in the latter wing is Ali Abdul Raziq, who published in 1925 the book "Islam and the Foundations of Governance," denying that Islam established a caliphate system and claiming that it was merely a historical experiment that could be dispensed with. This was a complete separation between religion and politics based on a purely Western rational logic. Also, as we mentioned, Taha Hussein, in his book "On Pre-Islamic Poetry" (1926), questioned some historical narratives of the Qur'an under the pretext of modern scientific methodology, which caused a shock in al-Azhar at that time. These represent a clear modernist/secular current more than Islamic rationality, but they undoubtedly emerged from the mantle of "using reason freely to understand the text" that Abduh established.
Alongside Egypt, reform movements in the Islamic world were influenced by this thought. In India, for example, Syed Ahmad Khan (1817–1898) emerged, influenced by al-Afghani and Abduh, calling for a reinterpretation of Islamic legislation compatible with British civilization, even denying miracles and esoteric interpretations just as Abduh did in many matters. His ideas spread through "Aligarh University." In Turkey, some pioneers of the Tanzimat movement and then the Unionists were influenced by the same rational inclination, leading to the adoption of secularism by Atatürk (which was an extreme culmination of that thought). Thus, Egypt is not the only stage; the contagion – if the term is appropriate – extended to the entire Islamic world.
Contemporary currents emerging from the mantle of rationality
In recent decades, we have witnessed the emergence of new currents that appear distinct but are essentially an extension of the reformist rational methodology. Among the most prominent of these currents are: the modern maqasid (objectives of the Sharia) current, facilitative jurisprudence (jurisprudence of minorities and balances), and what is called liberal Islam in academic circles.
The modern maqasid current: It is led by scholars and thinkers such as Dr. Yusuf al-Qaradawi, Dr. Ahmed al-Raisouni, and others. They focus on applying the objectives of the Sharia in every ijtihad (independent legal reasoning) under the pretext of highlighting the tolerance of Islam, but some of their fatwas (legal opinions) have sparked controversy for exceeding what conservatives considered consensus. For example, al-Qaradawi issued a fatwa permitting bank interest for necessity in some Western countries, and permitting work in usurious banks, stating, "It may enter into the composition of our economic system… it has become a widespread affliction… if one cannot refrain from it… there is no harm in accepting this work(50)"
