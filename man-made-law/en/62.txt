As indicated by the quotation we mentioned earlier(22), the Malikis establish that the public interest (maslaha mursala) is not an invented interest, but rather falls under the consideration of the Sharia through the extrapolation of its general texts.
Professor Ahmad al-Shanqiti (in his book: The Appropriate Description of the Law of Judgment) confirms that:
Describing public interest (maslaha) as being based on general principles does not negate its consideration in Islamic law, but rather distinguishes it from analogy (qiyas). Analogy has a specific basis that supports the particular instance of public interest, while the general principles have non-specific bases that support the category of public interest... Therefore, it is necessary for the jurist (mujtahid) to establish that the category of this public interest is recognized by the texts of the Sharia, otherwise it would be legislation based on personal whims." (Chapter Three: On Clarifying the Evidence of the School of Imam Malik). The scholars of the school have determined that Malik only considered public interest when the recognition of the legislator (Shari) for it was evident through its relation to his general principles and rules, and when the texts of the Sharia supported it in general, even if there was no specific evidence supporting it. This is an important condition; not everything that reason perceives as beneficial is considered a legal public interest, but rather only that which is of the same kind as what Allah has legislated similar to it.
Imam al-Shatibi (d. 790 AH) comments on this mechanism: "Public interests (masalih mursala) are only valid if they are aligned with a general principle, and this is determined through a complete induction of the evidences of the Sharia. What conforms to its generality is considered valid, and what contradicts it is rejected." (al-Muwafaqat, vol. 2, p. 286).
Third: The method of general induction (istiqra al-kulli): the bridge between public interest (maslaha mursala) and revelation.
The Malikis consider the general induction of the legal evidences to be the primary mechanism for linking public interest (maslaha mursala) with revelation. This methodology is based on:
Gathering the texts related to a specific chapter.
Deriving the general objective that these texts indicate.
Applying this objective to a new incident that has no "text," meaning there are no detailed evidences regarding it.
An applied example: the prohibition of trading in paper currencies if it leads to usury (riba) (a contemporary issue).
There is no direct text regarding "currency notes," but through comprehensive induction:
The texts prohibit usury (riba) in gold and silver: ﴾And Allah has permitted trade and forbidden usury﴿.
The general rule: "Everything that is a price in the market is treated like gold and silver in the rulings of usury (riba)."

(22) This is because the public interest (maslaha) in the unqualified is "not a public interest that is left unaddressed, but rather a public interest that the Sharia has considered through a series of texts and a collection of evidence. The description of the writers of usul (principles of jurisprudence) regarding the appropriate public interest in the unqualified is merely a term intended to distinguish between unqualified reasoning and analogy (qiyas), as analogy has a specific origin that attests to the particular public interest, whereas in unqualified reasoning there are no specific origins, meaning they do not attest to the particular public interest, even if they do attest to its type by consideration. Therefore, it is necessary for the jurist (mujtahid) who claims that there is a public interest that necessitates giving a particular ruling to the incident to prove that this public interest is of a type that the legislator (shari) has considered through legal texts, and to present the texts; otherwise, it is legislation based on opinion, which is a statement based on desire, and it is an innovation in religion.
