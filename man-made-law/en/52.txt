By human reason, while minds are varied, their perceptions are limited, and their estimations of interests are not infallible, if legislation were left to the standard of "what we see as just" or "what we consider beneficial," then God's legislation would be subject to the whims and conjectures of people, and it would be as if one were to argue against God with the pretext of limited intellect.
The legal ruling is known through text, not through conjecture, and true justice is what God has deemed just, not what is justified by the limited perceptions of people. How many legal rulings have their wisdom hidden from those who lack knowledge of them, such as the inheritance of women, the zakat threshold, and the limits of retribution? Yet, in the balance of the Sharia, they achieve the highest degrees of justice and solidarity. What Allah commanded the Children of Israel to do, to kill themselves, or what He commanded Ibrahim to do, to sacrifice his son, are but two examples that clarify that justice is not measured by reason, but by revelation.
However, Ibn al-Qayyim (may Allah have mercy on him) does not mean that pure reason determines justice or benefit. Rather, he asserts that justice is what the legislator has deemed just, and benefit is what the legislator has called benefit, making this the standard. In other places in I'lam al-Muwaqqi'in, he states: "The intellect does not independently know benefits and harms except in a general sense, while the details can only be understood through the Sharia" [I'lam al-Muwaqqi'in, vol. 1, p. 69], and he also says: "The Sharia does not come with what the intellect rejects, but rather with what the intellect is perplexed by" [Miftah Dar al-Sa'ada, vol. 2, p. 53]. He distinguishes between the Sharia being a standard for benefit and leaving the ruling to reason from the outset.
Imam al-Shatibi criticizes in al-Muwafaqat any understanding that makes benefit known through independent reason, stating: "It is not permissible for benefit to be purely rational, because reason does not encompass the intentions of the legislator, and the considered benefit is that which is indicated by the Sharia" [al-Muwafaqat, vol. 2, pp. 9–10]. Imam al-Qarafi also, in Nafais al-Usool, distinguishes between legal benefit and imagined benefit, saying: "Any rational benefit that is not supported by evidence from the Sharia is not considered" [Nafais al-Usool, manuscript at the Egyptian National Library].
Organizing the acquisition of benefits and organizing the prevention of harms:
Benefit in Islamic jurisprudence and principles is not understood as "everything that directly benefits a person," but is viewed as: "a comprehensive system that encompasses legitimate benefits and regulates them, considers outcomes, and prevents conflicts between individual and public benefits. Thus, we find that benefit is not limited to immediate gain, but includes:
Balancing interests (for example: prioritizing public interest over private, or preventing a greater harm over acquiring a lesser benefit).
Outcome and permanence: benefit is assessed by its consequences, not just by its immediate result.
Guiding values: not every benefit is a benefit unless it is regulated by the values of the Sharia, such as justice, mercy, and human dignity.
