It is difficult to imagine that individuals of their caliber would be unaware of the Masonic lodges’ deep associations with colonial powers and their opposition to the Ottoman Caliphate【aljazeera.net】. Contemporaries such as Shaykh Yūsuf al-Nabahānī, Dr. Muhammad Muhammad Ḥusayn, and others severely criticized them for this. Dr. Ghāzī al-Tūbah, in his study of Muhammad ʿAbduh, considered this Masonic affiliation one of the three great sins of the imam—alongside his cooperation with the colonial authorities and his excessive rational reinterpretation of matters of the unseen【aljazeera.net】.
As for the Pan-Islamic project (al-Jāmiʿah al-Islāmiyyah), originally championed by al-Afghani and later embraced by Sultan ʿAbd al-Ḥamīd II, it was conceived as a call to unite Muslims under a single political and religious banner to resist colonial fragmentation. Al-Afghani traveled extensively, calling Muslims—both Sunnis and Shiʿis—to forget their differences and stand as one. His journal al-ʿUrwah al-Wuthqā served as a platform to spread awareness of Islamic unity and resistance to occupation. Theoretically, this Islamic unity was framed as a religious bond—reviving the idea of a single ummah in which “there is no superiority for an Arab over a non-Arab except through piety.” But in practice, reformers also employed this project within a nationalist political context. For example, al-Afghani allied with Egyptian nationalists against the Khedive and later cooperated with the Ottoman Sultan—despite earlier criticisms—when he believed Muslim interests demanded it. After the fall of the Ottoman Caliphate, Rashīd Riḍā sought to reframe the Pan-Islamic idea as a political union led by the Arabs. He proposed, for instance, that the King of Egypt be made Caliph or that the Arab countries unite into a single state. However, this vision ultimately failed due to the reality of political fragmentation and colonial occupation.
The most concerning aspect of this trajectory was al-Afghani’s call for the multiplicity of governments in the Muslim world instead of the establishment of a single Islamic state uniting Muslims. How could he advocate dividing Muslims into mini-states while they were still under a unified Islamic Caliphate? This idea was premature and harmonized with British colonial plans that sought to dismantle and fragment the Islamic state. One critical point worth reflecting upon is that the Pan-Islamic bond gradually lost its spiritual dimension and became more of a political dream. It shifted from being a bond of religious allegiance to the Caliphate into a kind of political solidarity among nations and peoples bound only by common interest against external enemies. This transformation was a natural result of the reformers’ emphasis on civil and national revival as the priority. For them, religion became more of a civilizational motivator than a transcendent allegiance that transcended ethnicity and geography. Furthermore, the behavior of some of al-Afghani’s and ʿAbduh’s disciples contributed to this shift. For instance, Saʿd Zaghlūl, a student of ʿAbduh, led the 1919 nationalist revolution in Egypt, which had a liberal, not Islamic, character. He famously raised the slogan: “Religion is for God, and the nation is for all.”
