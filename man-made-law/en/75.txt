This means that the compilation of the Qur'an was rooted in the indication of a definitive text represented in the commitment to preserve the Qur'an, supported by other evidence such as the Prophet's (peace and blessings be upon him) command to write the revelation, based on an established legal principle (the means have the same ruling as the objectives, and what is necessary for the fulfillment of an obligation is itself obligatory, and there is no harm nor reciprocation of harm).
Thirdly: It is reported in Sahih al-Bukhari that Abu Bakr hesitated but then became convinced after Umar insisted, saying: "How can we do something that the Messenger of Allah did not do?" Umar said: "It is, by Allah, good." Their opinion settled because the basis for compilation existed during the time of the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) when the Qur'an was written in a scattered manner. It is known that the absence of action during the time of the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) does not indicate prohibition or innovation if there is something in the Sharia that indicates it, and the criterion is not whether the act is new, but rather that its lack of a legal impediment and its establishment on a legal basis makes it acceptable, as we will demonstrate shortly. As for innovation, its definition is: every act that contradicts the Sharia, meaning it contradicts the legal ruling when a method or manner for performing that legal ruling was established, and it is done in a manner that contradicts it. This does not apply to this matter, neither closely nor remotely, as the Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him) used to write what was revealed to him directly and place the new verse in its position in such-and-such surah after such-and-such verse, and this is a principle upon which the compilation is built.
Fourthly: The Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) said: "There is no harm nor reciprocation of harm" (narrated by al-Hakim). This is a clear legal principle indicating the obligation to remove harm. The companions' fear of the loss of the Qur'an due to the death of the memorizers is a certain harm that must be averted. Thus, the compilation of the Qur'an was in compliance with the command of the legislator to remove harm, and not based on a mere rational opinion of public interest (maslaha).
The practical Sunnah during the time of the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him): the existence of writing and documentation by his command: the evidence: Zaid ibn Thabit (may Allah be pleased with him) said: "We were with the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) compiling the Qur'an from pieces of parchment" (narrated by al-Hakim and authenticated by al-Dhahabi).
The inference: The Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) commanded the writing of the Qur'an and its collection in a scattered manner during his lifetime. Zaid ibn Thabit and a group of companions had a fixed role as scribes of revelation, and they would document the verses immediately upon their descent, by direct command from the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him), and he would instruct them to arrange them. This arrangement collects verses that were revealed later into their places in their surahs after verses that were revealed earlier, until the surah is complete. This is a form of preserving the noble Qur'an, and what Abu Bakr (may Allah be pleased with him) did is an extension of this very act.
The presentation of the Qur'an by Gabriel to the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) at the end of Ramadan: a legal proof of the arrangement, documentation, and compilation.
The evidence: The Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) said in the hadith of Ibn Abbas: "Gabriel would meet me every year and review the Qur'an with me, and I met him in the year I was taken (to Allah) twice." (narrated by al-Bukhari).
The inference: The review of the Qur'an by Gabriel with the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) in the last year twice confirms the completeness of the compilation and arrangement of the Qur'an, and that there was a fixed form of the Qur'an completed before his passing (peace and blessings be upon him), and all that remained was to gather the sheets in which it was spread, and Gabriel's review indicates the care to ensure that nothing from the Qur'an is lost, and that every verse is placed in its proper position in the surah to which it belongs, and this is very similar to what Abu Bakr, Umar, and Uthman did.
