However, in contemporary usage by some, it has degenerated into a pretext for diluting legal rulings under the guise of “accommodating the complexities of the modern age.”

How often do we hear the claim: “The fatwas of early scholars are no longer suitable for our current reality”?

This misunderstanding stems from a fundamentally flawed perspective regarding how Islam approached unprecedented occurrences (nawāzil) and human problems. Islam did not view these as purely economic or temporal challenges, nor did it treat life’s problems as ever-changing. Instead, Islam treated them as issues rooted in human nature—and since the essence of the human being is fixed, the solutions governing his physiological needs, instinctual drives, and the resulting societal issues are also permanent and unchanging. We have elaborated this in detail in our book The Miracle of Islamic Legislation. However, in the context of contemporary Fiqh al-Wāqiʿ, Fiqh al-Aqalliyyāt (minority jurisprudence), and Fiqh al-Muwāzanāt, many of the applications are mere justifications for abandoning established rulings—such as the rulings on hijāb, jihad, and the prohibition of interest (ribā), among others.

Fiqh al-Wāqiʿ and the Danger of Being Submerged in Reality The methodology of ijtihād (legal reasoning) consists of three essential stages, the first of which is: accurate understanding of the reality upon which a legal ruling is to be applied. This aspect of ijtihād is not based on transmitted texts, but rather on empirical inquiry. For instance, when Māʿiz confessed to the Prophet ﷺ that he had committed adultery, the Prophet ﷺ continued to probe: “Perhaps you only kissed her?” “Perhaps you merely touched her?” He continued questioning him until the exact nature of the act became clear, thereby enabling the proper application of the relevant Sharʿī ruling.

The act of adultery warranted the ḥadd of stoning, but had it only been touching or kissing, the ruling would have been entirely different. Thus, the precise nature of the reality determines the appropriate legal response.

This is why the prayer for a solar eclipse differs from the funeral prayer. In the former, failure to perform two bowings (rukūʿ) invalidates the prayer; in the latter, adding a rukūʿ deliberately invalidates it. The Prophet ﷺ said: “Whoever among you sees an evil, let him change it with his hand; and if he is unable, then with his tongue…” Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. While the word “evil” (munkar) in this hadith is general, the ruling for correcting the evil of a ruler differs from that of others: If the ruler’s wrongdoing does not reach the level of open disbelief, one is not permitted to change it by force. Thus, the identity of the doer impacts the legal mechanism for redress.
