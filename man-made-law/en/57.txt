The invalidation of the argumentation based on verses and hadiths:
The cited verses indicate the goal (the result after application), not the cause (the preceding reason (motive) for legislation).
The essential difference between the cause and the goal:
The goal is achieved after application.
The cause precedes legislation and is inherent to it.
Example:
﴾وَمَا أَرْسَلْنَاكَ إِلَّا رَحْمَةً﴿ indicates mercy as a goal, not as a cause.
The hadith "There should be no harm" indicates the negation of harm, not the affirmation of benefit as a cause.
There is no true causal formulation in these texts (such as: "in order to," "for the sake of," "because of," etc.).
The invalidation of the claim of consensus:
The valid consensus is only the consensus of the Companions.
The claim of consensus on "wisdom" does not imply that it is a "cause" in its legal sense.
The distinction between the result and the cause:
Bringing about benefits and averting harms is a result of applying the Sharia, not a motive or cause for legislation.
The legal cause is the meaning that the text indicates as a basis for reasoning (such as: ﴾so that it does not become a state﴿).
The invalidation of general induction:
The Sharia contains rulings that are reasoned and rulings that are not reasoned.
The causes mentioned in the texts do not all refer to a benefit or harm; rather, each has a specific meaning (for example: distraction from Friday prayer, or disbelief as a barrier to inheritance).
Describing them as beneficial is a human attribution from the perspective of a Muslim, not a legal indication from the text.
Critique of basing rulings on unmentioned benefits:
Making unmentioned benefits a proof leads to the domination of reason over legislation, which contradicts the principle of limiting indications to texts.
It is not permissible for reason to be the source of determining legal causes or building rulings upon them in the absence of text.
Refutation of the classification of benefits (necessary, need-based, and embellishing):
This classification is not legally precise:
"The five necessities" is not exhaustive, as there are other necessary objectives (such as preserving the state, security).
