This Islamic association was theoretically a unifying religious bond – meaning the revival of the concept of a single Ummah in which "there is no distinction between an Arab and a non-Arab except in piety." However, in practice, the reformists employed it in a national political context as well: for instance, we saw Afghani ally with Egyptian nationalists against the Khedive, and cooperate with the Ottoman Sultan (despite his previous criticisms of him) when he found that the interests of Muslims required it. In fact, Rashid Rida, after the fall of the Ottoman Caliphate, sought to transform the idea of Islamic unity into a political association led by Arabs (when he called for the King of Egypt to be the Caliph, or for the Arab countries to unite into one state). However, he failed due to the reality of fragmentation and colonialism.

The most dangerous aspect of the matter is "his call for multiple governments in the Islamic world instead of establishing a single Islamic state that gathers Muslims into one entity, and this is his understanding of Islamic unity"(49). How can he think of dividing Muslims into states while they are still under the Islamic state? This is a premature idea, and it resonates with British projects that were planning to fragment and colonize the Islamic state!

The point worthy of reflection is that the Islamic unity association gradually lost its spiritual significance and became closer to a political dream. It transformed from a bond of religious loyalty (to the unifying Caliphate) into a form of political solidarity among states and peoples that may only be united by interest against an external enemy. This transformation was a natural result of the reformers' focus on civil and national advancement first, so religion for them came as a cultural motivating factor more than a transcendent doctrinal loyalty across nationalities. Additionally, the behavior of some of Afghani and Abduh's students contributed to this transformation: for example, Saad Zaghloul (a student of Abduh) led a national revolution in Egypt in 1919 with a liberal, non-Islamic content, raising the slogan "Religion is for God, and the homeland is for all." Similarly, Ahmed Lutfi el-Sayed adopted Pharaonic Egyptian nationalism and called for completely separating Egypt from its Islamic surroundings. These were among the elite intellectuals who were influenced by Abduh's ideas at one stage of their lives, but they ultimately turned towards formulating national identities in the European style. Thus, it can be said that Islamic unity in its traditional sense (the bond of the religious Caliphate) was gradually emptied, and was replaced by a vision closer to a League of Islamic Nations or a formal political conference (as is the case with the Organization of Islamic Cooperation today). This development was not something the reformists theoretically desired, but it occurred implicitly as a result of their alignment with the concept of the modern nation-state.
