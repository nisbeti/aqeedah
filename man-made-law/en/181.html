He proposed visions for the reform of Al-Azhar and the religious institutes according to Cromer's moderate perspective. The stated goal was to develop the judicial and religious education system, but Britain aimed to integrate religious leaders into the modern state system and ensure their loyalty by educating them in a "liberating" legal culture that was not limited to the sciences of Sharia. Some contemporary scholars justified Abduh's collaboration by claiming that he sought to achieve some reform from within the framework of the occupation, while others considered it a complicity with the occupying authority at the expense of the independence of scholars.

On the other hand, many researchers consider Muhammad Abduh to be the father of the idea of "liberal Islam." He is the one who established that Islam is a flexible religion suitable for all times and places, with its fixed general principles and evolving applications. This statement was the beginning of distorting the religion, for the Sharia (Islamic law) is not flexible enough to apply to every thought, even if it contradicts it, but it is broad enough to encompass new issues using the same foundational methodology in deriving detailed rulings and general principles for new occurrences. The Sharia is not flexible at all, neither in its roots nor in its branches, and the problem lies in the criteria and standards for development. Abduh and his followers focused on development to the extent that what they described as fixed was lost amidst the whirlpool of changes, not to mention what they considered to be among the changes! This erroneous mentality in understanding the Sharia continued until it was adopted by many who are counted among intellectual circles, such as al-Ghannouchi and others, in dividing Islam into fixed and variable, as if they were being inspired with rulings that descend upon what they called the variable, or that Islam left it without texts to address it. Consequently, they took reason as a legislator in truth. Thus, Abduh paved the way for an indirect separation between religion and state: he considered the Sharia to govern ethics and general principles, while legislative details could change to suit temporal interests. From here, those who would go further in this separation emerged from his cloak – as Ali Abd al-Raziq did later – and thus many currents filled the Islamic world today with their directions that are detached from the Sharia, from the roots, and from the methodologies!

Rashid Rida himself went through stages in his position regarding nationalist and liberal thought. In his early days, under the influence of Abduh, he attempted to reconcile Western civilization with Islam, and supported, for example, the Young Turk movement, believing it would be reformist. However, after World War I, he defended the idea of the Islamic caliphate and attacked Turkish nationalism, which had abolished the caliphate. He then leaned towards building an Arab Islamic nationalist project: that is, a caliphate whose nucleus would be the Arabs after the collapse of the Ottoman state. Despite his dream of Islamic unity, he practically supported the Arab revolt against the Turks in 1916, hoping for Arab independence - this is a clear nationalist dimension.
