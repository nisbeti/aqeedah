However, the real turning point occurred in the modern era, with the emergence of movements known as reformist and renewal movements in the 19th and 20th centuries. In the midst of some scholars' attempts to confront the challenges of backwardness on one hand, and Western intellectual hegemony on the other, a new approach was proposed: to reinterpret the Sharia (Islamic law) according to its "overall objectives (maqasid)" instead of merely adhering to the apparent meanings of its specific texts – as they claimed – in order to "suit the times." Here, the concepts of public interest (maslaha) and objective played a pivotal role.
The leader of this trend is Jamal al-Din al-Afghani (d. 1897 CE) and his school, followed by Muhammad Abduh (d. 1905 CE), Rashid Rida, and others. These individuals raised the slogan "Islam is the religion of public interest (maslaha)" and reinterpreted the old saying of al-Tufi that if public interest conflicts with certain texts, public interest should take precedence. However, they went further than what al-Tufi had allowed:
For example, Muhammad Abduh took into account public interest (maslaha) in almost all of his rulings and fatwas, even if they contradicted the well-known opinions of the jurists. Among this is that he changed some rulings regarding charitable endowments (waqf) in Egypt under the pretext of developing them economically (such as his fatwa permitting the exchange of waqf properties or not adhering to the stipulations of the founder if the ruler deemed otherwise).
He also issued a fatwa permitting the benefits of the postal savings fund (which is a government savings bank) on the grounds of public interest (maslaha) for small depositors and economic development, a fatwa that sparked widespread controversy at the time and was considered by many to be a clear justification of the prohibited usury (riba). However, he justified it by claiming that the texts prohibited excessive and exploitative usury, while the benefits of "moderate" banks are a form of beneficial financial cooperation – in his opinion.
Here we notice a qualitative shift: "public interest (maslaha)" in this new thought now means what achieves direct worldly material benefit, even if it contradicts the apparent text of the religious law, on the assumption that the text may be contextualized to a specific historical circumstance or a particular interest at its time. From their perspective, religion is flexible and evolves with the changing interests of people. It is noteworthy that the term "objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia)" has been strongly revived in this context, but with an understanding that differs from that of previous scholars; for some, it has become a basis for developing and changing rulings rather than a criterion for understanding their established reasons.
