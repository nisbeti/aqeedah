From discipline in the principles to laxity and chaos!

One of the most prominent features that distinguishes the science of usul al-fiqh (principles of jurisprudence) in Islam is that it is a disciplined science based on solid foundations derived from revelation, and methodologies of thought and reasoning that have accumulated through codification and refinement based on induction, deduction, and reasoning, along with scientific methods grounded in legal principles that have been established and refined over the centuries. This ensures that the derivation of legal rulings is disciplined and based on sound principles, not on whims or arbitrary rational estimations.

Thus, usul al-fiqh is not merely a set of theoretical tools, but rather a comprehensive system of rules, standards, and methodologies aimed at protecting the process of ijtihad (independent juristic reasoning) from error, and ensuring that its results are consistent with the legal texts and the general doctrinal and legislative framework of Islam. It revolves around fixed legal determinants, such as ensuring that its outcomes are based on revelation, and preventing any purely rational legislation, as well as dealing with detailed evidence in a methodological manner in terms of understanding it and relating it to the reality in which the legal ruling is sought, among other determinants that ensure the proper functioning of the ijtihad process leading to the achievement of its objectives. Therefore, every legal school was not merely a collection of scattered fatwas (legal opinions), nor were its principles random or fabricated; rather, it was a scientific school with a comprehensive methodological framework that is consistent with its foundational concepts and rules in understanding the text, applying it to reality, organizing evidence, presenting it, and weighing it.

Islamic thinking methodologies have been established— as outlined in the book on epistemology and thinking methodologies— on the principle of "no cognitive chaos," and have emerged since the dawn of Islam in response to the command of revelation to use reason not to negate or selectively choose texts, but to understand them and apply them according to disciplined rules of thought and deduction. Hence, one of the most dangerous manifestations of contemporary deviation is the emergence of what is called "the jurisprudence of minorities," which represents, in some of its applications, a glaring model of the loss of principled discipline, and indeed a complete dissolution from the principles that ensure the integrity of ijtihad.

Islamic jurisprudence based on usul al-fiqh relies on definitive sources (the Qur'an, the Sunnah, consensus, and analogy) and established principles of derivation (general usuli rules). In the context of jurisprudence based on public interests (maslaha), we find the objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia), where we see that the concept of "interest" in legal ijtihad in this school is constrained by strict conditions:

It must be real (not imaginary).

It must be general (not partial).

It must be compliant with the Sharia, meaning it does not contradict a definitive text, consensus, or established legal principle.

The detailed evidence must indicate its consideration as an interest.

The main problem in most applications of "the jurisprudence of minorities" is the replacement of this "disciplined legal interest" with a "purely rational interest" derived solely from consideration of the Western reality and its pressures, without a close connection to the evidence.
