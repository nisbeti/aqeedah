All these elements do not operate individually but in integration, according to a precise intellectual system that preserves the textual and rational authority of the Sharia, and prevents dilution or stagnation.

The scholars' stance on the use of public interest (maslaha)

The positions of jurists regarding public interest (maslaha) have divided into three main directions:

Those who absolutely prohibit it: Among them are the Shafi'is and some of the Hanafis; they prohibited considering any interest that is not supported by a specific text. Their argument is that opening this door could lead to legislating based on personal desires and the possibility of opposing texts. Imam al-Shafi'i said in his famous message: "Whoever finds something good has legislated" – and in another narration: "Preference (istihsan) is indulgence, and whoever permits himself to prefer has made himself a partner with Allah in establishing rulings." Thus, al-Shafi'i's idea is clear: there is no room for mere opinion or reason, no matter how good something may seem, to legislate a ruling unless it is based on a legal proof. This position is consistent with his principle of limiting the sources of legislation to the Qur'an, the Sunnah, consensus (ijma), and analogy (qiyas).

Those who permit it under conditions: This position is represented by the Malikis, the majority of the Hanbalis, and some of the Hanafis. They permitted the use of public interest (maslaha) but under strict conditions, including that the interest must be real and not imaginary, general and not specific, and must not contradict a text or consensus. Al-Qarafi (who is a Maliki) said: "The appropriate (i.e., the interest) is what entails achieving a benefit or averting harm." Therefore, if the interest is realized under its conditions and is compatible with the objectives of the Sharia, they accepted it. Nevertheless, the disagreement remained: do they consider it an independent proof or is it actually referring back to other proofs (such as the principle of necessity or the generalities of the Qur'an and Sunnah)? Many of the scholars of the Maliki school believe it refers back to the generalities, meaning it is not a new source of legislation, but rather an application of a general legal principle to a situation without a specific text. This is a precise understanding that greatly narrows its scope. Thus, the scholar al-Shatibi stated: "Public interest (maslaha) is only considered if it falls within the objectives of the legislator."

Practical acceptance without clear theoretical foundation: This is attributed to some of the earlier Hanafis, who acted upon some preferences and interests as exceptions without establishing a theoretical foundation for them as an independent principle. Actions such as those reported about Abu Hanifa in his fatwa regarding the Malikis on accusations that undermine honor (in accepting the testimony of someone whose integrity is not known based on their apparent condition) and similar cases can be categorized as caring for the interest without direct textual evidence.
