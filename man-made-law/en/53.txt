Chapter: The Legal Standard in Considering Public Interest (maslaha) and the Limits of Reason in Ijtihad: Towards a Methodological Regulation that Does Not Allow for Personal Desires in Legislation
It is the wisdom of Allah that He has endowed humans, as social beings possessing the vital energy manifested in needs and instincts that require satisfaction and organization, and has honored them with reason as a tool for understanding and thinking, not as a source of legislation. The need for food, for example, drives a person to seek work, ownership, or exchange, all of which are relationships that require organization. Likewise, the instinct for procreation drives a person to marriage, necessitating legislation that organizes the relationship between male and female, lineage, dowry, guardianship, and so forth. However, reason, despite being a tool for understanding these relationships and their regulations, cannot legislate for them from the outset; rather, it receives legislation from revelation and comprehends it.
From this, it is essential that human relationships be built on an organization that achieves the procurement of benefits and the prevention of harms, rather than being left in chaos where each individual pursues their own interests according to their whims, which would lead to corruption rather than benefit. If people were left to determine the standards of buying and profit themselves, for instance, some might see commercial fraud as a means to achieve quick benefit, or that usury guarantees them assured profit. However, in reality, this leads to widespread economic corruption and to injustice and destructive disparities, as we see in the capitalist system.
While we can establish some regulations for legislation that organizes societal relationships and human behavior to procure benefits for both individuals and society and to prevent harms from them and from society within three categories, as is customary in research, such as classifying interests into necessary (like preserving life through the prohibition of murder), need-based (like permitting travel for trade), and enhancement (like prohibiting the consumption of impurities and addressing people with kind words).
However, this does not at all exhaust the classification of the Sharia, as we find types of legislation that fall under categories such as pure worship of Allah, testing and trial, education and purification, and absolute submission to affirm human servitude, and to elevate and bring them closer to Allah without regard to benefits and harms at all. The prayer with its numbers and the number of units, its specified times, fasting from dawn to sunset, the pilgrimage, the waiting period for a widow, fasting three days during pilgrimage and seven upon returning, and that ablution is from the two events (hadathayn) and not from sweat and dirt, all of these are purely devotional rulings that require absolute compliance, even if the wisdom behind them is hidden or seems burdensome or incomprehensible to the human mind.
Moreover, on the other hand, we find that "public interests" related to society may not align with "individual interests." For example, the public interest in prohibiting monopolies prevents a trader from raising prices even though he sees this as a personal benefit. Similarly, the prohibition of bribery exists despite the personal benefits it may provide to certain parties.
