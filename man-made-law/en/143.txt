Critique of relying on the principle of "the lesser of two evils": This chapter discusses the misuse of this principle, which has become "a key to justifying prohibitions," whereas in traditional jurisprudence it is only applied under strict regulations and exceptional conditions.
The concept of "the jurisprudence of reality": From a supportive jurisprudence to a ruling authority: The book warns against the transformation of "the jurisprudence of reality (fiqh al-waqi)" from being a tool for understanding the circumstances of reality in order to apply the legal rulings to that reality, to an authority above the text, which compels the jurist to suspend the legal text in the name of "understanding the era" or "the jurisprudence of possibility (fiqh al-mumkin)", which makes reality a standard above the texts, rather than beneath them.
The confusion between legitimate governance (siyasa shar'iyya) and ideological deviation: This section reveals how some preachers exploit the terms of legitimate governance to pass dangerous ideological and doctrinal compromises, and it emphasizes that legitimate governance does not mean undermining the Sharia to achieve immediate utilitarian goals.
Summary: The jurisprudence of regulated balances is a legal necessity, while the jurisprudence of unregulated balances, which is used to bypass texts in the name of "public interest (maslaha)" and "preventing harm," poses a danger to the constants of the religion, leading to a serious methodological slip towards legal dilution and adaptation to political whims.
Subject of the chapter:
Alongside the jurisprudence of minorities (fiqh al-aqalliyat), the term "jurisprudence of balances" (fiqh al-mawazanat) or "jurisprudence of priorities" (fiqh al-awlawiyyat) has become prevalent in the literature of some advocates; it refers to the balancing of interests and harms in emerging issues, and it aims to weigh the interests and harms in reality to favor the greater good and avert the greater evil.
The idea, in principle, is sound if it adheres to the methodology of balancing and preference in the principles of jurisprudence (usul al-fiqh), and is aligned with the rules of public interest (maslaha mursala) that we have previously established in various parts of the book, to ensure that the methodology for determining interests, and the methodology for weighing them against each other, is all governed by revelation, whether by relying on detailed evidence that specifies the interest, or by defining the interest in light of a precise legal application of the general rules and the objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia) according to the aforementioned criteria.
We may find balances between certain issues mentioned in the Noble Qur'an, such as what is found in the story of Moses (peace be upon him) with al-Khidr. Al-Khidr weighed the preservation of the ship against the tyrannical king seizing it, and the option of damaging it so that it would be saved for its owners. Such legal balances are indicated by legal evidence, but some of them may be specific to that incident. For example, he killed the boy so that his parents would not be burdened with tyranny and disbelief. We cannot say that this interest (maslaha) is applicable, because he was acting under divine revelation, and we do not have anything that informs us of what will become of any boy we might think, for instance, that he will burden his parents today. Thus, it cannot be compared to that incident; rather, it can be inferred that what the Sharia has clarified regarding interests and harms is that it sometimes weighs them.
