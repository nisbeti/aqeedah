For the chapter: Why do we investigate the conditions, attributes, and characteristics of legislation?

The purpose of this chapter is not merely a theoretical presentation of legislation, but a serious attempt to answer a fundamental question: What makes any legislation—regardless of its source—valid and suitable for organizing human life and regulating their behavior?

Human life cannot be properly conducted without laws that regulate actions, define relationships, ensure rights, and prevent chaos, aggression, and corruption. However, legislation can only fulfill its intended role if it possesses certain characteristics that make it sound, strong, just, and valid, capable of:

Achieving comprehensive justice without bias or oppression.

Safeguarding essential rights defined for humans, establishing justice among individuals in society, and preventing injustice and transgression against sanctities. Among these rights are: the right to life, the sanctity of the body, the sanctity of honor, the right to own what the legislator has permitted to be owned, the right to a fair trial, the duty of justice and fairness in treatment, and the duty to preserve the set of concepts, standards, and convictions upon which the state is founded and which society has agreed upon, through legitimate mechanisms such as enjoining good and forbidding evil, advising, and accountability. Therefore, people must be empowered to fulfill this duty, whether through forming political parties or by directly undertaking their responsibility to change what is wrong, and they are accountable for that. They should express their opinions in accordance with the concepts and regulations of the Sharia, free from slander, spreading immorality, undermining values, or insulting sacred matters, so that advice remains within its legislative framework and does not lead to a greater wrongdoing. The ruler is held accountable to prevent transgression against rights or the subjects within regulated mechanisms. This is a system broader than merely allowing individuals to speak freely; it is part of a framework that places responsibilities on society and its individuals to maintain the system of values, concepts, standards, and convictions adopted by the nation to prevent deviation from them. Humans are bound by legal obligations to achieve the perfection of their servitude to their Lord, and this commitment to obligations alone safeguards dignity and grants humans their true meaning.

In this context, and in light of the obligation for humans to reflect on the creation of the heavens and the earth, to reach the purpose of existence and interpret it, and in light of blending this responsibility for seeking the truth with its deep legal, ethical, and existential dimensions, it is guaranteed for the rational, responsible human the right to choose their belief without coercion to change it, preserving their dignity and conscious choice, and ensuring their freedom to adopt what they see as true. However, this freedom should not be used as a pretext to dilute established doctrinal truths based on definitive rational and legal evidence, nor to blur the distinctions between truth and falsehood, as this would lead to undermining the value of serious inquiry into the truth and weakening commitment.
