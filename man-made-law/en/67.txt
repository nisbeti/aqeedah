This ruling was based on a general Sharīʿah principle: the removal of harm (rafʿ al-ḍarar), citing the hadith: “There is to be neither harm nor reciprocating harm.” They also referenced the hadith: “Whoever sells a slave woman with whom he has intimate relations, her intimacy is for him alone.” From this, they inferred that the master’s intimacy is not merely a physical act but entails humane rights that cannot be nullified through sale. The ruling is thus rooted in general texts and objectives—like protecting dignity and preventing injustice.
2. Prohibiting the Execution of a Group for the Murder of One Individual (Unless Proven Conspiratorial)
Contrary to the majority of jurists, the Mālekīs prohibited executing a group of people for the killing of one person unless joint conspiracy is proven. They cited the hadith: “More than one person may not be executed for killing one person” (reported by al-Bayhaqī and authenticated by Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr), and argued that qiṣāṣ (retaliation) is built on justice and equivalence, not vengeance. They also referenced the verse: “And We ordained for them therein: a life for a life…” [al-Mā’idah 5:45], arguing that the language demands equivalence, which is absent in executing multiple individuals for one death, unless fairness is established.
3. Requiring the Consent of an Adult Virgin in Marriage and Prohibiting Her Forced Marriage
While some scholars allowed coercing an adult virgin into marriage, the Mālekīs required her explicit consent. They cited the hadith: “The virgin must be consulted regarding herself,” and: “A matron has more right over herself than her guardian, and the virgin is to be asked for permission.” Although these texts do not explicitly prohibit coercion, their tone and purpose affirm the value of consent and personal dignity. Since marriage is a contract of mutual agreement, coercion contradicts its essence. This ruling is thus grounded in sound scriptural objectives—not in subjective reasoning.
4. Rejecting Legal Stratagems (ḥiyal) to Circumvent the Prohibition of Usury (e.g., ʿīnah Transactions)
Mālekīs rejected deceptive sales contracts that disguise usury, such as selling a commodity on deferred payment then buying it back for cash at a lower price. They deemed this hidden usury. They based their judgment on the principle: “Matters are judged by their purposes,” and on the verses: “Do not consume one another’s wealth unjustly,” [al-Baqarah 2:188] and: “Allah has permitted trade and forbidden usury.” [al-Baqarah 2:275] They also cited the statements of the Companions emphasizing the closure of pathways to forbidden matters (sadd al-dharā’iʿ). Thus, even if the external form resembled a sale, the intention was usurious profit, which the Sharīʿah forbids.
5. Accepting the Sole Testimony of a Woman in Matters of Nursing and Childbirth
Although the default in testimony is two men or one man and two women, Mālekīs accepted the testimony of a single woman in cases typically known only to women—such as nursing or childbirth. They grounded this on the purpose of testimony: achieving knowledge, and interpreted the verse: “If there are not two men, then one man and two women from those whom you approve as witnesses...” [al-Baqarah 2:282],to mean that the number of witnesses is intended for preserving accuracy, not based on gender per se. They also cited the hadith of ʿUqbah ibn al-Ḥārith, in which the Prophet ﷺ separated him from his wife based on the sole testimony of a woman that she had nursed them both. This shows how Mālekīs established rulings from transmitted texts and extended them through maqāṣid-based analogy.
