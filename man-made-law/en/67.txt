They based their ruling on a general legal principle, which is the removal of harm, citing his saying (peace and blessings be upon him): "There should be neither harm nor reciprocating harm," and they also referenced the hadith "Whoever sells a slave and has goods in her, the goods belong to him," deriving from it that the master’s sexual relations are not merely a carnal relationship, but entail human rights that cannot be disregarded simply due to the sale. Thus, the ruling is governed by general texts and objectives such as the protection of honor and the removal of injustice.
Prohibition of punishing a group for the act of one in retribution:
The Malikis disagreed with the majority of jurists on this issue, and they did not permit that a group of people be killed if they killed one person, unless it is established that they all conspired together. They supported their view with the hadith "No more than one should be killed for one" (narrated by al-Bayhaqi and authenticated by Ibn Abd al-Barr), and that retribution is based on justice and equality, not on vengeance. They relied on Allah's saying: ﴾And We decreed upon them therein a life for a life﴿, arguing that the expression indicates equivalence, and equivalence is absent in punishing a group for the act of one, unless it is done according to established justice.
Conditioning the consent of a mature virgin in marriage and not forcing her:
Although some jurists permitted forcing a mature virgin, the Malikis required her consent, citing the hadith: "The virgin is to be asked for her consent," and his saying (peace and blessings be upon him): "The widow has more right over herself than her guardian, and the virgin is to be asked." They emphasized that the generality of these texts, even if they do not explicitly prohibit coercion, indicates through implication and purpose the respect for will and human dignity, and that marriage is a contract of mutual consent without coercion, which is a recognized interest based on legal transmission rather than personal desire.
Rejection of legal tricks in sales such as 'ina:
The Malikis rejected the manipulation of usury under the guise of sales, such as selling a commodity at a deferred price and then buying it back for cash at a lower price, considering this a hidden usury. They based this ruling on the principle: "Actions are judged by their intentions," and cited Allah's saying: ﴾And do not consume one another's wealth unjustly﴿, and His saying: ﴾And Allah has permitted trade and forbidden usury﴿, as well as the sayings of the Companions regarding blocking the means to harm. They viewed that the form of sale here is inseparable from the intention of usury, which is an application of a legal principle to a contemporary situation.
Acceptance of the testimony of a single woman in matters of breastfeeding and childbirth:
Although the norm for testimony is the presence of two men or one man and two women, the Malikis accepted the testimony of a single woman in matters that are typically known only to women, such as breastfeeding and childbirth, relying on the objectives of testimony, which is "to establish knowledge," and on Allah's saying: ﴾And if there are not two men, then a man and two women from those whom you accept as witnesses﴿, where they understood that the multiplicity in testimony is for the preservation of knowledge and accuracy, not based on the gender of the witness. They also cited the hadith of Uqbah ibn al-Harith when the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) distinguished between him and his wife based on the testimony of a woman that she had breastfed them both. Thus, they established the ruling on transmission and linked new issues through purposive analogy.
