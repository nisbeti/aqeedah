This indicates that interpretation without controls has led to transgressions that prompted even some pioneers of the school to reconsider its validity.
The dismantling of the traditional foundational structure and the opening of the door to renewal without controls
One of the direct results of prioritizing reason and public interest (maslaha) and interpreting texts is that the rational reformist school paved the way for dismantling some pillars of the foundational methodology in derivation that had been established over centuries. Its pioneers attacked the principle of "taqlid" (following established schools of thought) in jurisprudence, calling for the door of ijtihad (independent reasoning) to be opened wide after it had been nearly closed in the later centuries. This, in essence, is a commendable call to revive legal ijtihad; however, the problem is that they did not adhere to the controls of traditional legal ijtihad in many instances. Rather, some of them exhibited a tendency to undermine certain established foundations if they saw them as obstacles to the development they sought. This was manifested, for example, in their disdain for the consensus of jurists or in presenting unusual opinions for some interest. We saw how Abduh opposed the consensus of scholars on the prohibition of usury by separating postal deposits from it, and how he deviated from the legal methodology regarding endowments. The matter later expanded among some who followed in their footsteps to the extent of questioning the texts themselves. The saddening and lamentable fact is that these new rationalists did not limit themselves to questioning and rejecting the jurists' ijtihads, but some of them went further to question and reject hadiths and verses, ignoring them under the pretext that they were not suitable for "our time"... Thus, they are judging the revelation itself, both definitive and speculative, to please the infidel West! This is a reference to what the extremists of modernism - after Abduh and Rida - fell into, such as Ali Abd al-Raziq and Taha Hussein (we will come to them) in rejecting some authentic texts or interpreting the history of Islam itself from a purely materialistic perspective.
The discourse of these individuals aimed to dismantle several constants in the sciences of Sharia: they undermined the authority of some prophetic hadiths under the pretext of opposing reason or science, and they questioned some of the definitive rulings of the Qur'an on the grounds that they were not suitable for modern human rights (such as the penalties for apostasy and criminal punishments). Some of them even went so far as to call for the dismissal of all legal evidence, relying solely on “public interest,” “custom,” and “reality.” These individuals did not content themselves with placing legal rulings under scrutiny, but they went beyond that to the foundations that had never been a subject of dispute, seeking to undermine their rules and structure, leaving only public interest, custom, habit, and reality. All of these are not independent sources of legislation in reality, but are regulated through revelation; yet they treated them as if they were the only source!
In other words, they ended up with a vague legislative reference: reason, public interest, and reality take precedence over the text. If they found the text aligning with their desires, they acted upon it; if they found it opposing, they claimed it was “contextual,” or they interpreted it, or outright rejected it. Thus, they opened the door to ijtihad and renewal, but it was an ijtihad that was not constrained by the original scientific methodology established by the scholars of usul al-fiqh (principles of jurisprudence) to protect derivation from error. Everything became subject to reconsideration among the advocates of radical renewal: from the Islamic family system, to the system of governance, to the obligatory religious duties known by necessity in the religion. All of this was done in the name of development and consideration of the changing circumstances of time and place.
