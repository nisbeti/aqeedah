Detail: The capacities of minds vary in strength and weakness, and in assessing harm and benefit, and understandings differ among people; what one person sees as good, another sees as bad. The estimation of minds also varies regarding the means to achieve benefits, and minds differ in understanding the harmony of laws with the fabric of public law in the state and their realization of the system of values and legislative objectives, among other factors whose existence cannot be denied, which lead to the inability of reason to truly grasp benefits.
There is no such thing as "pure, abstract, or absolute reason" that directs the course of social development, characterized as "good by nature," and free from whims and biases. Moreover, the reason that is free from deficiency and desire, which is not influenced by passion and ignorance, does not exist in the realm of reality; rather, it is an "ideal." One of the most prominent manifestations of the shortcomings of human reason in the field of legislation is its lack of a fixed objective standard in perceiving values and determining solutions; human reason is not an abstract entity free from desire, nor is it a purely rational force capable of producing comprehensive and binding legislation for all people. Rather, it is a relative reason, influenced by cultural environment, social affiliations, personal experiences, temporal context, and the psychological and cognitive factors surrounding it. As a result, rational judgments differ among individuals and communities, and even the reasoning of a single individual varies in evaluating issues depending on their stages and experiences.
What one particular mind sees as good may be viewed as bad by another mind, and what one nation perceives as a good creation or a legitimate solution may be seen by another nation as deviation or error, and it may even be viewed as an aggression against values and identity.
