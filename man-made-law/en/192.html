the conclusion and recommendations

After this lengthy exploration in the research "The Authority of Legislation between Reason and Revelation in Islamic Thought," we arrive at a conclusion that emphasizes the most important findings and results we have reached, asking Allah to grant us success in clarifying the truth supported by evidence, aiming to establish a compelling argument as requested. The most prominent results can be summarized as follows:

First: Legislation is a pure right of Allah alone – this is the foundation of belief and monotheism. For Allah, Exalted is He, is the Creator who knows the affairs of His creation, the most knowledgeable of what rectifies them and what corrupts them; thus, it is only He who has the right to establish for them laws and regulations. We have demonstrated this with explicit Qur'anic texts such as the verse: ﴾Indeed, judgment belongs to Allah﴿, and His saying: ﴾Or do they have partners who have legislated for them in religion that which Allah has not permitted?﴿ (Ash-Shura: 21) – this is an interrogative denial that clarifies that it is not permissible for anyone to legislate without Allah's permission. Consequently, every law or system that contradicts what Allah has revealed is legally invalid, even if its proponents believe in its validity. A ruling can only be considered legitimate if it is based on evidence from the Book or the Sunnah or what arises from them, such as consensus or analogy, and the like.

Second: The role of reason in legislation is understanding and application, not creation and innovation. We have seen the consensus of scholars describing reason as a tool for understanding the texts of revelation and deriving rulings from them, not as an independent source for establishing rulings from the outset. No matter how intelligent reason may be, it remains relatively limited, constrained by time and place, and is even susceptible to error and desire. Therefore, it cannot be trusted in comprehensive legislation. Yes, reason is necessary for deriving the nuances of rulings from the texts and applying them to realities, and this is the correct ijtihad (independent juristic reasoning). However, the framework of ijtihad is the text: it revolves around it in existence and non-existence; if a text exists, it must be followed and its guidance accepted, and if the text is absent, the mujtahid (jurist) seeks an analogy or a principle to apply. In all cases, reason remains subordinate to evidence, not the other way around. We have quoted strong words from the imams in condemnation of those who follow their reason in the religion of Allah at the expense of the tradition. Thus, the claim that "reason can be a legislator or a ruler" is completely false in Islamic thought.

Third: The limitations of human reason in fully grasping collective interests. We have proven through intellectual analysis and real-world evidence that a person may perceive something as beneficial while it is actually harmful, and vice versa. We enumerated the limitations of reason: temporal (lack of knowledge of the future), spatial (influence by the culture of their environment), psychological (bias towards their desires or upbringing), and ignorance of many truths. Hence, the Sharia came to address this limitation; it is a grave error for some to place the limited reason as a ruler over the complete Sharia, or to claim it is filling what they perceive as a "legislative void." We have established that there can be no legislative void in religion; rather, the limited must submit to the complete, and the deficient must yield to the perfect.
