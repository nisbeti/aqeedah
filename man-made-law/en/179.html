Renewal between the Usuli Methodology and Western Disruption

To clarify, we present in the following table a comparison between the traditional Islamic usuli (principles of jurisprudence) understanding of certain legislative concepts and what the modern rationalist school has proposed as a different approach, and the impact of that on the structure of legislation:

Traditional Usuli Understanding

Modern Rationalist School Proposal

Legal Concept

A blessing used in understanding revelation and deriving rulings, but it is not infallible and follows the legal evidence. Clear reason is not prioritized over a correct text, as scholars believe there is fundamentally no contradiction between reason and explicit transmission.

Reason is a primary source of legislation, which reformists elevate to the point of prioritizing it over the texts of the Qur'an and Sunnah in cases of conflict. They believe that religious rulings must be rationally justified by clear benefits; otherwise, they are subject to change.

Reason (Ra'y)

The Qur'an and Sunnah are the basis of legislation. The apparent meanings of texts are authoritative unless there is a valid counter-evidence. It is obligatory to act according to the text and follow its implications according to the rules of language and usul al-fiqh, while considering the objectives without negating the text.

The text is constrained by the circumstances of its time and can be interpreted or marginalized if it appears (to modern reason) to contradict the public interest or development. Many texts for them are speculative or historical and can be re-read to align with the "spirit of the age."

Legal Text

The consensus of the Companions, or the consensus of the people of Medina, or the consensus of the Ummah (scholars) is a valid proof in various usuli schools that preserves the stability of legislation. The legal heritage is the result of cumulative ijtihad (independent reasoning) that is regulated by principles and methodologies.

They do not see consensus as binding if it contradicts the present interest. They criticize many of the ijtihads of earlier jurists as "rigid traditional jurisprudence" that must be reviewed. Some have completely ignored consensus in definitive matters (such as the permissibility of minor usury, for example).

Consensus and Heritage

Recognized as a cause or wisdom in legislation under conditions: it must be legally regulated and not contradict a text or consensus. It is taken into account in qiyas (analogy) and masalih mursala (unrestricted public interests) with precise controls (such as considering the objectives of the Sharia and the general evidence), according to some usuli jurisprudential schools.

They have expanded the consideration of public interest to the point of making it abrogate the text at times. For them, public interest is what reason sees as beneficial for people in their era, even if it contradicts the apparent meaning of the Sharia. They raised the slogan "Wherever there is public interest, there is the law of God" in a modern rational sense, not in its regulated usuli meaning.

Public Interest (Maslaha)

These are the overarching goals of legislation (preservation of religion, life, intellect, lineage, and wealth). Its role is to guide the mujtahid (jurist) to understand the reasons for rulings and their correct application without abrogating the texts. It is not an independent source of legislation but is subordinate to revelation.

They have made it a tool for changing rulings under the pretext of achieving the "supreme objective." Some slogans, such as "renewing the religious discourse," for them mean adapting legal rulings to align with modern values like absolute freedom and absolute equality. Some even expand the claim that the objective (like public interest) takes precedence over a detailed legal ruling if they conflict.

Objectives of the Sharia (Maqasid al-Sharia)

Ijtihad is the exertion of effort to understand texts and derive rulings for new occurrences, while adhering to established principles. Renewal means reviving religion in hearts, refuting doubts, and applying rulings to the changing reality without altering the religion.

For them, ijtihad is almost unlimited; it is permissible to reconsider any ruling under the pretext of development. Renewal is understood as the continuous change of religion to fit contemporary civilization, even if it leads to abrogating some of what is considered fixed in the Sharia under the slogan "the evolution of rulings with the evolution of times."
