The Companions knew that there was a version with which the revelation was concluded, preserved in the final recitation. Ibn Hajar said in "Fath al-Bari" (9/45): "The final recitation is that upon which the authoritative manuscript was established, and all other conflicting readings were neglected. For this reason, Zayd ibn Thabit relied on it in the compilation." Thus, the summary is that their work was a confirmation of the revelation, not an exercise in rational interest (maslaha), and a collection of the pages in the form of the final recitation.

The written compilation was permissible in his time, while compiling it into a single manuscript represents a transition from a state of permissibility to a necessity of preservation and safeguarding, and it does not constitute the establishment of something that was not legislated.

Summary: The compilation of the Qur'an is not a foundation for a new legislation nor is it among the public interests (maslaha), but rather it is a continuation of what the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) did, in light of general texts that preserve the religion, and it is a removal of harm based on the principle of "there should be neither harming nor reciprocating harm," which is based on a detailed evidence.

The oath of the killer with the heirs of the blood in cases of accusation: There is no direct text regarding this, but the Maliki scholars believe that the principle "blood should not be shed in vain" necessitates the swearing of an oath by the killer if he denies it, in order to preserve blood and protect lives. This meaning is also indicated by the saying of the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him): "Ward off punishments by means of doubts."

The incident:

The oath of the accused killer when there is a charge, that is, if a murder victim is found and there is no conclusive evidence against the perpetrator, and the heirs of the victim accuse a person of murder, but he denies it, should the accused be made to take an oath?

Some Maliki scholars permitted taking an oath from the accused in this case, even though the principle is that "the burden of proof is on the claimant and the oath is on the one who denies."

The alleged evidence (according to those who consider it a public interest (maslaha murasala)):

This issue, according to some jurists, is considered an application of public interest (maslaha murasala), based on:

Achieving justice and preventing the loss of lives.

Averting the corruption resulting from the spread of killing without punishment.

Protecting souls and preserving lives.

They have cited the principle: "Blood should not be shed in vain," and the hadith: "Avert punishments by means of doubts," and they consider it a rational interest (maslaha) that has not been established by specific evidence, thus it falls under the category of unregulated interests (masalih mursala).

The response and verification (according to Imam al-Nabhani):

"And among the evidence they used is the oath of the accused killer when suspected. They saw that blood should not be wasted, so they ruled that he should be made to swear an oath. This incident is also rejected, for the killer cannot be made to swear an oath because the blood money (diya) must be based on confession or evidence, as established from the hadith of the oath (qasama) and others. It has not been authentically reported from the Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him) that he ordered the killer to swear an oath when suspected, so there is no evidence for it, and it cannot serve as a basis for analogy (qiyas), nor is it considered a legitimate public interest (maslaha) in Sharia."
