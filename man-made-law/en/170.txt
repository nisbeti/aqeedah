The goodness of justice and the ugliness of oppression are known in general, but the details of the laws that achieve justice and eliminate oppression are not something the intellect can grasp, so the legislator came to organize life in a way that establishes these virtues, which were clarified in text.
Likewise, the law warned us against two afflictions of the intellect: desire and doubt, or conjecture. Desire, meaning the inclination of the soul and its desires, may beautify matters for a person contrary to the truth. Thus, Allah, Exalted is He, said: ﴾And do not follow desire, lest it lead you astray from the path of Allah﴿ [Ṣād: 26]. As for doubt, it is the confusion in understanding or the arrival of a false idea that misleads thought if it is not fortified with knowledge and guidance. From here comes the command to seek knowledge and to verify, and not to engage in matters one does not understand, as a protection for the intellect from superstition and error.
The most prominent limitations on the intellect in the law are that it should not exceed its capacity. This means that it should remain within the bounds of what it can understand and submit to what cannot be comprehended in detail. This is evident in the realm of the unseen: for example, the intellect cannot conceive of the essence of Allah, Exalted is He (nor even the reality of the human soul, nor instincts), so its role here is to believe and submit to what Allah has informed about Himself. Similarly, in the realm of legislation: the intellect may not grasp the wisdom behind some rulings in detail, so it should not reject them for that reason, nor should it take the search for wisdom as a means to judge the legal rulings or comply with them. For instance: why is the Fajr prayer two units instead of four? This is beyond the capacity of the comparative intellect, so one submits in obedience to Allah's command. As for what reveals its wisdom through indications provided by legislation, it is a blessing and an increase in goodness, but if it does not manifest, then the belief that Allah is the Most Wise and the Most Knowledgeable suffices the rational Muslim.
Some scholars of Islam have erred when they attempted to balance the role of the intellect and the role of transmission (naql), and to avert contradiction between them, by separating the role of the intellect in beliefs from its role in understanding legislation. In the realm of beliefs, a person is tasked with reflection and judgment to arrive at the proof of the existence of the Creator, Exalted is He, and to some of His attributes that the intellect can utilize and demonstrate from their results, such as the attributes of knowledge, power, and creation, without the attributes of mercy and severity in punishment, and similar attributes that the intellect cannot perceive their effects in its view of the universe. It is expected that transmission will clarify what belongs to Allah in terms of attributes, and then the intellect reflects on proving the truth of the prophethood of the prophets and their miracles, until it establishes that this is the Prophet of Allah, and this is His message. At that point, the intellect ceases to be a judge, until it submits to the message and does not judge it with its intellect thereafter. It is not correct for it to delve into Allah's justice in punishing the disbelievers, because it does not possess the necessary cognitive tools to understand the issue in all its dimensions, nor can it measure Allah's justice against the concept of justice among humans, nor can it judge Allah's actions, nor question Him about them or about His wisdom. It cannot encompass knowledge of Allah, Exalted is He. Similarly, its role in legislation is limited to understanding and deriving rulings, and the intellect does not have the capacity to legislate or enact laws. There is no such thing as pure intellect or absolute intellect; rather, every person has their own intellect, inclinations, opinions, and desires. Thus, the role of the intellect is completely nullified when producing legislation, and we have previously established this conclusively in the first chapter of this book.
