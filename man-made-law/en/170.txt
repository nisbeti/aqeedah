Reason recognizes the general goodness of justice and the evil of oppression, but the intricate legal details that realize justice are not fully within its capacity—so revelation provides them through text.
Second, the Sharīʿah warns against two afflictions of the intellect: desire (hawā’) and doubt (shubhah). Desire misguides by beautifying falsehood. Hence, the Qur’an states: “And do not follow desire, lest it lead you astray from the path of God” (Ṣād: 26). Doubt is confusion in understanding or the reception of a false idea that leads astray if the intellect is not fortified with knowledge. Thus came the command to seek knowledge, verify claims, and refrain from delving into what one does not master. Third, the most critical boundary: the intellect must not transgress its domain. That is, it should halt at the limits of what it can comprehend, and submit to what lies beyond. This is especially evident in matters of the unseen. For example, the mind cannot conceive of God's essence, nor even the true nature of the soul or instincts. Its role is to believe and submit to what God reveals about Himself.
Similarly, in legislation: the intellect might not grasp the wisdom behind some rulings. It is not permitted, then, to reject them, nor to demand the wisdom before obeying. For instance, why is the dawn prayer two rakʿāt instead of four? This lies beyond the comparative capacity of reason, so the believer submits purely to divine command. If the wisdom is apparent from supporting legal indications, then it is a blessing; if not, then belief in God’s perfect wisdom and knowledge suffices the intelligent believer. Misuse of Reason in Theology and Law Some scholars mistakenly attempted to strike a balance between reason and revelation by separating their domains—assigning reason to theology (ʿaqīdah) and revelation to legal matters (sharīʿah). In belief, they claimed, one is obligated to use reason to establish God’s existence and some of His attributes, such as knowledge, power, and creative force—attributes that are evident in the universe. However, other attributes like mercy or severity in punishment are not observable in the cosmos and thus await revelation to define them.
Once the intellect affirms the truth of prophecy and miracles, it is no longer a judge over revelation. It must submit to the divine message and cease to interrogate it. One cannot, for example, question the justice of God's punishment of disbelievers, for the human intellect lacks the comprehensive tools to grasp the full dimensions of divine justice, nor may it equate divine justice with human standards. In legislation, reason is restricted to understanding and deriving meaning—it has no authority to legislate or enact laws. There is no such thing as a "pure intellect" or "universal rationality"—every person possesses his own mind, biases, views, and desires. Thus, reason is completely excluded from being a source of legislation. This was already definitively established in the first chapter of this book.
