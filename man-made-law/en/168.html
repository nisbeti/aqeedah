Chapter Four: Reason in the Theory of Knowledge and the Method of Legal Deduction

Executive Summary of Chapter Four: The Saying "There is No Ijtihad with a Text" and the Limits of Its Application:

This chapter addresses one of the most controversial and misinterpreted foundational rules in modern times, which is the rule: "There is no ijtihad (independent juristic reasoning) with a text." The aim is to clarify its correct meaning and expose the ways it has been misused in contemporary rational discourses that claim the right to bypass texts in the name of ijtihad or public interest (maslaha).

The book distinguishes between two distinct meanings of the rule:

The correct foundational meaning: It is not permissible to engage in ijtihad in a matter where there is a clear text, definitively established and indicative, because it suffices for opinion, and reason should not oppose revelation. As for texts or detailed evidence that have a speculative indication, it is necessary to follow the foundational rules in ijtihad to derive the text from them.

The distorted meaning: This is the claim that the rule completely restricts the freedom of ijtihad, or that it does not bind in cases of new realities, which opens the door to suspending texts under the pretexts of "understanding the age" or "considering changes."

The chapter illustrates how some contemporary thinkers, in the context of their renewal project, have diluted this rule, claiming that texts are only applicable in a temporal and contextual environment similar to their revelation; however, if the time changes, it is permissible to bypass them under the umbrella of ijtihad, public interest, and objectives (maqasid). Here, the book reveals the contradiction of these claims with the essence of the Sharia, which is an eternal discourse, and its objectives are everlasting.

The book then moves on to analyze the methodological paradox among advocates of bypassing the text; they confuse between speculative texts in which ijtihad is permissible and definitive texts that must be accepted, and they interpret the latter under the pretext of "purposive ijtihad" or "interest-based ijtihad."

The chapter also refutes some contemporary doubts, such as: that the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) engaged in ijtihad in worldly matters, so why should we not engage in ijtihad? Or that the Companions (may Allah be pleased with them) contradicted some hadiths, or that "public interest" may sometimes be stronger than the text. It responds to these with established principles and precise scientific methodology.

At the end of the chapter, the book emphasizes that the comparison between reason and text is only in understanding the text, not in rejecting it, and that if reason does not submit to revelation, it goes astray. It asserts that opening the door to unrestricted ijtihad without controls is the beginning of secularizing the Sharia and transforming it into human ideas subject to environment and desires.

Content of Chapter Four:

After clarifying the limits of the role of reason in legislation in the previous chapters, we now turn to discuss the status of reason within the Islamic theory of knowledge and the method of legal deduction. We will rely on the author's propositions regarding the theory of knowledge and methods of thinking and reasoning, as well as some discussions from the author's book "Ijtihad and Taqlid," to illustrate the role of reason as a tool for understanding.
