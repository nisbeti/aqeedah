Chapter Four: Reason in Epistemology and the Method of Sharīʿah-Based Inference

Executive Summary of Chapter Four: The Maxim “No Ijtihād with an Existing Text” and Its Proper Scope

This chapter examines one of the most contested and misapplied principles in contemporary Islamic legal discourse: the maxim “No ijtihād where a text exists”. The objective is to clarify its precise, orthodox meaning and to expose the distortions introduced by modern rationalist narratives that invoke this principle only to circumvent revelation in favor of reason or utility.

The author distinguishes between two fundamentally different interpretations of the maxim:

The Correct Usulī Interpretation:

Ijtihād is impermissible in cases where an explicit text exists that is both definitive in transmission (thubūt) and definitive in meaning (dalālah). In such instances, the revealed text suffices and there is no need, indeed, no room, for personal opinion. Reason is not to be used to override revelation. However, when a text is either speculative in its transmission or speculative in its indication, then sound legal reasoning (ijtihād) becomes necessary, operating under the established principles of uṣūl al-fiqh to derive the ruling from the text.

The Distorted Modern Interpretation: This claims that the maxim restricts intellectual freedom and should not be applied in contemporary circumstances. It opens the door to the suspension of scriptural texts under the pretense of “contextual understanding,” “accommodating change,” or “meeting the needs of the times.”

The chapter explains how certain contemporary reformists, in the context of their broader renewal projects, have diluted this principle. They argue that texts are only applicable to the historical and cultural circumstances of their revelation. If those conditions change, the text may be bypassed in favor of a new judgment rooted in utility, context, or so-called maqāṣid (higher objectives). The author demonstrates how this logic contradicts the very core of revelation, whose message is universal, timeless, and divinely preserved in its objectives and substance.

The chapter then analyzes the methodological confusion among those who advocate bypassing revelation. It shows how they conflate texts that are open to ijtihād—i.e., speculative texts—with those that are definitive and binding, misappropriating the tools of “maqāṣid reasoning” or “utilitarian ijtihād” to reinterpret even those rulings that carry definitive proof (qaṭʿī al-thubūt wa al-dalālah).

The author also refutes several contemporary misconceptions, such as: “The Prophet ﷺ himself exercised worldly judgment—why can’t we?” “The Companions sometimes departed from hadith—why can’t we?”

“Sometimes, maṣlaḥah (public interest) is more compelling than the scriptural text.”

Each of these is addressed through rigorous reference to foundational uṣūlī principles and a precise understanding of the scholarly tradition. In conclusion, the author affirms: The proper role of reason in relation to revelation is to understand the divine text, not to override it.
