Riḍā also criticized atheistic communism and socialism, but he did not object to what later came to be known as "Islamic socialism," which was proposed as an alternative, including redistributive measures like zakāt and Islamic social justice【aljazeera.net】. Indeed, among his followers and their intellectual heirs in Egypt and Syria, some began adapting certain socialist principles within an Islamic framework—like Muṣṭafā al-Sibāʿī’s writings on “Islamic Socialism.”

In short, the reformists laid the foundation for the idea that Islam can be adapted to accommodate all modern systems: it could absorb nationalism (love of country and anti-colonialism), accept liberalism (civil and constitutional liberties), and even internalize the social justice of socialism without embracing its atheism.

They considered this adaptation necessary to prevent Muslims from lagging behind in progress【aljazeera.net】.

We should also note their position on the issue of women in this modernization context. ʿAbduh supported women’s liberation—albeit relatively—and their education. His student Qāsim Amīn famously advocated for the removal of the veil in his book The Liberation of Women (1899)【aljazeera.net】. Riḍā wrote a biography of Amīn in al-Manār, indicating serious interest in his ideas. While ʿAbduh himself did not call for complete unveiling, his intellectual campaign paved the way for louder voices advocating for greater women’s participation in public life—all under the banner of “renaissance” and keeping pace with advanced societies. To summarize: The rationalist reformers adopted a method of accommodating modern ideologies and movements as much as possible. They sought to give nationalism, liberalism, and other ideas an Islamic veneer, believing this would demonstrate Islam’s capacity to evolve and stay relevant. They succeeded, to a large extent, in shaping a class of “enlightened Muslim intellectuals” who embraced Western modernity under a superficial Islamic framework. However, many critics argue that this method gradually stripped Islamic identity of its distinctiveness, reducing religion for some generations to flexible spiritual slogans that could conform to any ideology—after it had once been a comprehensive worldview with immutable foundations. Afghani and ʿAbduh’s Relationship with Freemasonry and the Pan-Islamic Project

A wide controversy has surrounded the relationship of Jamal al-Din al-Afghani and Muhammad ʿAbduh with Freemasonry. It is historically documented that al-Afghani joined a Masonic lodge in Egypt around 1876, and even established the “Star of the East” lodge, which was aligned with French Masonic ideals【aljazeera.net】. Muhammad ʿAbduh also entered Freemasonry under the guidance of his teacher, al-Afghani【aljazeera.net】. Sources indicate that al-Afghani invited Khedive Tawfiq to join the lodge with him in order to promote the idea of consultative governance (shūrā), and they pledged within the lodge to work towards a constitutional system to limit the despotism of Khedive Ismail. Regardless of intentions, this affiliation with Freemasonry is seen by most scholars as a grave error.
