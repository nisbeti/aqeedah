In this regard, they prioritized the judgment of reason and public interest (maslaha) over the indication of the text in cases of conflict, opposing the methodology of the Ahl al-Sunnah in making reason subordinate to revelation rather than a judge over it.

Afghani and Abduh reproduced the statement of Najm al-Din al-Tufi (d. 716 AH) regarding public interest (maslaha), where he stated that "Islam is the religion of public interest." They even adopted al-Tufi's view that if public interest conflicts with certain texts, public interest should take precedence. However, they expanded the scope of this principle beyond what al-Tufi himself could bear, as they did not restrict it to the criteria mentioned by scholars (such as the conflict being with a speculative text or a regulated unqualified public interest). Thus, reason became capable for them of establishing or suspending legal rulings based on its estimation of public interest, even if this contradicted the apparent meanings of the Qur'an and Sunnah or the consensus of the ummah. Some researchers have described the modern rationalist school as an extension of the thought of the Mu'tazila, who "elevate reason and prioritize it over the texts of the Qur'an and Sunnah." This statement is a conflation of issues, as the Mu'tazila are a group of theologians and not a legislative school like the Hanafis and Shafi'is, and their rationalist school was limited to the rational evaluation of good and evil within the scope of doctrinal discussions, and they did not advocate for legislation through reason!

Practical examples of prioritizing public interest (maslaha) over textual evidence

The fatwas of Imam Muhammad Abduh are a prime example of this approach. He issued a fatwa permitting the interest on funds deposited in postal savings (a government savings institution at the time), claiming that it was “a legitimate benefit and a modern investment” and not prohibited usury. Abduh relied on a distinction he considered “rational” between the exorbitant usury that is explicitly prohibited by text and the limited bank interest that he viewed as a form of financial cooperation. In reality, this fatwa contradicted the consensus of scholars; the text of the prophetic hadith is explicit: “Every loan that brings benefit is usury.” A significant controversy arose around it at the time, and many considered it an analysis of prohibited usury. Scholars such as Sheikh of Al-Azhar Salim al-Bishri and Sheikh Muhammad Bakhit al-Muti'i (the mufti of Egypt at that time) opposed it, and even Abduh's student Rashid Rida hesitated before later defending it.

Similarly, Abdu changed some rulings regarding charitable endowments (awqaf) under the pretext of achieving economic benefit for them: he permitted the exchange of the assets of the endowment and did not adhere strictly to the conditions set by the endower if the ruler saw that there was a public interest (maslaha) in doing so. This contradicts the established legal principle "the condition of the endower is like the text of the legislator," which has been upheld by the jurists of the schools of thought, especially in the Hanafi school. However, Abdu believed that the interests of modern society necessitated flexibility in the management of endowments to invest them in ways that benefit people instead of being rigidly bound to outdated conditions.

There is no doubt that these fatwas and changes were based on prioritizing "public interest (maslaha)" in its modern sense. The concept of public interest among the new rationalists has come to mean direct worldly benefit - whether economic or social - even if it contradicts the apparent evidence of the Sharia text. They assumed that texts that are definitive in authenticity or meaning could be contextualized to a specific historical circumstance, or that their rationale was to achieve a particular interest at their time. Thus, from their perspective, the legal ruling evolves with the evolution of interests.
