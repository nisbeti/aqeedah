The ijtihad (independent juristic reasoning) of Abu Hanifa was not purely rational and outside of revelation; rather, it is a precise connection between texts that regulate the actions of the imam, impose respect for public order, protect public wealth, and prevent disputes. This is part of the responsibilities that the legislator has entrusted to the imam. Additionally, it provides a solution to the dilemma of what if two Muslims revive land and dispute over it? Abu Yusuf said: "What Abu Hanifa said does not contradict the narration, [meaning that his statement does not conflict with the hadith], but rather the contradiction of the narration would be to say: 'If he revives it with the permission of the imam, then it does not belong to him,' [i.e., this scenario would be the one that conflicts with the hadith].
As for the one who says it is for him, this is following the tradition, but with the permission of the imam so that his permission serves as a resolution among them in their disputes and the harm some cause to others. As for me, [Abu Yusuf], I believe that if there is no harm to anyone and there is no dispute over it, then the permission of the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) is valid until the Day of Judgment. If harm comes, it is based on the hadith: "There is no right for a wrongdoer's claim."(24)
There are governing principles that are essentially derived and rule over secondary juristic opinions, such as the principle of "no harm and no harming" and the principle of resolving disputes by the authority and its responsibility for its subjects. I apply both of them in ijtihad (juristic reasoning), restricting the absolute text to not contradict them. Therefore, the reference for his opinion is not pure reason but rather the juristic opinions derived from the foundations and generalities of the Sharia (Islamic law). His juristic reasoning was "strong in interpreting the texts by their purposes, motivations, and causes, not limited in explaining them to the apparent meanings of their expressions"(25)
This form of istihsan (juridical preference) is a preference for a general indication derived from the totality of evidence, not an invention of a ruling from nothing. It is known that the problem with istihsan and with masalih mursala (public interests) is their lack of strict methodological controls that protect against errors in their application, making them suitable at times, and unsuitable at others! As for the times when they are suitable, you find them falling under the principles of usul al-fiqh (foundations of jurisprudence) in applying general rulings to their particulars, or expanding in ijtihad (independent juristic reasoning) by linking the issue being deliberated by the jurist to related issues, as Abu Hanifa did in the previous matter.
Critique of Imam al-Nabhani on the use of public interest (maslaha) in legal reasoning:
Similarly, when the Malikis employed public interest (maslaha murasala), they did not do so based on an independent rational conception, but rather on the basis that such interests fall under the general principles of the Sharia and are understood in light of its overarching objectives (maqasid al-sharia) established by the texts.
