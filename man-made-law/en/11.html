However, in modern times, we have begun to see the features of a method of thinking that defines public interest (maslaha) in general, specifically a rational approach detached from the legal evidence. That is, it does not attempt to derive the public interest from the legal evidence, but rather allows its intellect to determine specific benefits it hopes to achieve from an action, which it considers to be interests, and then it either manipulates the legal text, marginalizes it, or abrogates it in favor of this rational estimation.

The issue began - as the research will clarify - from the conception that there are interests (masalih) that the Sharia has neither acknowledged nor rejected, leading to the mistaken assumption that the Sharia was silent about them, meaning: without a direct text addressing their ruling. Therefore, it became permissible to exercise independent legal reasoning (ijtihad) regarding them based on principles stipulated by the later Malikis (after Ibn al-Arabi (d. 543 AH)) based on "the examination and induction of the objectives of revelation"(5) and based on "deriving rulings from established principles and recognized general legal rules"(6) (such as the principle of no harm and no reciprocating harm)(7), and based on "the absence of contradiction with a text or an established principle from revelation," which is known as the unregulated interests (masalih mursala)(8). The issue of unregulated interests was not known in the Maliki school before that; rather, it was attributed to them by examining their method of ijtihad, due to Imam Malik's inclination to consider meanings in the texts. (5) It is a condition that the public interest (maslaha) falls under the major objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia) (the preservation of religion, life, intellect, lineage, and property). Al-Shatibi (d. 790 AH) states: "Any interest that is not supported by the foundations of the Sharia in terms of consideration or cancellation is not considered... rather, it must be regulated under a general principle," al-Muwafaqat, vol. 2, p. 284. The Malikis derive the validity of public interest from a comprehensive view of the texts of the Sharia. Al-Tahir ibn Ashour clarifies: "The authority of the unregulated public interest is based on the complete induction of the objectives of the Sharia" (Usul al-Nizam al-Ijtima'i, p. 195). Ibn al-Qayyim (d. 751 AH) - who is influenced by the principles of the Malikis - stipulates: "Public interest is not considered unless it is connected to the objectives of the Sharia, not separate from them" (I'lam al-Muwaqqi'in, vol. 3, p. 14).

(6) The general legal principle (al-qaidah al-kulliyyah al-shar'iyyah) is a universal ruling under which specific cases fall; it is a general legal ruling (its meaning is universal) that serves as a foundation from which many rulings branch out, and the rulings derived from it are specific in relation to it. This principle is applicable in all areas of jurisprudence and is not limited to a specific chapter, unlike legal maxims.

(7) Ibn Ashur (d. 1393 AH) says: "The public interests (masalih mursala) are based on the general principles of the Sharia that have established the removal of hardship and the realization of benefit as a fundamental principle" (Maqasid al-Sharia al-Islamiyya, p. 122), and also on an accepted principle among them, which is: "Blocking the means (sadd al-dhara'i)." An example of this is the prohibition of selling weapons during a fitna (civil strife) (a public interest for them) based on the principle: "Everything that leads to fitna is prohibited," which is derived from the texts prohibiting fitna, and the principle: "The means to the unlawful is unlawful." (This reliance invalidates its status as being based on no evidence!) because this principle is based on a definitive Qur'anic origin: ﴾And do not insult those whom they invoke besides Allah, lest they insult Allah in enmity without knowledge﴿ [al-An'am 108], and cooperation in sin is prohibited due to His saying: ﴾And cooperate in righteousness and piety, but do not cooperate in sin and aggression﴿ [al-Ma'idah 2]. From here, it is strange to consider these rulings as "based on no evidence"!

(8) This is because the public interest (maslaha) in the unqualified (mursal) is "not a public interest that is left unaddressed, but rather a public interest that the Sharia has considered through a multitude of texts and a collection of evidence. The description by the scholars of usul (principles of jurisprudence) of the appropriate public interest as unqualified is merely a term intended to distinguish between unqualified reasoning and analogy (qiyas), as analogy has a specific origin that testifies to the particular public interest, while in unqualified reasoning there are non-specific origins, meaning that they do not testify to the particular public interest, although they do testify to its genus by consideration. Accordingly, it is necessary for the jurist (mujtahid) who claims that there is a public interest that necessitates giving a specific ruling to the incident to prove that this public interest is of a genus that the legislator (shari) has considered through legal texts, and to present the texts; otherwise, it is legislation based on opinion, which is a statement based on desire, and it is an innovation in religion. Whoever performs an action that is not in accordance with our command, it is rejected." (See: The Theory of Public Interest in Islamic Jurisprudence, pp. 65-66.)
