However, the real turning point occurred in the modern era, with the rise of so-called reformist and renewalist movements in the 19th and 20th centuries. In their struggle to confront underdevelopment and Western dominance, some scholars proposed a new approach: reinterpreting Sharia through its higher objectives rather than being bound by its specific textual appearances—supposedly to make it suitable for the modern age. Here, maṣlaḥah and maqāṣid became central.

This trend was led by figures like Jamāl al-Dīn al-Afghānī (d. 1897), his student Muhammad ʿAbduh (d. 1905), Rashīd Riḍā, and others. They promoted the slogan: “Islam is the religion of benefit,” reviving al-Ṭūfī’s thesis that if maṣlaḥah conflicts with a text, the maṣlaḥah should prevail. But they took it even further:

Muhammad ʿAbduh, for instance, applied maṣlaḥah to nearly all of his legal opinions, even if they contradicted established juristic consensus. He modified endowment laws in Egypt under claims of economic development (e.g., permitting the substitution of waqf assets and non-compliance with the donor’s conditions when deemed beneficial by state authorities).

He also permitted interest from government savings institutions under the justification that it served small depositors and stimulated economic growth. This ruling was widely viewed as legitimizing usury. ʿAbduh argued that only exploitative usury (usury in multiples) was prohibited in the texts, while “moderate” bank interest was a form of mutual financial cooperation.

Here we see a qualitative shift: maṣlaḥah in this new thought now meant any direct material benefit—even if it contradicted the clear text—on the assumption that texts could be historically contextualized and that religion must evolve with people’s changing needs. Maqāṣid al-sharīʿah was revived and reinterpreted in this context—not as a guide to understanding fixed laws, but as a license to revise them altogether.
