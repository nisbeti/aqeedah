The Sharīʿah is timeless—not because it changes, but because it was revealed in a universal framework that can address evolving conditions without distortion.
Their slogan: "Wherever there is benefit, there is the Sharīʿah." If the benefit is genuinely a Sharʿī-recognized benefit, affirmed by suitable evidence or sound principles, then yes—the Sharīʿah affirms it.
For example, beneficial knowledge, fulfilling people’s needs, and organized civic life are all supported by Sharʿī texts.
But if the claimed benefit is merely a worldly gain that contradicts divine commands, how can it be claimed to be God’s law? Suppose someone says: “It is in our national interest to form alliances with unbelieving nations, even if this leads to fighting other Muslims.” This is plainly false: the Sharīʿah strictly forbids allying with disbelievers against Muslims, regardless of how “beneficial” it may appear. Some even issued fatwas allowing alliances with occupying foreign powers against Muslim states under the guise of “national interest.” This is not from Sharīʿah, it reflects a lack of loyalty and disavowal rooted in divine commands. Final Summary:
The interest recognized in Sharīʿah is what constitutes real benefit for creation, and it is identified through revelation—either explicitly or through validated principles like effective suitability (al-munāsib al-muʾaththir). Granting unrestricted authority to human reason to define benefit without reference to the Qur’an and Sunnah is a methodological and theological deviation. It reduces the religion from divine revelation to subjective human speculation. This is precisely what the Prophet ﷺ warned about: “Beware of newly invented matters. Every innovation is misguidance.” (Sunan Abū Dāwūd, authentic). What is innovation (bidʿah)? Is it not legislating based on opinions and desires? Is it not implying that the Prophet ﷺ failed to convey the complete religion?
Just as there are heretical innovations in belief and worship, so too have innovations emerged in law and governance. These are promoted in our time under names like "Fiqh al-Wāqiʿ" or "Contemporary Jurisprudence," but in truth they are arbitrary human desires cloaked in attractive slogans. One final example: under the guise of benefit and personal rights, some—under Western liberal influence—have begun advocating the decriminalization of sins like drinking alcohol, claiming it harms only the individual.
