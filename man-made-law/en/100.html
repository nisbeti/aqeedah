An example: In his memoir “Political Memoirs” (p. 299, Egyptian General Book Organization, 1985), Muhammad ʿAlī ʿUlūbah (former Egyptian Minister of Justice) recounts that Shaykh al-Marāghī (Grand Shaykh of al-Azhar 1928–1930 and 1935–1945) once told lawmakers, “Bring me the Western law you wish to implement, and I will tell you which fiqh school supports it.”

Historical context: This occurred during the British occupation of Egypt, when lawmakers imported Western codes (especially French civil law).

al-Marāghī’s aim: He sought to reduce dependence on the West by providing nominal Islamic justification for these laws by:

Finding superficial resemblances between them and minority fiqh opinions.

Relying on the maxim: “Legal rulings change with changing times.”

Scholarly critique: Scholars like al-Ṭāhir ibn ʿĀshūr in “Maqāṣid al-Sharīʿa” (p. 367) described this as a “patchwork method” that begins with the secular law and then seeks a fiqh-based rationale for it. Ibn ʿĀshūr stated: “It is not valid to make reality the foundation and then subject the texts to it; rather, the texts are the foundation.”

Using Maqāṣid as a Pretext for Overriding Texts

The concept of maqāṣid (legal objectives) has been distorted from its role as a tool for understanding texts into a license for abandoning them. It is now invoked to justify ignoring categorical rulings, under the guise of serving “interests” or alleviating “hardship,” despite the consensus of usul scholars that maqāṣid may only be invoked in the absence of textual evidence—and never to contradict it.

Deviation in Applying Necessity and Hardship

Minority Jurisprudence has expanded the concept of ḍarūrah (necessity) beyond recognition. Even a doubtful necessity is now used to justify the permissibility of usury, the uncovering of private parts, or abandoning walāʾ and barāʾ (allegiance and disavowal). This represents a dangerous departure from the stringent criteria established in usul al-fiqh: that necessity must be real, certain, temporary, irremediable by other means, and strictly limited in scope.

Conclusion

The practice of “Minority Jurisprudence”—as undertaken by certain actors—reveals a deep methodological crisis. It entails dismantling the usulī architecture that governs ijtihād and replacing it with circumstantial rationalism that ultimately undermines the immutable foundations of the faith.
