For example, the story mentioned in the memoirs of Muhammad Ali Al-‘Aluba (former Egyptian Minister of Justice) in his book "Political Memories" (p. 299, Egyptian General Book Organization, 1985), where he quoted al-Maraghi (Sheikh of Al-Azhar 1928–1930 and 1935–1945) saying to the legislators: "Bring me the Western law you wish to apply, and I will tell you the legal school (madhhab) that can be relied upon!"

Historical background: During the British occupation of Egypt, Egyptian legislators imported Western laws (such as the French in civil law).

Al-Maraghi's goal: He sought to reduce dependence on the West by finding an Islamic legal basis – even if only formally – for these laws, through:

Searching for similarities between them and recognized (even if unusual) legal opinions.

Employing the principle: "The rulings change with the change of time."

Historical criticism: Scholars like al-Tahir ibn Ashour described it in "Maqasid al-Sharia" (p. 367) as a "composite" approach that starts from positive law and then seeks a legal justification for it, reversing the principle: The scholar Ibn Ashour said: "It is not correct to make reality the basis and then subject the texts to it; rather, the texts are the basis."

Reliance on "maqasid" statements in a way detached from their evidence:

The concept of "maqasid" has transformed from a criterion for understanding texts into a tool for undermining them, as it has been used as a justification for bypassing definitive texts and neglecting legal rulings, under the pretext of "achieving public interest (maslaha)" or "removing hardship," in blatant disregard for what the scholars of usul (principles of jurisprudence) have established that maqasid can only be invoked in the absence of text, and do not justify violating definitive rulings.

Deviation in dealing with "necessity" and "hardship":

The jurisprudence of minorities relies on an unregulated expansion of the concepts of necessity, to the extent that a "suspicion of necessity" is sufficient to justify the permissibility of usury or exposing nakedness or nullifying loyalty and disavowal, which is a serious deviation from the strict standards that have regulated necessities in the science of usul, in that they must be real, certain, temporary, cannot be avoided by other means, and must be measured according to their extent.

Summary:

The jurisprudence of minorities – as practiced by some entities – reflects a deep methodological crisis, rooted in the dismantling of the foundational structure governing ijtihad (independent juristic reasoning), and replacing it with rational and circumstantial estimates, ultimately leading to the undermining of the constants of religion. True legal renewal does not come from demolishing the rules, but from consciously applying them in reading reality and implementing the texts in a way that preserves the integrity of the Sharia and the interests of the Ummah.
