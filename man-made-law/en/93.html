Public interest in the jurisprudential heritage: from the constraints of the early scholars to the expansion of the later ones

As previously indicated, the early imams such as Abu Hanifa, Malik, al-Shafi'i, and Ahmad – despite their differing methodologies – did not deviate in their overall approach from the principle that the text is the foundation, and that the role of opinion and reason is to apply the text and implement it in relation to actual circumstances. Indeed, the Malikis referred to some juristic opinions as "unrestricted public interests" (masalih mursala), while the Hanafis termed some of them "istihsan" (meaning to prefer a hidden analogy over a clear analogy or a general text for the sake of public interest), and the Shafi'is and Hanbalis called some of them "analogy with an invalid difference" or "refining the legal context", etc. However, all agreed on framing public interest within the rules of Islamic law and the prohibition of establishing a completely new ruling that has no basis in texts, consensus, or sound analogy.

Some later Hanafis expanded slightly in istihsan and viewed it as not deviating from being "a specification of an analogy for a stronger evidence".

Later Malikis, such as al-Tufi (d. 716 AH), attempted to give public interest a special preference in cases of conflict, even stating his famous phrase: "If public interest conflicts with the text, public interest should prevail in transactions," but he restricted this by requiring that the text be of uncertain indication and that this public interest be clear and definitive. Nevertheless, his formulation was considered a slip and was rejected by the majority of scholars because it opens a dangerous door.

Al-Shatibi (d. 790 AH) in al-Muwafaqat presented a brilliant theory of the objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia), emphasizing that public interests do not exceed those five necessities and the preservation of their complements. He established a golden rule: "The true public interest is that which achieves the intent of the Legislator; thus, the servant has no public interest except in what aligns with obedience to Allah."

It can be said that until the end of the ninth century AH, the idea of public interest remained confined within scholarly frameworks and was not used to undermine a definitive principle or to justify transgressions. Then came the era of decline and colonization, and the influence of Sharia weakened in life, leading some scholars to feel the need to ease matters for people with fatwas that consider "public interests" under the pressure of difficult realities. For example, fatwas appeared permitting the temporary suspension of certain punishments under specific circumstances, such as the famous year of famine during the time of Umar (and this has a basis, as in the suspension of cutting off hands during the year of Ramada due to necessity).

The principles of necessity permitting the prohibited were sometimes used excessively during the colonial era under the pretext of considering the weakness of people or achieving the interest of averting greater harm.
