Rousseau expressed this reality brilliantly by saying: "Il faudrait des Dieux pour donner des lois aux hommes," meaning "We need gods to enact laws for people!"
And even in scientific fields that rely on experimentation and observation, such as medicine for example, humanity still knows very little about its body and its systems; how many diseases did doctors think were psychological only to later find out they were organic! And how many medications did they believe were beneficial, only to reveal their harm later after years of use! If a person does not have complete knowledge of their own body that is between their sides, nor of the physiology of the interaction between its organs and systems, how can they be confident in their understanding of society as a whole? And how can they legislate systems that govern relationships between individuals, or satisfy their instincts, or determine the values of good and evil, while they are incapable of grasping the deep network of interactions that link actions to their outcomes, values to their consequences, and legislations to their effects in the present and future? Furthermore, the issues that determine the validity of judgment regarding righteousness and goodness or their absence are complex, or known only relatively or incompletely to the mind, which will undoubtedly affect its judgments and its ability to weigh one against another. Perhaps a person may consider the ugly as beautiful, or turn away from an action that contains their benefit to its opposite for the reasons previously mentioned. And perhaps they may prioritize one value at the expense of another, thus nullifying the objectives and goals.
Human reality is not subject solely to mechanical calculation; rather, psychological, spiritual, and emotional factors intertwine within it. These are matters that the mind alone cannot measure accurately or base universal judgments upon. Moreover, these factors vary from one individual to another and from one environment to another, which makes the construction of general rational legislations highly contradictory and fragmented.
This real shortcoming has resulted in two serious matters:
The first: reliance on experimentation in legislation: laws are enacted, then their effects are monitored; if they appear to succeed, the state retains them, and if they lead to disasters, they are abolished after the harm has occurred. We have seen this in the legalization of drugs such as marijuana, or in the imposition of educational curricula that promote atheism or deviance, only to later reveal the devastating psychological and social effects they have caused.
