Introduction

This is an extensive scholarly research on the principles of Islamic jurisprudence that discusses the reference of legislation between reason and revelation in Islamic thought. It is a fundamental topic and a delicate issue that should only be approached with utmost precision in its foundational principles and awareness of methodological errors to avoid them.
This research aims to critique the idea that human reason can possess the authority of legislation or serve as a reference for judging actions, whether in describing them as good or bad, or beneficial or harmful, or in terms of reward and punishment, or in determining public interest (maslaha), or regarding whether the rational judgment on actions and relationships achieves benefit or averts harm, and thus to subject the legal ruling to this rational estimation.
Some Islamic jurisprudential schools in the past(1) directed themselves towards inferring the objectives of Islamic law at the level of the Sharia as a whole. The principles of jurisprudence have established a set of general rules either from the reasons scattered in a collection of texts that converge on those rules or from those general rulings under which a group of particulars can be categorized. They have thus codified those general rules that are suitable for use in all areas of jurisprudence, not limited to a specific chapter like jurisprudential rules. Some schools also attempted to derive the goals, wisdoms, and interests at the level of detailed evidence, estimating behind some detailed legal evidences what connects them to interests achieved by applying these evidences in reality. They said: The generality of the Sharia came to bring about benefits and to avert harms(2), and they established that the Sharia only came in its rulings with what is beneficial(3), meaning that behind the application of legal rulings derived from detailed evidences are interests that are realized for creation or benefits that they bring, (not necessarily material ones only). (1) Imam al-Juwayni (d. 478 AH) and his student Imam al-Ghazali (d. 505 AH) from the Shafi'i school inferred the objectives of the Sharia, and Imam al-Shatibi followed them, identifying five necessary objectives: the preservation of life, property, religion, intellect, and lineage. Imam Taqi al-Din al-Nabhani added to them the objective of preserving security, the objective of preserving the state, and the objective of preserving human dignity, inferring them through the severity of punishment on the perpetrator of a crime that violates them legally. The one who rebels against the state with arms is fought, and whoever breaks the pledge of obedience and is pledged as a second caliph is killed, among other rulings and details that can be reviewed in the book "The Islamic Personality," part three: (Principles of Jurisprudence) by Taqi al-Din al-Nabhani. Thus, the necessary objectives became eight, and by examining the evidences, we found that establishing justice, enforcing the Sharia, worship, guidance, clarifying rulings, and mercy are general objectives for which the Book was revealed and the Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him) was sent, and neglecting them leads to disbelief, tyranny, oppression, or immorality and severe punishments! Thus, the objectives of the Sharia became fourteen.
(2) The book "Rawdat al-Nazir" by Ibn Qudamah (d. 620 AH) is considered the earliest known foundational source that explicitly formulated the phrase as it is commonly circulated today, while the formulation of al-Izz ibn Abd al-Salam in "Qawa'id al-Ahkam" (d. 660 AH) was more comprehensive and influential.
(3) Imam al-Shafi'i mentioned in "Al-Risalah" that "the rulings of the Sharia take into account the interests of the servants," and it was reported from Imam Ahmad in "Masail Imam Ahmad" by Abu Dawood that he said: "The Sharia is based on interests." The first to formulate the idea that the Sharia came to bring benefits and avert harms was the judge Abd al-Jabbar al-Hamadhani (d. 415 AH) in his explanation of the five principles, but he formulated it in the context of a discussion on the theory of rational good and evil. The first to establish the concept in a purely foundational context was Imam al-Haramayn al-Juwayni (d. 478 AH) in "Al-Burhan fi Usul al-Fiqh," where he said: "The Sharia is a consideration for the interests of creation," but he did not mention averting harms.
