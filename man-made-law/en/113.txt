Ninth: Coercion to resort to secular courts in marital disputes and recognition of their absolute authority:
The established legal ruling: It is obligatory to refer to the Sharia of Allah. Allah, Exalted is He, said: "But no, by your Lord, they will not truly believe until they make you judge in that which they dispute among themselves" {Quran 4:65}. It is forbidden to refer to the tyrant (taghut). Allah, Exalted is He, said: "Have you not seen those who claim to believe in what has been revealed to you and what was revealed before you? They wish to refer to the taghut while they were commanded to disbelieve in it" {Quran 4:60}. There is consensus on the prohibition of referring to other than the Sharia of Allah in matters that contradict an established legal ruling.
The position of "minority jurisprudence" that concedes: Some fatwas and positions implicitly or explicitly encourage acceptance of the inevitability of resorting to Western courts in all marital disputes (sometimes inheritance) without making a genuine effort to apply the legal rulings through Islamic arbitration or seeking recognition of the system (except in a very limited scope). The focus is on "benefiting from the system" and "gaining rights" regardless of the source of the ruling.
Their justifications based on public interest (maslaha):
The inevitability of the supremacy of secular law in Western countries and the lack of a complete practical alternative.
Protecting the rights of the weaker party (often the woman) that may be lost if she does not resort to the courts.
Achieving stability and integration and not creating conflict with the system.
Critique of systematic concession:
Neglecting a fundamental contractual principle: which is the obligation to refer to the Sharia of Allah and the rejection of referring to the taghut. Acknowledging the authority of secular courts to settle disputes is an acknowledgment of their superior legitimacy in organizing the lives of Muslims, which contradicts the methodology of loyalty and disavowal.
Pure rational interest: Focusing on "gaining material rights" or "worldly stability" while neglecting the greater right, which is adherence to the Sharia of Allah and not recognizing the legitimacy of what contradicts it. The interest here is purely worldly, separating religion from practical life.
Acceptance of reality instead of changing it or adapting to it within constants: Instead of making a serious effort to develop legally recognized Islamic arbitration mechanisms or calling for amendments that allow for the application of personal status rulings for Muslims (as in the personal status cases of Jews, for example), reality is accepted as a final axiom.
Emptying legal contracts of their content: If the contract (marriage) is legitimate, but the resolution of the dispute is done outside the Sharia of Allah, then this contradicts the essence of the contract.
