“Pure reason does not contradict authentic transmission. Rather, it attests to and confirms it… Yes, there may be in the texts what reason cannot comprehend, but not what it deems impossible.” Many religious matters surpass human comprehension but do not contradict it. The claim that “pure reason never contradicts authentic transmission” is itself false, having no basis in intellect or revelation. Reason simply does not have that role in Islam—except in theological proofs based on rational evidence, as previously discussed. Sharīʿah has unambiguously declared that revelation is the sole source of legislation—not reason. As Allah says:
“Follow what has been revealed to you from your Lord, and do not follow besides Him any allies. Little do you remember.” (al-Aʿrāf: 3), So following revelation through the intellect’s effort to derive meaning from the texts is the desired path. The wisdom behind Sharīʿah and the rationale for its rulings is too vast for individuals to grasp independently. Thus, God preserved the objectives of His law by sending messengers. Our role is to understand those objectives and act upon them—not to invent new objectives from our own minds. Thus, the statements of the Salaf are numerous in warning against using pure reason in religious matters. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib (may God be pleased with him) said: “Had religion been based on opinion, the bottom of the leather sock would have been more deserving of being wiped than the top. But I saw the Messenger of Allah ﷺ wipe the top of his socks.”(Related by Abū Dāwūd with a good chain of narration.) This means that the devotional nature of legal rulings may sometimes go against what intellect alone deems logical serving as a test of obedience and a check on intellectual arrogance. Intellect might say that wiping the bottom of the sock cleans better, but the Sharīʿah commanded wiping the top as a symbol of the comprehensiveness of ritual purity. The believer obeys. Thus, our scholars have always said: “Ijtihād is the exertion of effort to seek a probable ruling from the Sharīʿah”—not to create a ruling.
This is explicit in the hadith of Muʿādh ibn Jabal: “I will exert my reasoning and spare no effort.” He did not say: “I will legislate by my opinion,” but that he would search diligently within the revealed texts, and if he did not find a direct text, he would apply analogy (qiyās).
