Legitimacy may be disregarded or suspended on the pretext that it does not suit the times. They support this with the principle: "Custom is authoritative," and sometimes they elevate it to a foundational principle that takes precedence over texts.

However, the origin of this principle in the books of usul (principles of jurisprudence) was not intended to change established legal rulings, but was confined to matters of ijtihad (juridical reasoning) that are speculative and related to customs, such as oaths, prices, claims, and bequests, where the legislator has stipulated that custom is to be considered above all else.

Secondly: The usuli (jurisprudential) response to the principle

The legal ruling does not change because it is related to the address of the legislator and not to reality.

The legal ruling is related to the act as it is, and it has a reason that is defined by a text, and it is not reality that creates the ruling.

Indeed, the diversity of occurrences necessitates a new application of the established ruling, not its change, as in the differing circumstances of divorce, fasting, or transactions; this is called the realization of the relevant criteria (tahqiq al-manat), not a change in the ruling.

The difference between the change of fatwa (legal opinion) and the change of ruling

The legal ruling is a universal constant, while the fatwa is the application of the ruling to a specific incident.

What changes in the fatwa is the reality of the incident, not the text of the ruling, nor the methodology of derivation.

If the understanding of the mujtahid (jurist) changes, or new evidence appears to him, then his changed ijtihad does not mean a change in the ruling due to time or place, but rather due to the emergence of evidence or a new preference.

Thirdly: Proving that the rulings are valid for all times and places

The Qur'an was revealed in a language that carries general, comprehensive meanings, and is suitable for derivation in every era without alteration or distortion.

The rulings are applicable to new incidents using the same established usuli methodology, and not by introducing foreign elements such as environment or custom in place of the text.

Fourthly: Clarifying the danger of the principle in undermining legislation

The claim that time and place change the rulings implies that revelation was specific to the time of the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him), and that legislation today must rely on human reason.

This is a dangerous entry point that transforms reason into a legislator, which contradicts the essence of Islam: that judgment belongs solely to Allah.

Building legislation on claimed interests, or on a changing reality, is a substitution of Western legislations for Sharia, as seen in their positions on usury, the limit of theft, apostasy, and women.
