Chapter Three: The evolution of the concept of interest (maslaha) in jurisprudence and the danger of its modern use in what they call: "the jurisprudence of minorities," "the jurisprudence of balances," and "the jurisprudence of reality." We briefly review the historical development of the employment of the idea of "interest": from its limited and regulated consideration by the imams such as Malik and al-Ghazali, based on revelation, to its theoretical expansion during the periods of decline, reaching our era where trends such as "the jurisprudence of minorities," "the jurisprudence of balances," and "the jurisprudence of reality" emerged, relying on reason. We will analyze the dangers of using the concept of interest in these modern contexts, where it may be used as a pretext to bypass definitive texts or to change legal rulings under the pretexts of facilitation or necessity, leading to serious deviations in religious rulings, such as the claim of the change of definitive rulings or the change of rulings with the change of time and place.

Chapter Four: Reason in the theory of knowledge and the methodology of legal reasoning. We rely on the book "Theory of Knowledge and Methods of Thinking and Reasoning" by Thaer Salama and the section on "Ijtihad and Taqlid" by Thaer Salama to establish that reason is a tool for understanding the text and deriving legal rulings, not an independent source of legislation. In this chapter, we highlight the limits of the role of reason in Islam: reason is the basis of obligation and through it revelation is understood, but it is revelation that illuminates the path and guides it to the legal rulings derived from the discourse of revelation, and to the correct objectives, and it is for reason to deduce from the Sharia to arrive at an understanding of its method in achieving certain objectives, or specific societal customs it aims to realize, or general principles it can apply and utilize, or rulings and interests that its application achieves, all of which is done while drawing from the pure Sharia using its intellect for understanding and contemplation. We also address the methodology of correct ijtihad as established by scholars, which is the effort to understand and apply the legal evidence, not to deviate from it based on mere opinion.

Chapter Five: Modern rationalist schools from the era of Abduh and al-Afghani to the present. We critically analyze the emergence of the rationalist trend in the modern era led by Jamal al-Din al-Afghani and his student Muhammad Abduh, then Muhammad Rashid Rida, reaching contemporary figures who raised the banner of religious renewal by placing interest (or rational objectives as they understand them) above the texts of Sharia. We will present clear examples of their interpretations: such as the fatwas of Muhammad Abduh where he considered "interest" even if it contradicted the consensus of the jurists, including his famous fatwa permitting bank interest (usury) under the pretext of public interest. We will also address those who came after him among the advocates of what is called "renewing religious discourse" who justified the suspension of legal penalties or changing family rulings under the pretext of achieving the objectives of Sharia or keeping pace with development. The aim of this chapter is to expose the contradiction in the methodology of these individuals: they call for the authority of reason and interest, but the result has been a deviation from the constants of Sharia and opening the door to human desires.
