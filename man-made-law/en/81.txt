That no text or consensus contradicts it.
If it contradicts a legal text or a clear consensus, it is void as a cause.
That it is consistent.
That is, whenever the cause is present the ruling is present, and whenever it is absent the ruling is absent.
That it is transitive, not restricted to one case.
A cause may not be confined to a single instance.
It must extend to other cases for analogy upon it to be valid.
That a legal proof establishes it.
A cause is not established by reason or by public interest alone, but must rest on the Book, the Sunnah or the consensus of the Companions.
That it is not itself another legal ruling.
A legal ruling is not given a reason by a legal ruling like it, because that leads either to circularity or to a description unfit to be a motive, as in saying: the reason for cutting off the hand is the cutting off of the hand.
C. Strict controls:
Rejecting the “imagined cause” or the “wisdom that may fail to hold” as an objective, just as public interest left unrestricted (maslaha mursala) is rejected as an independent source of law.
D. An applied example:
The prohibition of hiring at the time of the Friday call to prayer, by analogy with the prohibition of selling, for the reason of “distraction from prayer.”
The legal determinants that describe the universals under which the particulars fall:
In the process of ijtihad, the jurist studies reality in order to understand it accurately, and this is a purely rational inquiry. Then he studies the legal determinants that describe the universals under which this reality can fall, in order to bring to mind the controls it may come under, such as: what are the determinants and controls that define the concept of intoxicating wine, and distinguish it from a sedative, from vinegar, or from an anaesthetic used in medicine? Then the ruling on wine is applied to the reality that contains those determinants, so it is said, for example: whisky is wine, so it is forbidden, in small and large amounts.
