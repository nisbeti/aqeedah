The established principles of preference lead to a reversal of rulings, making the certain (qat'i) dependent on the speculative (zanni), and the legal (shar'i) dependent on the political, which is a methodical deviation of great danger.

The alternative principled approach is based on a comprehensive epistemological system, which makes the criterion for preference the strength of the evidence, not the predominance of interest (maslaha), and considers the proper application (husn al-tanzil) a result of sound understanding and control, not a result of concessions or adaptation to reality. In this way, fatwa (legal opinion) regains its scholarly status as an extension of prophethood, not as a tool for passing policies.

Ijtihad (independent juristic reasoning) can only occur by presenting the strongest evidence, not the closest interest, and the principled methodology in weighing detailed evidences represents the cornerstone for restoring the authority of the text, adhering to revelation, and neutralizing desires, and it is considered a solid legal alternative that suffices against the "jurisprudence of balances" based on subjective estimates.

Other angles for establishing balances from a principled perspective:

First: Criteria for balancing between concession (rukhsah) and obligation (azimah)

Concession is not merely a "facilitation," but an exceptional legal ruling based on a valid excuse, and it is permissible to act upon it under conditions including:

The existence of a valid legal excuse: such as coercion, illness, and travel.

The obligation does not completely cease: the original ruling remains, and the concession alleviates hardship but does not lift the obligation.

Concessions should not be used as a pretext to violate the objectives (maqasid) or to suspend the law.

Second: The principle of necessity (darurah) and its criteria

Necessity is to be assessed according to its measure, and its scope is narrow:

Nothing that is prohibited in itself is permitted except to the extent of averting harm.

Necessity should not be used as a pretext to violate certain rulings or to suspend the law.

General rulings should not be established based on rulings of necessity.

“Necessity does not invalidate a rule, nor does it create a ruling, but it is given an exceptional ruling in its circumstance.”

Third: Choosing between two prohibitions (the principle of the lesser of two evils) or averting the greater of two harms by committing the lesser of them, provided that:

Both harms are actualized, not hypothetical.

The choice is limited to them, and there is no third option.

The choice is of lesser harm and less violation of legal rulings.

The aim of this type of balancing is:

“Committing the lesser of two harms when they conflict, while adhering to the permissible extent and refraining from expansion without limits.”
