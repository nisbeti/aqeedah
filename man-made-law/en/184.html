The crux of the matter in this context is that the modern rationalist school became associated with new and controversial relationships and alliances - such as the connection with Freemasonry and following the path of modern political projects. Their declared goal was to serve Islam and Muslims through these means, but many saw this as a significant danger. For Freemasonry and other dubious organizations exploited some of these individuals to achieve their own ends and then discarded them. Likewise, nationalism and liberalism, when pushed to their limits, resulted in Westernizing movements that removed religion from the center of public life. Therefore, Islamic critics (such as Muhammad Hussein, al-Nadwi, and al-Jundi) consider this phase pivotal in the penetration of Western thought into Muslim societies from within, through figures who raised the banner of religious reform while - consciously or unconsciously - dismantling the old structure to prepare for a new construction aligned with the Western model.

The impact on the Egyptian intellectual elite and modern Islamic currents

The school of Afghani and Abduh had a profound effect on the elite of thinkers in Egypt in the early twentieth century, and subsequently on the currents of modern Islamic thought as a whole. A number of prominent figures of that era were directly influenced by or studied under Muhammad Abduh: it suffices to mention Saad Zaghloul (leader of the 1919 Revolution and later Prime Minister of Egypt), Ahmad Lutfi al-Sayyid (the father of Egyptian liberalism and a pioneer of nationalism), Qasim Amin (a pioneer of women's liberation), and Abd al-Rahman al-Kawakibi (a thinker of the Renaissance and author of The Nature of Despotism), and even the poet Ahmad Shawqi in some aspects of his thought. In the larger Islamic East, we find the influence of Afghani extending to the Indian subcontinent through his student, the poet of Islam Muhammad Iqbal, and to the Levant through many figures, including the Mufti of Syria, Sheikh Taha al-Fayyad, and others.

Dr. Ghazi al-Touba notes that Muhammad Abduh left clear marks on the formation of the Egyptian cultural elite that emerged after him. He mentions among them: Qasim Amin, Saad Zaghloul, Ahmad Lutfi al-Sayyid, Taha Hussein, and Abbas Mahmoud al-Aqqad. These either studied directly under Abduh (like Saad and Lutfi) or were influenced by his ideas through readings and intermediaries (like Taha Hussein and al-Aqqad). They each followed the modernization project in their respective fields: Saad Zaghloul led a liberal project to politically and socially liberate Egypt (although he was educated in al-Azhar, he adopted a civil approach that practically separated religion from politics). Lutfi al-Sayyid established the concepts of regional nationalism and cultural secularism in Egypt. Taha Hussein developed historical criticism of texts to the extent of questioning some Qur'anic narratives (such as the story of Ibrahim).
