In one narration: "The Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) flogged forty, and Abu Bakr flogged forty, and Umar flogged eighty, and each was a year, and this is more beloved to me."

This indicates that the increase is not a new legislation based on public interest (maslaha), but rather a permissible punishment within the upper limit.

The scholars differentiated between the fixed prescribed limit and what is left to the discretion of the ruler in repeated cases, and when the consumption of alcohol became widespread, Umar punished with eighty as a severe punishment.

In summary: the increase is a matter of permissible punishment within the texts, not a new legislation based on public interest.

Note: The usul scholars differed on whether the punishment for drinking alcohol is a prescribed limit or a discretionary punishment, with two opinions. The evidence for those who considered it a discretionary punishment is the hadith: "Whoever drinks alcohol, flog him; if he returns, flog him; if he returns, flog him; then kill him" (narrated by Ahmad and Abu Dawood). They said: If it were a prescribed limit, it would not exceed three, so it is a discretionary punishment that is intensified according to repetition. On one hand, and on the other hand, they relied on it as a discretionary punishment through studying the nature of the text that explicitly states the punishment as a limit. On a third hand, Umar ibn al-Khattab and Ali ibn Abi Talib compared drinking alcohol to slander, as drinking alcohol leads to delirium and falsehood, so they prescribed it the limit of slander (narrated by al-Bayhaqi and others). Thus, the increase is a result of slander that exceeds mere drinking of alcohol.

In all these examples, even among those who exerted effort in using public interests, we notice that the rulings were not established from abstract rational concepts, but rather from efforts that apply the general legal principles to the specific incidents. The reference is always revelation, and public interest does not stand alone in legislation, but is extracted from the objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia) and its derived indications. The fundamental error is considering these issues as independent of evidence when it has been established that they are not.

The conclusion: there is no legislative reason, but rather a reason that serves revelation:

Neglecting the general principles and objectives in ijtihad may lead to incomplete rulings. However, linking the specific cases to their general principles, and observing what the overall evidence has established from general rules – such as justice, alleviating hardship, and preserving necessities – produces a ruling that is more precise and more aligned with the intent of the legislator.

This is the methodology of the imams of ijtihad: they did not separate public interest from the text, nor did they rely on reason as a legislative source, but rather considered reason a tool for understanding revelation and utilizing its indications. Their attempts were not aimed at establishing "what reason sees as beneficial," but rather clarifying "what the law intended from the outset."

It is worth noting that applying general rulings to their parts and studying the meanings indicated by the texts, and the inductive reasoning and methodologies that were manifested in the Maliki school when public interests were utilized, are accepted methodologies, even if some of their branches are incorrect, for the reasons we presented in the previous chapter.
