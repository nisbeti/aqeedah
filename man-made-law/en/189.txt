As for Anwar al-Jundi (1917–2002), who was a researcher in modern Islamic thought, he dedicated dozens of books and articles to refuting the claims of the Westernizers. In his book "A'lam wa Mawaqif" and others, he addressed the personalities of Jamal al-Din al-Afghani and Muhammad Abduh. Al-Jundi wrote about the role of Freemasonry and Orientalism in dismantling the Ottoman Caliphate and encouraging narrow nationalist movements, hinting that colonialism would not have succeeded without the contribution of some Easterners – whether in good faith or ill – in executing its intellectual plans. Generally, Anwar al-Jundi viewed Muhammad Abduh as a "teacher" for an entire generation of writers who gradually distanced themselves from true Islam. He was the head of the "Arab Rationalists" school, which included Shibli Shumail, Farah Anton, Salama Musa, and Taha Hussein… and these were either outright atheists or extremely secular. Although Abduh himself remained committed to the fundamentals of faith, his opening of the door to interpretation and rationalism without limits benefited others who exceeded what he would have accepted. Here lies the danger.
Similarly, the scholar Abu al-Hasan al-Nadwi (1913–1999) in India had a firm stance. In his famous book "What Did the World Lose by the Decline of Muslims?", he criticized the approach taken by some advocates of revival in the Islamic world by imitating the West. He pointed to the modernist trend in Egypt and India, naming some figures like Sir Syed Ahmad Khan (the equivalent of Abduh in India) and how a generation dazzled by the West emerged from under his cloak, leading reform down perilous paths. Al-Nadwi emphasized the necessity of distinguishing between praiseworthy change and blameworthy change, and he mentioned that many of the transformations desired by the so-called renewers were, in reality, destructive to the authentic Islamic spirit, even if they appeared to be progress. In this, he agrees with Dr. Hussein in essence. Al-Nadwi also praised, in other places, the works of the righteous scholars who confronted these waves and revived pure faith without excess or negligence – such as the efforts of Sheikh Mahmoud Hassan and Hussain Ahmad Madani in India, who led liberation movements without compromising on religion, or the efforts of Ibn Badis and al-Ibrahimi in Algeria, who resisted colonialism culturally without making concessions on the fundamentals. The comparison is clear between the conservative reformist line led by these figures and the rational reformist line led by al-Afghani and Abduh in the East. Al-Nadwi favored the conservative approach because it preserved the authenticity of the reference while striving for wise renewal.
It is also necessary to mention Sayyid Qutb's (1906–1966) critique of this trend – he who experienced the liberal current and then turned against it after his guidance. In his book "Ma'alim fi al-Tariq" and others, Sayyid Qutb speaks of a "psychological defeat" among some intellectuals in the face of Western civilization, which led them to attempt to twist Islam to conform to material civilization, seeing this as a great danger. Sayyid Qutb clearly refers here to the trend of Abduh and those like him, even if he does not name individuals. Likewise, Malek Bennabi (the Algerian thinker 1905–1973) wrote about the necessity of the intellectual independence of Muslims and the avoidance of dissolving it in dependence on the West, and this can be considered an indirect critique of the rationalist school that attempted to embellish Islam to fit European standards.
