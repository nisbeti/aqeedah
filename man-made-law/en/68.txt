Summary:
All these Maliki rulings that are sometimes thought to be based on "maslaha mursala" (public interest) are, in fact, derived from the texts of revelation, either through the objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia) established by the induction of the law, or through valid analogy based on reasons taken from the texts. Thus, the concept of maslaha for the Malikis is not purely rational, but is regulated by the Sharia, connected to it in its construction and reasoning. This clearly demonstrates that reason in the Maliki school serves revelation and is not a legislator independent of it.
Maslaha in the jurisprudential schools (Hanafi and Maliki): Complements revelation, not based on reason
Many people believe that istihsan (juridical preference) according to Abu Hanifa, or the application of maslaha mursala among the Malikis, represents a rational approach outside the framework of the legal texts, and that the jurist in these matters relies on maslaha as perceived by his intellect, thereby altering the legal ruling or establishing a new ruling that has no basis in the Sharia. This is a mistaken idea that contradicts the methodology of the imams and casts doubt on their jurisprudence regarding the rational legislation we warn against, even though these principles have often been misapplied due to a lack of methodological controls that protect against error in their use.
The correct view, as indicated by the induction of their juristic efforts, is that the jurists among the imams of jurisprudence did not grant reason the authority to legislate, but rather saw that rulings are derived only from revelation: either directly or through deduction from its principles and universals. As for reason, its role is to understand the implications of the texts and to deduce what is not explicitly stated, within the limits extracted from the evidences of the Sharia, not from an independent rational authority.
Thus, ijtihad (independent juristic reasoning) is not about extracting a ruling from abstract reason, but is an endeavor to discover what is indicated by revelation, by linking particulars to their universals, branches to their roots, and texts to their objectives. If it appears that analogy or the apparent meaning leads to a certain ruling, but this ruling results in a harm similar to what the Sharia has prohibited in another context, then deviating from this analogy or apparent meaning—such as in istihsan or the application of maslaha mursala—is not based on whim, but rather an attempt to reach a deeper understanding of the structure of the Sharia and its overall implications, even if this methodology is not always correct.
Illustrative example: Reviving dead land and the condition of the imam's permission:
In the hadith of the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him): "Whoever revives dead land, it is his," the apparent meaning of the hadith indicates that reviving the land is a sufficient cause for owning it. However, Imam Abu Hanifa conditioned the permission of the imam in this matter, stating: "Whoever revives dead land without the permission of the imam, it does not belong to him, and the imam has the right to take it from him." His student Abu Yusuf was asked: "What led Abu Hanifa to oppose the hadith?" He clarified that Imam Abu Hanifa did not disregard the text, but rather saw that the unrestricted application of it in the context of an organized state would lead to chaos in the distribution of land and harm to the public good, which contradicts the objectives of the Sharia in preserving order and justice.
