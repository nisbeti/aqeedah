Conclusion
All of these Mālekī rulings—though often assumed to be based on maṣlaḥah mursalah—are in reality derived from the revealed texts: either through inductively derived maqāṣid of the Sharīʿah or valid analogical reasoning grounded in textual causes (ʿilal). Thus, for the Mālekīs, “interest” is not a purely rational construct but a Sharīʿah-bound principle, anchored to revelation both in its formulation and application.
This demonstrates clearly that reason in the Mālekī school is a servant of revelation—not a legislator alongside it.
Maṣlaḥah in the Doctrinal Schools (Ḥanafī and Mālekī): A Complement to Revelation, Not a Foundation for Independent Reason
Many people mistakenly believe that istiḥsān (juridical preference) in the Ḥanafī school, or maṣlaḥah mursalah (unrestricted public interest) in the Mālekī school, represents a rationalist tendency that falls outside the framework of the revealed texts. They assume that the mujtahid (jurist) relies on personal judgment to determine what is beneficial, thereby altering the Sharīʿah ruling or creating a new one not rooted in revelation. This is a mistaken view that contradicts the methodology of the founding Imams and casts suspicion upon their jurisprudence, suggesting that it was based on independent rational legislation—something we firmly reject. That said, these legal tools have indeed been misused at times due to a lack of methodological safeguards to prevent error.
The correct view—confirmed through inductive analysis of their juristic efforts—is that the jurists of the classical schools never granted the intellect legislative authority. Rather, they held that rulings are to be derived solely from revelation, either explicitly through texts or implicitly through derivation from the Sharīʿah’s general principles. The function of the intellect is thus to understand the meaning of the texts and to extrapolate rulings for cases not explicitly mentioned, and this process must remain within the parameters extracted from Sharīʿah evidence—not from a freestanding rational authority.
Thus, ijtihād is not the act of deriving rulings from pure
reason, but a diligent effort to discover what the revelation implies by connecting particulars to general principles, branches to their roots, and texts to their underlying purposes. If a case arises in which qiyās (analogy) or a literal reading appears to indicate a certain ruling, but applying that ruling leads to a harm of the same type that the Sharīʿah has prohibited elsewhere, then abandoning that literal ruling—whether through istiḥsān or maṣlaḥah mursalah, is not an appeal to whim. Rather, it is a deeper attempt to apprehend the Sharīʿah’s structural coherence and comprehensive aims. Admittedly, this approach is not always executed flawlessly.
