At the beginning of the 21st century, a group of writers and intellectuals emerged who were trained under the guidance of American institutions seeking to fund “moderate Islamic networks.” These individuals adopted Western modernist values in full and sought to reinterpret Islam accordingly. Among them are Muhammad Shaḥrūr in the Levant, Ḥasan Ḥanafī in Egypt, and ʿAbd al-Majīd al-Sharfī in Tunisia, among others. While they do not trace their intellectual roots directly to ʿAbduh or Rashīd Riḍā, they are heavily influenced by Western philosophical thought. However, a close analysis of their ideas reveals a clear continuity with the rationalist reformist mindset: namely, the re-reading of sacred texts to align with modern values (democracy, secularism, human rights in the Western sense). Examples include:

Denying the classical division of the world into Dār al-Islām and Dār al-Kufr. Reinterpreting jihād as purely defensive. Implicitly or explicitly accepting the separation of religion and state, claiming “Islam is a religion, not a state.”

Calling for the abolition of fixed punishments (ḥudūd), claiming they are unsuitable for the modern era. All of this echoes the proposals of ʿAlī ʿAbd al-Rāziq and Ṭāhā Ḥusayn, but now couched in more academic language. These thinkers are, without doubt, ideological heirs of the rationalist school—even if they do not openly acknowledge the lineage—because the core principle remains the same: subjecting divine revelation to the standards of modernity.

It becomes evident from all of the above that the intellectual legacy of the rationalist reformist school continues to shape contemporary thought in diverse forms. Despite periods of decline—such as the mid-twentieth century—the school experienced a powerful resurgence after the 1980s. It remains deeply entrenched in thought patterns promoted and funded by the West, with open media platforms provided to propagate it as a means of undermining Islam “from within.” The gravest consequence, as previously illustrated, is the undermining of certainty in revelation as the supreme authority, and the replacement of divine standards with modern secular rationalism and Western legal systems.
