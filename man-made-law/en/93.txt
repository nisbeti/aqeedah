Maṣlaḥah in Classical Jurisprudence: From Strict Regulation to Late Expansion
As previously discussed, the early imams—Abū Ḥanīfa, Mālek, al-Shāfiʿī, and Aḥmad—though differing in methodology, consistently upheld the primacy of textual evidence. Reason and opinion were used to apply and understand the text—not to override it. Indeed, the Mālekīs referred to certain forms of reasoning as "maṣāliḥ mursalah" (unbound interests), the Ḥanafīs used "istiḥsān" (juristic preference) to depart from clear analogies for stronger evidence or broader utility, and the Shāfiʿīs and Ḥanbalīs employed methods like "refined cause identification" or "analogy with eliminated difference." Despite terminological differences, all schools agreed that maṣlaḥah must be regulated by Sharʿī principles and that entirely novel rulings lacking support in texts, consensus, or sound analogy were impermissible.

Certain late Ḥanafīs expanded istiḥsān slightly, framing it as the prioritization of a stronger legal principle over strict analogy.
Some later Mālekīs, like al-Ṭūfī (d. 716 AH), attempted to grant maṣlaḥah special precedence in cases of conflict, famously stating: “If maṣlaḥah conflicts with a text, maṣlaḥah takes precedence in transactions.” He, however, restricted this to texts of probabilistic meaning and cases involving clear, overwhelming benefits. Nonetheless, the scholarly majority rejected this formulation as dangerously open-ended.
Al-Shāṭibī (d. 790 AH), in his seminal work al-Muwāfaqāt, provided a brilliant theoretical framework for the maqāṣid al-sharīʿah (objectives of Sharia). He emphasized that real interests are confined to the five essentials and their complements. He famously declared: “True benefit is that which aligns with the will of the Lawgiver—man has no benefit except in obeying God.”
Up until the end of the 9th century AH, maṣlaḥah remained within scholarly constraints and was not invoked to justify undermining definitive texts or to authorize egregious violations. Then came the era of decline and colonization, during which the influence of Sharia diminished in public life. Some scholars, facing harsh realities, began issuing fatwas accommodating "interests" to alleviate hardship. This marked the beginning of expansion:
Fatwas appeared temporarily suspending certain ḥudūd punishments due to dire conditions, such as the famous famine during ʿUmar’s caliphate when he suspended amputation for theft out of necessity.
The legal maxim "necessities permit prohibitions" was used—at times excessively—during colonial times to justify accommodations under claims of necessity or greater harm prevention.
