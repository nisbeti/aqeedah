The Sharia (Islamic law) has recognized certain matters as valid, from which rulings can be derived through analogy (qiyas) based on the rationale of the text or consensus (ijma). The second category includes those matters that the Sharia has declared invalid, such as the invalidity of a mufti exceeding the prescribed order of expiation for someone who is able to free a slave when he has relations with his spouse during Ramadan, by moving to fasting for sixty days, in order to deter him, under the assumption that he might take the violation lightly each time. This is contrary to the order stipulated in the verse and the hadith as well; thus, this leap constitutes a benefit (maslaha) that the Sharia has ruled to be invalid. The third category includes matters that the Sharia has neither declared invalid nor recognized as valid in specific detailed evidence related to them, neither in their essence nor in their type; these are "unrestricted by evidence." However, the texts of the Sharia indicate their validity in a general sense, meaning that the benefits of all three types in the Sharia are those that are based on specific texts (i.e., those that have detailed evidence related to them) and are known by the text, that is, by the detailed evidence (referring here to: the recognized benefits and those that the Sharia has invalidated). What is not known by the text (i.e., what is unrestricted by the detailed evidence related to it) has been recognized as being sought by the general texts in the Sharia, and upon this basis, the legal rulings are built when the specific legal text (i.e., the detailed evidence related) is absent in the incident or in something similar to it. Thus, the benefit becomes the evidence, and they differentiate between legal and non-legal benefits, stating that the benefits that are valid as evidence are those that align with the objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia)(4). This third category was established as a general evidence used by those schools of thought, which is "unrestricted benefits."
With this methodological framework in thinking, and with these controls, they attempted to regulate the process of using unrestricted benefits as evidence that is not based on whim or pure rational opinion in establishing legal rulings. Rather, they relied on those benefits that they derived from the general legal evidence, from universal principles, and from the objectives of the Sharia. Thus, reason was not presented as a ruling legislator. This is a way of understanding and a methodology for dealing with legal evidence. However, in reality, we find that this methodology often fails to prevent the derived ruling from relying on rational opinion regarding what reason determines as beneficial, because their process of induction is unregulated, disconnected from textual causes, and subject to differing rational assessments of benefit due to the absence of objective criteria that regulate the determination of the legality of this benefit or that one. This also prevents the construction of a benefit or misleads it based merely on a superficial reading or an unverified outcome of the texts, despite the fact that al-Shatibi, for example, conditioned comprehensive induction of the evidence of the Sharia to extract the benefit, and imposed strict conditions on it that we will mention later, God willing. However, some of its applications in the jurisprudence it is based on resulted in a weak link between the derived benefit and revelation, even if the jurist mistakenly perceives its strength. In all cases, we find regarding the issue a set of detailed evidences or universal principles related to it, which the jurist overlooked, thinking the ruling was unrestricted by them. However, this method of thinking completely rejected the presentation of reason as a ruling legislator. The crux of the matter is that the problem is methodological, as sufficient rules have not been established to make it adequately regulated, leading to the entry of rulings that have a weak connection to revelation, even if the jurist intended to completely separate them from reason and whim.
