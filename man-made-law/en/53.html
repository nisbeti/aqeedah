Section: The Sharīʿah Standard for Recognizing Maṣlaḥah and the Limits of Reason in Ijtihād

It is among God’s wise arrangements that He embedded within the human being – as a social creature – energetic faculties represented in needs and instincts that demand fulfillment and regulation. He honored him with reason as a tool for understanding and reflection, not as an independent source of legislation. The need for food, for example, drives a person to seek work, ownership, or exchange – all of which require regulation. Similarly, the reproductive instinct prompts marriage, which calls for rulings governing the male-female relationship, lineage, dowry, guardianship, etc. Though the intellect facilitates comprehension of these relations, it does not independently legislate them, but rather receives rulings from revelation and interprets them.

Thus, human relations must be governed by legislation that ensures the attainment of benefits and the prevention of harms – not left to chaos in which each individual seeks their own interests by personal whims, leading to corruption. If, for instance, people were left to set the standards of trade and profit, some might believe commercial fraud or usury ensures short-term gains. Yet such practices breed systemic economic corruption and oppression, as is evident in capitalist economies.

While some jurists categorize rulings related to social and individual conduct under three types – ḍarūriyyāt (necessities, e.g., preserving life by prohibiting murder), ḥājiyyāt (needs, e.g., permitting travel for trade), and taḥsīniyyāt (embellishments, e.g., using polite language and avoiding impurities) – this classification does not encompass all of Sharīʿah.

There exist rulings related to pure devotion, trials and tests, moral training, and submission to divine authority that are not built upon benefits or harms at all. Acts such as the number and timing of prayers, the fast from dawn to sunset, the rituals of pilgrimage, and the mandated waiting period for widows all require pure compliance, even if their wisdom is not apparent or mentally fathomable.

Also, public interests may conflict with individual benefits. For example, the prohibition of hoarding serves societal welfare, even if the merchant sees personal gain in it. Likewise, bribery may offer advantages to specific parties but is outlawed for its societal harm.
