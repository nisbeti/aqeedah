The legislations produced by human minds will always remain subject to change and fluctuation according to these differing perspectives, which prevents the realization of the condition of stability and permanence necessary in legislation, and hinders the production of a legal system that possesses the quality of obligation or gains general acceptance.

For example, justice is a value that is universally agreed upon as good and correct, yet the dilemma lies in how to achieve justice. This can only be accomplished by establishing a sound system that realizes justice; thus, the value of justice is achieved through external matters, not from itself. These external matters are the controls of actions, namely legislations and systems. Given human ignorance of consequences and results, one may perceive the achievement of justice in matters that turn out to yield results that involve injustice. Similarly, this applies to the pursuit of benefits and the prevention of harms.

Moreover, the benefit may pertain to the individual alone, to bring about his own interests, or it may be a public interest. Here, people's views on what achieves these benefits and what prevents harms conflict, influenced by whims, immediate perspectives, and trial and error, resulting in a devastating outcome for society. "What is the benefit that is brought about, and what is the harm that is averted? Is it what a person loves and hates? Or is it what benefits him and harms him? Or is it good and evil? Or beauty and ugliness? Or is it something else? There is no doubt that we need criteria with which we can judge that the system brings about benefit and averts harm!"

We have demonstrated in our book The Black Box of Western Thought that society cannot rationally agree on unified legislations. In fact, the democratic systems that are supposed to represent the collective will have systematically failed to produce legislations that truly reflect the interests of the people or represent their consensus, whether through electing a ruler, forming parliaments, or enacting laws. The electoral process itself is tainted by biases, exclusion, and manipulation, and does not produce a representative who embodies the consensus of the nation or even represents its majority, but rather represents the relative majority that enabled him to obtain the most votes from those who participated in the electoral process, which strips him of the legitimacy of representing the people's opinion. Furthermore, the elected representatives do not possess an objective mechanism to achieve balance between conflicting interests of the people or satisfy the collective mind, or to refer back to them for their opinions regarding the laws they legislate. Instead, they often produce legislations that are imposed in the name of democracy, without stemming from genuine consensus, comprehensive understanding, or representation of what they call the general will.

Thus, the human mind is inherently incapable, in light of this relativity and subjectivity, of producing stable, just, acceptable, and binding legislations for all members of society, let alone for different societies. This necessarily requires the existence of a legislative reference that transcends this relativity, possessing an absolute standard, and a source encompassing wisdom and justice, which is the reference of divine revelation.
