The Critique of Minority Jurisprudence and Its Danger to Minorities!
Executive Summary:
This section critiques and analyzes contemporary minority jurisprudence, clarifying that it does not represent a genuine extension of Islamic jurisprudence, but rather an extension of a modern rationalist school influenced by the West, which seeks to align Sharia with Western reality, not the other way around. The book demonstrates how this jurisprudence is based on severing the connection between legal rulings and the Islamic doctrinal context, through justifying adherence to secular laws, political participation in systems that do not adhere to Sharia, issuing fatwas permitting usury, and concessions related to Islamic identity, under the pretext of achieving public interest (maslaha) or coexistence.
The chapter shows that this jurisprudence does not stem from a solid usuli (foundational) methodology, but from a pragmatic, utilitarian approach that prioritizes rational interests over textual evidence, and empties texts of their definitive meanings under the claim of "interpreting reality," which has led to a gradual disconnection between the Muslim and his doctrinal identity. The book links this trend to the thoughts of Abduh and Afghani, presenting a collection of contemporary examples of fatwas that contradict definitive texts under the guise of "public interest," and concludes with a warning about the effects of this jurisprudence on the Islamic identity of Muslims in the West and on the future of Islamic existence itself, considering that minority jurisprudence is, in reality, a dissolution of doctrinal specificity in the crucible of liberal integration.
The book shows that this "jurisprudence" does not rely on established usuli principles, but rather emerged within an environment saturated with liberal influences and governed by the pressures of the political and social realities in Western societies, where it is intended for Sharia to adapt to the Western model, not the other way around.
Key Themes:
The Methodological Root of Minority Thought: The book connects minority jurisprudence with the propositions of the modern rationalist school, especially the current that began with Abduh and Rida, where public interest was prioritized over text, and the review of definitive rulings was permitted under the pretext of facilitation, leading to the emergence of a new jurisprudential school detached from the classical usuli methodology.
Circumventing Texts Under Claims of Public Interest: This section highlights examples of fatwas from contemporary scholars that permit actions explicitly prohibited, such as:
Working in usurious banks,
Taking usurious loans to buy homes,
Being lenient regarding hijab,
Legal justification for equality between males and females in inheritance.
