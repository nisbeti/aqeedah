Say, 'Has Allah permitted you, or do you invent (lies) against Allah?'﴿ [Yūnus: 59]. And how can we believe that the Sharia is complete, then claim that rational interest (maslaha) has come to complete what is lacking in it?!
Islamic law (sharia) has come as the final revelation, carrying a complete and absolute reference to divine revelation, and it has liberated humanity from the futility of rational interpretations. Such opinions that arise from whims are always unregulated. The noble sharia has established fixed principles of justice, so the interest lies in following the revelation, not in opposing it. It has confined warnings to revelation and obligated individuals to conduct their behavior and manage their interests according to the commands and prohibitions of the sharia. Here, the fundamental question arises: who has the right to legislate? Is it Allah, the Creator of humanity and the Knower of their interests, or is it the imperfect human, veiled from the unseen? This question is the crux of the disagreement among various intellectual schools, both ancient and modern. With Allah's help, we will dissect it, analyze its dimensions, and clarify the intellectual and practical deviations it has led to, while establishing a regulated legal alternative.
Research Methodology and Its Sections
The research is divided into five main chapters preceded by this introduction and followed by a conclusion that includes the most important findings and recommendations, along with scientific documentation of the verses, hadiths, scholars' sayings, and research references. The research progresses logically according to the following plan:
Chapter One: The Limitations of Reason and the Impossibility of It Being a Reference for Legislation; we discuss the limitations of human reason and its inherent deficiencies that prevent it from establishing a comprehensive and just legislation. We benefit from the book "The Miracle of Islamic Legislation" by Thaer Salama and some of what is mentioned in the study "The Black Box of Western Thought" by Thaer Salama to highlight the inability of human systems to achieve societal objectives and values in isolation from revelation. We also demonstrate the connection of rational legislations to personal desires and specific cultures, which makes them relative and unsuitable to serve as a general reference.
Chapter Two: Critique of Public Interest (Maslaha Mursala) and the Distinction Between "Appropriateness" and Modern Rational Interest. We will address the concept of "public interest (maslaha mursala)" as discussed by the usuliyyin (scholars of Islamic jurisprudence), relying on the book "Public Interests (Masalih Mursala)" by Dr. Mahmoud Abdul Hadi Faour, and the book "Islamic Personality (Part Three) - Usul al-Fiqh" by the scholar Taqi al-Din al-Nabhani, as well as the book "Maqasid According to Imam al-Shatibi - A Jurisprudential and Usuli Study" by Dr. Mahmoud Abdul Hadi Faour, among many other references. We will clarify that what has been considered public interests by some jurists was not a separate rational legislation, but rather based on the general objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia) and its comprehensive principles.
