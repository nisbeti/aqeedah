"Real interests that produce what the legislator intends by establishing religion, preserving life, intellect, lineage, and wealth." [al-Muwafaqat, vol. 1, p. 36, edited by Mashhur Al-Salman], so partial or momentary benefit is not sufficient to consider it a legal interest (maslaha), rather it must fall under a system of objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia) that achieves public interests and ensures balance and justice.
Sheikh Mustafa al-Zarqa says in his definition of recognized public interest (maslaha): "The recognized public interest in Sharia is the preservation of the objectives of the Legislator, and it is not merely the achievement of some benefit, but rather the achievement of a benefit that is regulated by the values and standards established by revelation." [Al-Madkhal al-Fiqhi al-Aam, vol. 2, p. 891]. Commentary: Al-Zarqa indicates that public interest in Sharia is not merely a benefit, but is linked to legal objectives and regulatory frameworks, and it cannot be so unless it is presented against these standards.
Taqi al-Din al-Nabhani – in Islamic Personality – establishes the distinction between public interest (maslaha) and benefit from another perspective: "Benefit may be illusory or temporary, while the interest considered in the Sharia is what achieves the intent of the Legislator, not what a person merely sees as good through reason or desire," [Islamic Personality, vol. 3, pp. 320–322]. Commentary: The interest is what is "good in its essence according to the measure of the Legislator," not what is good according to the measure of taste or desire. It is assessed within the framework of legislation, not in isolation from it.
What did Ibn al-Qayyim say in I'lam al-Muwaqqi'in:
We often hear those who cite Ibn al-Qayyim in I'lam al-Muwaqqi'in:
"The Sharia is based and founded on justice and the interests of the servants in this life and the Hereafter. It is entirely justice, entirely mercy, and entirely interests. So every matter that moves from justice to oppression, from mercy to its opposite, and from benefit to harm, is not part of the Sharia." [I'lam al-Muwaqqi'in, vol. 3, p. 11]. Thus, Ibn al-Qayyim sees that the interest (maslaha) in the Sharia is a manifestation of organized justice and mercy, and not merely a benefit that is observable by the eye, but rather what the system of the Sharia achieves in terms of goodness for creation.
This statement has been criticized for potentially making public interest (maslaha) the criterion, rather than the text, and that reason does not encompass the true public interest, nor does it always grasp its wisdom and outcomes. Thus, giving the male a share equal to that of two females may seem unjust to some minds, while it is, in the balance of Allah, pure justice, as established by a definitive text.
The truth is that making public interest (maslaha) the criterion for whether a ruling is from the Sharia or not is an entry point for reason into legislation, which is what Allah has forbidden, because public interest can only be known through the Sharia. If the Sharia is rejected in favor of public interest, then truth is rejected in favor of desire.
In truth, extracting this statement from the intellectual context of Ibn al-Qayyim leads to intellectual calamities, as Ibn al-Qayyim's saying: "Every issue that moves from justice to oppression, and from benefit to harm, is not part of the Sharia" - in its absolute form - is a misleading statement, which opens the door to rational interpretation of texts, and suggests that the criterion for benefit and justice is independent.
