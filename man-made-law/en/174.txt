Introduction: The Roots of the Reformist Rationalist School
Since the late 19th century, the Islamic world witnessed the emergence of a new intellectual trend known as the “Modern Rationalist School” or “Reformist Movement.” This trend is tied to prominent figures like Jamal al-Din al-Afghani (1838–1897), Muhammad Abduh (1849–1905), and Muhammad Rashid Rida (1865–1935), among others. These reformers championed slogans of revival and renewal, advocating for the elevation of reason and the liberation of thought from rigid imitation (taqlīd), to the extent that reason was made supreme, even above revelation. Their movement represented an attempt to reconcile Islam with both its inherited legal tradition and the spirit of the modern West, seeking a hybridized framework of religious commitment and civilizational progress. This chapter critically examines the role of this school along seven key axes:
Elevation of reason and utility over revelation in legislation.
Reinterpretation of religious texts through utilitarian or symbolic readings under modern influences.
Laying the groundwork for dismantling traditional legal structures and unleashing unbounded “renewal.”
Adapting Islamic thought to nationalist, liberal, and socialist currents and coloring Islam with these ideologies.
The influence of Freemasonry and the pan-Islamic project in shifting the ummah’s unity from a religious to a political-nationalist identity.
The school’s direct links with Egypt’s modern intellectual elite, and its influence on contemporary ideologies like liberal Islam, minority fiqh, fiqh of balancing interests, and unregulated maqāṣid fiqh.
A critical review of their methodology and assumptions, as critiqued by scholars like Dr. Muhammad Muhammad Hussein (especially in Our Fortresses Are Being Undermined From Within), Anwar al-Jundi, Abu al-Hasan al-Nadwi, Ziyad Salama (in his study on Hassan al-Banna), and others.
Each of these axes will be explored in detail, analyzing the school's effect on how Muslims think about revelation, scripture, and religious authority. Comparisons will be drawn between traditional usūlī understandings and the reinterpretive frameworks of the modern rationalist school, supported with documented sources and structured tables.
