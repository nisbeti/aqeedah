Induction is the observation of partial rulings to derive a general rule, and it is used in deducing the objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia).
A. Conditions of induction:
It must be complete or predominant (not deficient), otherwise it leads to misguidance in inference.
B. The danger of expansion:
The "deficient induction" is rejected in considering interests as an independent proof, just as al-Nabhani criticizes the saying that the preservation of reason and interest is the cause of the ruling, not a ruling resulting from it.
The legal objectives (maqasid al-sharia) and their restrictive role, not foundational
A. The difference between the cause (illah) and the objective (maqsad):
The cause: a specific description that can be measured.
The objective: a general wisdom, from which rulings are not derived, but by which the consistency of the ruling with the generality is reviewed.
B. Example: A new rule is not legislated merely because it achieves "interest," but there must be a valid legal proof that establishes it.
Ranks of proofs and their sequence
A. The order:
The Qur'an > the Sunnah > consensus > correct analogy.
Rules such as abrogation, specification, and unrestricted and restricted are referred to in cases of apparent conflict.
B. Dealing with the probable and the definitive:
The considered probability (rajih) is a proof in practical rulings, unlike in belief.
Methodological controls for ijtihad
A. There is no ijtihad in definitive matters.
B. The use of rules such as:
Presumption (istishab): the ruling remains unless a negation arises.
Blocking the means (sadd al-dhara'i): preventing the means to the prohibited.
Preference (tarjih): when proofs conflict, the stronger in chain or indication is preferred.
Conclusion: The unity of the methodology and the integration of tools
The methodology of legal ijtihad is not a selective process, but an interconnected system:
Language and indication ← generating meaning
General rules ← organizing branches
Analogy ← controlled expansion
