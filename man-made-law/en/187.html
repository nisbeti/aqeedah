The concepts of "balances" and "the jurisprudence of minorities" in the Western arena are not a free scientific endeavor as marketed, but rather a Western and Arab-funded industry aimed at shaping a new Islam that aligns with non-Islamic priorities... We are faced with the phenomenon of "buying fatwas," where money has been mobilized to re-engineer legal rulings. This is undoubtedly one of the most dangerous challenges facing the heritage of Islamic jurisprudence in the present age." This alarming statement aligns with the diagnosis of Dr. Muhammad Muhammad Hussein half a century ago when he said, "The West has devised traps for such reformers to distort the religion from within their environment."

Modernist Islamists (new liberals): At the beginning of the twenty-first century, a group of writers and intellectuals emerged, crafted by American institutions seeking to fund moderate Islamic networks, who fully adopt Western modernity values and reinterpret Islam in light of them. Among them are Muhammad Shahrour in the Levant, Hassan Hanafi in Egypt, and Abdelmajid al-Sharafi in Tunisia, among others. They do not directly rely on Abduh and Rashid Rida, but have been influenced by Western philosophical thought. However, upon analyzing their ideas, we find them to be an extension of the logic of reinterpreting texts to align with contemporary values (democracy, secularism, human rights in their Western sense). For example: they deny the division of the world into Dar al-Islam and Dar al-Kufr, advocate for a defensive interpretation of jihad only, and implicitly accept the separation of religion from state, claiming that "Islam is a religion, not a state," while some of them dismiss definitive rulings such as the punishments (hudud) on the grounds that they are unsuitable for our time... All of this echoes the propositions of Ali Abd al-Raziq and Taha Hussein but in a modern academic language; they are the descendants of the rationalist school, even if they do not declare it, as the essence is the same: making modernity the governing reference for understanding the text.

It is clear from the above that the intellectual impact of the rational reformist school has continued in various forms to this day, and despite periods of ebb and flow—where this current waned in the mid-twentieth century and then returned strongly after the eighties—it remains a deeply rooted current in the thought that the West has prepared, funded, and opened media platforms for to strike at Islam "from within"(51). This has brought with it serious negative consequences, as we have seen, the most prominent of which is the destabilization of certainty in the reference of revelation and legal standards in contrast to the glorification of material reason and Western systems. This point, in particular, has been the focus of severe criticism from prominent figures in Islamic thought opposing this school.
