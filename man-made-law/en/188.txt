The modern rationalist school has faced criticism since its inception from Islamic scholars and writers who felt its danger. In the writings of Dr. Muhammad Muhammad Hussein (1911–1977), who is one of the prominent researchers in modern Islamic thought, there is a deep analysis of what he termed the "Western intellectual invasion" of the Islamic world from within. He dedicated his books "National Trends in Modern Arabic Literature" and "Our Fortresses Are Threatened from Within" to uncover the roots of Westernization and intellectual change, discussing in detail the role of Jamal al-Din al-Afghani and Muhammad Abduh and their school in this regard.
Dr. Muhammad Muhammad Hussein believes that these reformers—despite the sincerity of some of their intentions—fell into the intellectual traps set by the West. They attempted to refute the accusation of stagnation against Islam in the face of Orientalist critiques, but they deviated to the opposite extreme, as he stated. He says, may Allah have mercy on him, in describing their methodology: "The sincere among them lean the Islamic values to the utmost that the texts can bear towards Western values, and thus they fall into the snares devised by the West for such as them. In their effort to dispel the accusation of stagnation (which the Westerners attach to the Sharia), they deviate to the utmost opposite side in demonstrating its flexibility, until this flexibility reaches the point of laxity and the absence of self and foundations, making it suitable to be a tail to any system and subject to whims. Thus, they end up nullifying the function of religion, because instead of rectifying the deviations of life with the texts of the Sharia, they manipulate the texts of the Sharia to justify the deviations of contemporary life." This scathing critique summarizes the main objection to the methodology of the rationalist school: that in its pursuit of Western modernity, it has stripped the Sharia of its identity and dignity, transforming it—among those who followed it excessively—into merely a repository of texts that can be reshaped according to the demands of the current reality. Revelation is no longer "ruling" for these later followers, but rather "ruled" and "subordinate" to reality.
Dr. Hussein also criticized the association of the symbols of this school with colonialism and its tools: in "National Trends," he pointed to the role of the British in encouraging Abduh's school to change the traditional Islamic mentality. He cites, for example, Lord Cromer, the ruler of Egypt, stating (in his reports) that he supports the program of "Sheikh Muhammad Abduh's School," which is based on cooperation with Europeans and introducing their civilization to the country, and that the appointment of Saad Zaghloul as Minister of Education (1906) was an implementation of this policy. Indeed, Saad Zaghloul was one of Abduh's close students, and his assumption of the educational role helped secularize the educational system and lay the foundations for the Egyptian university away from al-Azhar. Dr. Hussein comments on this, denouncing the cultural subservience of some of the elite to the British, while also criticizing their admiration for the model of Western civilization despite its hostility to the creed. He links the ideas of Abduh to those who came after him, including writers who took an antagonistic stance towards the Arab Islamic heritage under the banner of development. For instance, he believes that the call for "popular literature" in colloquial dialects and the separation of language from classical Arabic actually originated in the embrace of this reformist school under the influence of Orientalists. Likewise, the tendency towards Westernization in poetry and prose among the Apollo group and the Diwan school—all of which are Egyptian literary movements influenced by the liberal thought established by Abduh and his school in the first half of the twentieth century.
