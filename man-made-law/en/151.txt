The justification mentioned: "A balance between the harm of abolishing the prescribed punishments (hudud) and the harm of distorting the image of Islam in the West by applying a harsh punishment." Some advocates of the idea of postponing the discussion of the prescribed punishments in Islam, which may be seen by the West as contrary to its principles, claim that implementing these punishments now could alienate people from Islam or be used as a pretext by enemies to tarnish its reputation, so it is in the public interest (maslaha) to leave it temporarily! Then the matter developed among others who openly stated: "The objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia) in establishing justice and mercy are achievable without physical punishments, so we can replace them with lighter discretionary punishments (ta'zir) that are in line with the development of the times!" This is, in fact, a cancellation of the prescribed punishments and heralds great evil.
The historical background of the fatwa: Why do they issue a fatwa to abolish the punishment of stoning? As for the immediate political and social context, it is the pressure of the "War on Terror" after September 11, and the intensification of Western media attacks on Sharia, labeling it as "barbaric," especially after the implementation of certain punishments in countries like Nigeria (2002). Secondly, the influence of "Islamic elites" with the discourse of "human rights," and the transformation of concepts such as "human dignity" and "cruelty" into a reference that supersedes the textual evidence of Sharia (the "Islam and Human Rights" conference in Geneva 2004). Thirdly, the rise of a trend to reinterpret punishments, such as the writings of Shahrour, which claim that punishments are historical and not religious. In all these approaches, there is an intellectual capitulation to Western liberalism, in their attempt to prove "Islamic liberalism" by conceding to constants that Western civilization sees as "violating human rights." Thus, the fatwa was not for practical reality (as no one applies punishments in the West), but rather to: strip the punishments of their sanctity and pave the way for discussions on their abolition in the Islamic world (as later occurred at the "Reform of Islamic Jurisprudence" conference in Qatar 2010).
Moreover, the funding of some of these councils by Western governments (such as the Dutch Ministry of Foreign Affairs in 2004) conditioned the "modernization of religious discourse" in accordance with liberal values.
Please pay close attention to this point, as many of these councils are funded by Western governmental entities.
The historical witness: How they transformed "practical impossibility" into "religious legitimacy"!
In the commentary of "Sheikh" Abdullah bin Bayyah (a member of the European Council) on the decision (2007), he said: "We do not abolish the punishment, but we suspend its application in the West because the punishment is not applied where there is fitna!"
The problem: transforming "realistic impossibility" (the absence of an Islamic authority) into a "legal justification" (fitna), while the legal excuse is merely the inability to implement, for the Sharia only excuses the inability to implement, not the absence of an Islamic authority. It did not make the absence of the state a justification for abolishing or suspending rulings; rather, it regarded it as a circumstance that prevents implementation without affecting the ruling itself. Therefore, it was possible for the muftis to simply state: the punishments are the exclusive responsibility of the Islamic state alone, as it is the one that undertakes their implementation within the framework of the comprehensive and integrated system of Sharia.
