The Islamic way of thinking has changed due to the rationalist school

There is no doubt that the spread of the ideas of rationalist reformers has led to a profound transformation in the way some Muslims think about texts and revelation. Instead of the traditional view that considers the legal text (whether the Qur'an or authentic Sunnah) as the final word and the arbiter over human opinion, a generation has emerged that subjects the text to the courts of reason and the scales of "progress." Many intellectuals— even those who appear religious— now hesitate to accept certain definitive legal rulings if they see that Western thought criticizes them. Instead of Muslims being proud of their religion and ready to present it to the world as an alternative, some feel that they must justify their religion before Western civilization and "refine" it to align with its values. This defeatist mentality is precisely what Dr. Muhammad Hussein warned against when he said that they "have reached a point of laxity in the flexibility of Sharia… and made it a tail to any system." Yes, the concept of the reference for legislation has changed among wide segments: after divine revelation was the highest reference, for some, the reference has become a mixture of the data of "reality," "pragmatic reason," "modern universal values," and Western liberalism, taking from religion only as much as it aligns with that. In this sense, the way of thinking has largely become "Western pragmatic reasoning," as stated in the research question.

The pragmatic utilitarian thinking that has infiltrated contemporary Muslim thought has led many to deal with legal rulings based on the logic of direct material benefits. If a ruling does not show immediate benefit or align with societal whims, it is rejected, and the search for ways to mitigate or suspend it begins. This is exactly what the rationalist school has pursued from the beginning: evaluating rulings using a limited human rational scale, instead of submitting to the established wisdom of God and seeking the legal wisdom within the framework of faith in the unseen. Phrases like "rulings change with the times," "Islam is a religion that keeps pace with development," and "wherever there is benefit, there is Sharia" have become a basis used to reinterpret and practically abrogate Sharia, and to permit prohibitions. The new jurisprudence— among its extremists— has become about finding interpretations for every text that contradicts Western values, or freezing it under the banner of objectives (maqasid), or claiming that it was only suitable for its time. Unfortunately, this mentality has transferred from the elite intellectuals to the general educated populace through media and education. It is no longer surprising to hear an ordinary Muslim arguing against the prohibition of usury because it has "become an economic necessity today," for example, relying— without realizing it— on an old fatwa by Abduh that justified "bank interest." Or another might argue that legal punishments like the amputation of a thief's hand were for a bygone era and do not fit "modern human rights." These ideas did not arise from a vacuum; rather, they are the result of decades of the spread of writings and orientations of the rationalist school in its various forms.

In this regard, Abu al-Hasan al-Nadwi says: "Religion does not keep pace with every development; rather, the truth is that religion distinguishes between sound change and unsound change." This is what has escaped the minds of those people.
