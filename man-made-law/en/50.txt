As for the relationship between maṣlaḥah and manfaʿah (utility), the legal theorists expressed it thus: “A maṣlaḥah is something accompanied by goodness in both its ends and means, while manfaʿah may merely be a desire, whim, or exploitation.” For instance, ribā (usury) provides an immediate financial benefit, but it is not a maṣlaḥah because it leads to corruption. Hence, Islam harshly prohibited it, stating explicitly: “Allah has permitted trade and forbidden usury.” (2:275) It is even described by Allah as a deed of Satan.
In fact, many modern economic systems have institutionalized ribā under the guise of rational interest and maṣlaḥah, framing it as the foundation of development and finance. Banks and global institutions were built upon it, laws were codified to protect it, and people were encouraged to borrow based on it—while many found no problem with it intellectually. Yet the Sharīʿah judges it as profound corruption, proving that recognizing something as a maṣlaḥah cannot rely on unaided reason but must be weighed on the scale of revelation.
Another example is gambling and sports betting. Many justify these as forms of entertainment or economic stimulation—particularly in gaming and sports sectors. Yet the Sharīʿah considers them grave corruption and labels them filth: “Intoxicants and gambling… are abominations of Satan’s work.” (5:90).
Pornography distribution is defended by some as a form of “personal freedom” or “economic benefit” based on supply and demand. Some countries even legislate protections for it. Yet in Sharīʿah, it is a severe crime: “Indeed, those who love that immorality be spread among the believers—for them is a painful punishment.” (24:19)
Profiting from monopolies may appear as an economic advantage to traders, but in Sharīʿah it is a sin that causes widespread harm to the poor and disrupts market balance. The Prophet ﷺ said: “Whoever hoards is sinful.” Surrogacy and tampering with lineage may be viewed as compassionate medical solutions for infertility or income sources for poor women. But in Islamic law, they are severe corruptions: “Call them by [the names of] their fathers; that is more just in the sight of Allah.” (33:5).
Extramarital relationships are deemed psychological necessities or personal rights in Western systems. But Islam sees them as moral corruption, undermining the family structure, promoting fornication, and destabilizing lineage. Hence, the Sharīʿah forbids them and classifies zinā as a major sin: “Do not approach unlawful sexual intercourse. Indeed, it is ever an immorality and is evil as a way.” (17:32).
Euthanasia is sometimes proposed as a compassionate interest to ease suffering or as an individual’s right to end their life. But Islamic law regards it as aggression against the soul, a breach of servitude, and a transgression against God’s exclusive right over life and death: “Do not kill the soul which Allah has forbidden—except by right.” (6:151)
Gender reassignment and manipulation of sexual identity are sometimes labeled psychological interests. Yet in Sharīʿah, they are violations of the innate nature (fiṭrah): “The natural disposition Allah instilled in mankind.” (30:30) The Prophet ﷺ warned: “Cursed are the men who imitate women and the women who imitate men.”
Dear reader, when you encounter these examples, you might naturally recoil, since your mind operates through Islamic values that frame these actions as devoid of maṣlaḥah.
