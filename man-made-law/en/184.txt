Also, as previously mentioned, Ṭāhā Ḥusayn, in his 1926 book On Pre-Islamic Poetry, cast doubt on certain historical Qur’anic accounts under the guise of scientific methodology, triggering outrage within al-Azhar at the time. These individuals represented a more secular-modernist orientation than a purely Islamic rationalism, though they clearly emerged from the intellectual framework established by ʿAbduh—namely, the call to free rational interpretation of the texts.
Outside of Egypt, reformist movements across the Muslim world were likewise influenced by this thought. In India, for instance, Sir Sayyid Ahmad Khan (1817–1898) was influenced by Afghani and ʿAbduh. He called for a similar re-interpretation of Islamic law to align with British civilization. He went so far as to deny miracles and supernatural interpretations—paralleling ʿAbduh’s own approaches in many respects. His ideas spread through Aligarh University. In Turkey, some early pioneers of the Tanzimat reform movement and later the Young Turks adopted the same rationalist inclination, which eventually culminated in the establishment of Atatürk’s secular state—a radical embodiment of this thought.
Thus, Egypt was not the only stage on which this intellectual trend played out; rather, the contagion—if the term may be used—spread across the Islamic world. Contemporary Currents Emanating from the Rationalist Reformist Tradition
In recent decades, new currents have emerged that, while appearing novel on the surface, are in essence an extension of the rationalist reformist methodology. Among the most prominent of these currents are: the Modern Maqāṣid Movement, Facilitatory Jurisprudence (including minority fiqh and fiqh of balancing interests), and what has come to be known as Liberal Islam in academic circles. • The Modern Maqāṣid Movement This trend is led by scholars and thinkers such as Dr. Yūsuf al-Qaraḍāwī, Dr. Aḥmad al-Raysūnī, and others. They place heavy emphasis on applying the higher objectives (maqāṣid) of the Sharīʿah in all forms of legal reasoning, under the banner of showcasing Islam’s mercy and flexibility. However, some of their fatwas stirred considerable controversy for overstepping what conservative scholars regard as consensus (ijmāʿ).
For instance, al-Qaraḍāwī issued fatwas permitting bank interest in cases of necessity in Western countries, and even allowing work in interest-based banks, stating: “This has become a widespread affliction. If one is unable to avoid it, then there is no harm in accepting such a job…” He expanded the principle of necessity on the pretext of the obligation to work, stating: “Shaykh Yūsuf al-Qaraḍāwī said that one may work in such institutions to gain skills and experience not available elsewhere, with the goal of eventually transitioning to Islamic institutions or entrepreneurship where such expertise may be applied.” (IslamOnline: Fatwa on Working in Interest-Based Banks). He also argued: “If we were to prohibit every Muslim from working in banks, the result would be that non-Muslims—especially Jews—would dominate the banking sector. This would pose great harm to Islam and Muslims. Furthermore, not all banking work is interest-based. Much of it is permissible, such as brokerage and deposits. The impermissible parts are fewer, so it is acceptable to tolerate them while striving to change the financial system to one more pleasing to one's religion and conscience. The Prophet ﷺ said, ‘Actions are by intentions.’” “Islam does not reject gradualism in addressing such issues… proceeding with conviction and will.” All of these positions are justified by appealing to maṣlaḥah (interest) and rafʿ al-ḥaraj (removal of hardship).
